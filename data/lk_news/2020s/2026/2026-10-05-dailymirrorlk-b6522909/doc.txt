New buses, old habits

For years, Sri Lankans complained about the state of public transport. We complained about buses that were old, uncomfortable and unsafe. We complained about the high steps that made getting into a bus difficult for elderly passengers. We complained about overcrowding, poor maintenance and the general condition of public transport.

A country cannot call itself modern while its people are forced to travel in buses that look as though they have survived several decades without anyone seriously caring about their condition. That is why the introduction of modern low-floor Metro buses in Colombo is a welcome change. The Government has finally begun replacing some of the outdated public transport infrastructure with a more modern fleet. The introduction of these buses is not merely about having newer vehicles on Colombo’s roads. It is about making public transport safer, more accessible and more comfortable.

The low-floor design is particularly important. For an elderly person, a passenger with a disability or someone struggling with mobility, the difference between a high step and an accessible entrance is not cosmetic. It can determine whether getting onto a bus is easy or frightening.

Why should railway passengers still have to accept ageing coaches, neglected stations and inadequate facilities as though these are unavoidable features of life in Sri Lanka? The Government needs to look beyond buses and begin modernising the entire public transport system. The railway network must also be brought into the 21st Century.

But there is an uncomfortable question that needs to be asked. While getting into one of these modern buses, I noticed something deeply disappointing. Some of the seats were already dirty. There were scratch marks on the railings.

These may seem like small things. They are not. They tell us something about the bigger problem facing this country. We want a modern Sri Lanka.

We demand new buses from the Government and then behave as though the buses belong to nobody. We demand clean public places and then throw rubbish. We demand better infrastructure and then damage it.

We complain that governments do not maintain public assets while some members of the public seem determined to destroy them before they even have a chance to be properly maintained. This has to stop.

A bus does not belong to the President. It does not belong to the Transport Minister. It does not belong to the bus driver. It belongs to the people. The money spent on public transport comes from the public purse. And even when buses are donated by friendly countries, they are still public assets once they are placed into service.

The fact that a bus was donated makes its protection even more important, not less. Perhaps we have become so accustomed to poor public services that when something new finally arrives, we assume it will eventually become just as neglected as everything else.

That attitude is precisely what must change. The Government has a responsibility to modernise. But citizens have a responsibility to protect that modernisation. We cannot keep blaming politicians for everything that goes wrong in this country.

A country is not modern simply because it has modern buses. A country becomes modern when its people understand that public property must be respected. If you see someone damaging a bus, say something. If you see someone throwing rubbish inside it, say something. If your child scratches a seat or damages a public facility, do not laugh it off as harmless behaviour.

Teach children that public property is their property too. The new buses should be the beginning of something bigger. They should be the beginning of a serious effort to modernise public transport, improve public services and provide Sri Lankans with the standard of infrastructure they deserve.

But they should also become a test of us. For decades, Sri Lankans have been asking the Government to give us better buses. Now that we are finally getting them, we have no excuse.
