When the United National Party (UNP) celebrated its 80th anniversary last Sunday, it did not appear that anyone considered it a major political event in the country.

Had they been in power, the event would have been the lead story in many national newspapers the next day, but this time media attention on it seemed to be insignificant.

The UNP is a party that deeply impacted  Sri Lankan history. When Sri Lanka gained independence from the British in 1948, power was transferred from the British to the UNP. It was such a powerful force in the past that it secured a five-sixths majority in Parliament in 1977, making it  the only party which set that record. The UNP is the mother of Sri Lankan capitalist parties.

However, in the last two general elections, the party suffered a crushing defeat. In 2020, the party failed to win even a single seat at the district level, securing only one seat through the National List. In the 2024 general election, the party won only one seat in the Nuwara Eliya district, which was undoubtedly due to the votes of Ceylon Workers’ Congress (CWC) supporters. CWC leader Jeevan Thondaman was elected to that seat, as the UNP and CWC contested jointly in that district.

In the Kalutara and Kandy districts, the UNP contested under the name of the New Democratic Front (NDF), winning one seat in each of those two districts. The UNP did not secure a single seat in any other district. This raises the question of whether this is all the UNP managed to earn politically over the past 80 years.

Out of the 78 years since Sri Lanka gained independence, the UNP has ruled the country — either alone or in coalition with other parties — nearly 37 years, spending 30 of those years ruling alone. But now, doubts remain as to whether the party will ever come to power again.

The UNP was founded in 1946. Since then, the party founder and Sri Lanka’s first Prime Minister D.S. Senanayake led the party for 6 years, his son Dudley Senanayake for 14 years (1952–53 and 1960–73), John Kotelawala for 3 years, J.R. Jayewardene for 19 years (1956–60 and 1973–88), and Ranasinghe Premadasa for 5 years. Over the past 80 years,

Ranil Wickremesinghe has been the party leader for the longest period—serving as leader for 32 years from 1994 to the present day. It was during his tenure that the party hit rock bottom, mainly due to power struggles.

Power struggles for leadership have been the main cause of the UNP’s decline. Six major leadership tussles have taken place within the party, three of which occurred during Wickremesinghe’s era. Because of these six struggles, the party saw three major splits.

The first power struggle occurred between D.S. Senanayake and S.W.R.D. Bandaranaike. Bandaranaike did not prolong it; he left the UNP and founded the Sri Lanka Freedom Party (SLFP) in 1951, marking the first split.

In the 1970 general election, the United Front government led by Sirimavo Bandaranaike won a two-thirds majority in Parliament. Following this, a cold war began between UNP leader Dudley Senanayake and deputy leader J.R. Jayewardene, representing the second leadership struggle. Many say the emotional distress from this was the cause of Dudley Senanayake’s death in 1973.

In 1991, a conflict arose between President Ranasinghe Premadasa and then-ministers Lalith Athulathmudali and Gamini Dissanayake, causing the UNP to split for the second time with the formation of the Democratic United National Front under Lalith and Gamini’s leadership. Many attribute different reasons for this: some say Gamini and Lalith came from elite families and did not wish to work under Premadasa, who came from a humble background. Others state that after Premadasa became President, he interfered in all sectors of his administration, leading his own ministers to call it a “one-man show,” and that this interference triggered the conflict.

Just as the second leadership clash ended with Dudley Senanayake’s death, the third conflict ended when Premadasa was assassinated by an LTTE suicide bomber on May Day in 1993. D.B. Wijetunga assumed the presidency and the party leadership then.

As the 1994 presidential election approached, Gamini Dissanayake rejoined the UNP and took over the party leadership. During a presidential campaign rally, he led in Thotalanga, Colombo, another LTTE suicide bomber attacked, killing around 60 people including Dissanayake.

Following this, Ranil Wickremesinghe took over the party leadership since which, three internal party rebellions have broken out against his leadership.

Within the first six years of Wickremesinghe’s leadership, the UNP lost several elections, including provincial council elections and the 2000 general election. The People’s Alliance led by President Chandrika Kumaratunga created a narrative that elections could not be won under Ranil’s leadership. This strongly impacted the frustrated second-tier UNP leaders. Consequently, in 2001, a group led by then-UNP General Secretary Gamini Atukorale emerged within the party, taking the stance that then-deputy leader Karu Jayasuriya should become party leader.

Perhaps because Wickremesinghe supported his father during the 1991 dispute, Sajith Premadasa supported Wickremesinghe during the 2001 rebellion led by Atukorale. When the UNP won the general election that same year and Wickremesinghe became Prime Minister, the internal fight ended though only for the moment.

After Sarath Fonseka, who ran with UNP support, lost the 2010 presidential election, another internal power struggle began — this time led by Sajith Premaasa. Karu Jayasuriya, who had opposed Wickremesinghe in 2001, backed him this time. This internal strife lasted from 2010 to 2014, until party secretary Tissa Attanayake brokered a compromise between the two factions in 2014. In January 2015, Maithripala Sirisena won the presidential election with UNP support and appointed Wickremesinghe as Prime Minister.

However, as the 2019 presidential election approached, Sajith rebelled again, becoming the leader of the Samagi Jana Balawegaya (SJB) in 2020. Except for a very few, most UNP members joined his party, marking the UNP’s third split. In the last general election, Wickremesinghe entered the fray as the leader of two parties—the UNP and the NDF—further fragmenting party votes. The culmination of these events has led to the UNP’s present predicament.

It was a UNP government that transformed an economy reliant on colonial plantation revenues into a liberal, diversified economy. Additionally, through the Mahaweli Development Programme, it constructed several large reservoirs and expanded agricultural development. Although these did not fully meet the country’s needs, the nation’s situation today would have been far worse without them.

Even if compelled by circumstances, it was a UNP government that introduced the Provincial Council system as a solution to the ethnic problem. However, lacking a consistent economic policy, development work under the party eventually stalled, and the UNP ultimately had to rely even on the Sri Lanka Podujana Peramuna (SLPP).
