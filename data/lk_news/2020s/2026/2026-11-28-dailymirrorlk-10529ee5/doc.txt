S&P upgrades Sri Lanka to ‘B-’

Revenue up 20% through September, helping govt. post a nine-month budget surplus

Reserves recover to US$ 7.09bn in October despite a higher fuel import bill

Interest still takes up about 40% of govt. revenue, with bigger external repayments ahead

Sri Lanka secured a sovereign credit upgrade from S&P Global Ratings yesterday, with the agency raising its long-term rating to ‘B-’ from ‘CCC+’ and judging a repeat of the 2022 default unlikely over the next two years. Stronger tax collections and a recovery in foreign reserves have given the Government more room to meet heavier external debt repayments in the coming years, S&P said. The agency retained a stable outlook on the long-term foreign- and local-currency ratings and raised both short-term ratings to ‘B’ from ‘C’. The move brings S&P’s long-term rating in line with Fitch, which upgraded Sri Lanka to ‘B-’ in September.

“The ‘B-’ ratings reflect our assessment that the improvements in Sri Lanka’s macroeconomic settings have become more entrenched,” S&P said.

Government revenue rose 20 percent in the first nine months of 2026, with gains across income taxes, domestic taxes on goods and services, and taxes on international trade. S&P projects general government revenue at 17.5 percent of GDP this year, nearly double the 8.9 percent recorded in 2022.

The agency expects a full-year fiscal deficit of 1.8 percent of GDP despite the surplus through September, with expenditure picking up in the final quarter. It forecasts the deficit to widen to 4.5 percent in 2027 as tax relief and higher capital spending take effect.

The stronger revenue base and the phasing out of expensive debt incurred in 2022 are easing the interest burden. S&P expects interest payments to absorb about 34 percent of revenue by 2029, down from about 40 percent this year. That forecast factors in higher coupon payments on Sri Lanka’s macro-linked bonds. Stronger economic performance and a firmer rupee compared with 2023 are likely to trigger terms that could also lift principal payouts by 17 percent to 22 percent, depending on the bond series. The external position remains vulnerable. Gross official reserves climbed from US$ 6.5 billion in June to US$ 7.09 billion in October, but S&P expects the current account to swing to a deficit of 1.4 percent of GDP this year from a surplus of 1.6 percent in 2025.

Tourism earnings fell 9.2 percent in the first nine months, while remittance growth slowed to 5.6 percent year-on-year in September from more than 30 percent in May. The Middle East accounts for about 40 percent of Sri Lanka’s remittances, leaving a key source of foreign exchange exposed to a prolonged war.

S&P expects growth to slow to 4 percent this year from 5 percent in 2025 before recovering to 4.5 percent in 2027. Its stable outlook assumes the government will continue rebuilding fiscal and external buffers, with revenue reforms and cost-recovery pricing for utilities expected to continue beyond the end of the IMF programme in March 2027.

The agency said it could lower the rating if a sharp deterioration in public finances or the external position revived funding and liquidity pressures. A further upgrade would require progress on at least two fronts, including a substantially lower interest burden, stronger external finances, a slower build-up of government debt or more stable financing conditions.
