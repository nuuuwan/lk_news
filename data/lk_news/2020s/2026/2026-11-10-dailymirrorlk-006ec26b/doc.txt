Formation of Israeli government held up by thorny political issues

Sami Abu Shehadeh, leader of Balad Party of the Arab List, said that Palestinian citizens of Israel are being treated as illegitimate partners despite their turnout

Last Saturday,  Israeli President Isaac Herzog said that he would ask the Yashar party chairman, Gadi Eisenkot, to explore the possibility of forming the post-election  government. Sixty seven of the 120 Members of the Knesset (MKs) had recommended Eisenkot for the position of Prime Minister, and only 46 backed Benjamin Netanyahu’s coalition. Seven had abstained.

But all is not well with the two coalitions. Parties are divided on many issues, but the ones which are difficult to solve are two: One, exempting the orthodox Jews (called Haredi or Haredim) from compulsory military service and secondly, striking an alliance with the Arab parties. Traditionally, alliance with any Arab party is considered politically suicidal by the Jewish parties.

The Haredim and their parties are adamantly demanding exemption from compulsory military service while all other Jewish parties consider it necessary, as Israel is constantly fighting with its Arab neighbours and now Iran also.

The Arabs, a minority in Israel, have become an important political factor now because the Arab parties have won 19 of the 120 seats in the Knesset. But Jewish parties do not want to be politically tarnished by accepting an alliance with the Arabs, their traditional enemies and rivals.

On October 20, a week before Israel’s elections, dozens of Orthodox Jews were detained on suspicion of evading compulsory military service or the draft. Those young men were students of Jewish religious schools long exempted from mandatory military service.

A 2024 court ruling had said that Haredi men must be drafted alongside other Jewish youth. The Netanyahu government failed to either legalise the draft exemption or enforce the court’s ruling. But  Netanyahu  said that Haredim who were not studying should enlist.

With Israel fighting on several conflicts, those exemptions have become increasingly unpopular. The majority of Jewish voters want Haredi communities to share the burden of fighting for the country. But Haredi political parties are key potential coalition members and never fail to have their way.

Haredi communities tend to prioritise religious Jewishness over responsibilities to the State. Interestingly, most Haredim do not consider themselves Zionist, even though Zionism is the foundational philosophy of the State of Israel. They argue that it was sacrilegious for Jews to artificially create a State of Israel instead of waiting for God to return Jews to the land of Israel. Where nationalist politicians emphasise the secular State and military service, the Haredim see their religious study itself as a service to the Jewish community at large.

Haredi Jews strictly observe Jewish religious law and disapprove of liberal interpretations. They typically live in Haredi-majority neighbourhoods, where large families are the norm – six children, on average. About half of the men devote themselves to full-time religious study. The poverty rate among them is 33% (more than double the percentage for non-Haredi Jews) according to a piece in the journal “The Conversation.”

Haredis are about 14%  of the Israeli population. But the community is growing quickly because of its high birth rate. Projections by the Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics estimate that almost a quarter of Israelis will be Haredi by 2050.

The Haredim or Haredi are represented by two main political parties. Those Haredim who are Ashkenazi (Jews of Northern and Eastern European origin) gravitate to the United Torah Judaism, or UTJ, and those who are Sephardi or Mizrachi (whose ancestors came from South Western Europe and the Middle East) gravitate to a party called Shas. A third Haredi party, Haredi Public, formed in July 2026, represents those who feel underserved by Shas and UT.

Likud, led by Benjamin Netanyahu, one of the major conservative Jewish parties, is seen by the Haredi parties as a natural ally. They have been coalition partners under Likud administrations. The fact that Likud has needed Haredi parties to form coalitions has given the latter a lot of negotiating power.

However, most Jewish Israelis see Haredi exemption from conscription as unfair and costly.  Governments have been subsidising the Haredis in many ways. Religious schools for teenage boys and even married Many Haredi families rely on government support.

In 2024, Israel’s Supreme Court ruled that a blanket exemption from military service for the Haredis was not legal. Netanyahu’s government struggled to enact a compromise, with the result Shas and UTJ left the Likud-led coalition in 2025.

But this did not lead to mass conscription. In 2026, fewer than 500 draft evaders have been arrested. In July 2026, at the urging of Haredi parties, the government passed a law to temporarily prevent the arrest of draft evaders.

Former Prime Minister Naftali Bennett – leader of the Together alliance (seen as a key contender to oust Netanyahu) has threatened to cut subsidies to the Haredi community if they continue to defy the draft. Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the centrist Yashar party (another top contender to replace Netanyahu) has proposed allowing only 3% of Haredi men exemption from the draft, and that too, only to enable them to study. Seven in 10 Jewish Israelis favour drafting the Haredim.

In July 2025, the United Torah Judaism (7 Knesset seats) and Noam (1 seat) withdrew from both government and coalition, while Shas (11 seats) strategically withdrew from government while remaining in the coalition to prevent complete governmental collapse. These moves reduced Netanyahu’s Knesset majority from 68 seats to precisely 60.

When the Haredi parties exited Netanyahu’s coalition, Likud replaced them with Jewish nationalist parties that emphasised the State. Particularly notable was the decision to include the Jewish Power party, an offshoot of an earlier group banned for racism.

The Arab parties, the “Joint List” and “Ra’am”, also hold the key to government formation in Israel. Israel has 2.1 million Arabs (20% of the population). The Arabs voted massively in the recent elections and got 19 out of 120 seats. “They’re the kingmakers,” said  journalist Christiane Amanpour on X.

But the more important question is whether those who want to form a government are willing to invite the Arab parties to join them.

At one extreme, there are voices like that of Yair Netanyahu, the son of the Prime Minister Netanyahu who said on X: “These elections proved that the greatest danger to the survival of the State of Israel, aside from the Iranian nuclear programme, is what is called Israeli Arabs. A country cannot survive long-term when 20% of its population identifies with its enemies and wants its destruction. Muslims cannot, and can never, live peacefully with “infidels” as their neighbours.”

The “Joint List” Arab bloc chairman, Ahmad Tibi, ignored that stinging comment. But a group of prominent Israelis put out an open petition saying: “The desire of the vast majority of Israel’s Arab citizens, as expressed in their voting patterns, to participate in the parliamentary process and to play a full and equal role in the affairs of the State, is of the utmost importance and must not be ignored. It is essential for the future of democracy and the fabric of Israeli society, as well as to the Israeli-Palestinian conflict.”

Given the fractured nature of Israeli Jewish politics, Arab participation in the country’s democracy would become critical for the stability and survival of governments, and for treaties and productive changes in the entire West Asian region.

As senior journalist Dan Perry wrote in an opinion piece for “The Jerusalem Post,” Israeli parties should “stop overthinking, thank the Arabs for their support, and get on with saving the country.”

While some observers have spoken of repeating the election to enable a Jewish grouping to emerge with a clear majority, President Herzog has ruled out a fresh poll. Thus, both the Netanyahu and the Eisenkot blocs have to find ways of forming a government.
