El Niño, Middle East crisis set to push food prices higher

Colombo, Aug. 19 (Daily Mirror) - In the wake of the El Niño weather phenomenon affecting global agriculture and the Middle East crisis creating hurdles for shipping and trade, prices of imported food items, including essential commodities, are likely to rise by 20-25 percent in the coming months, importers warn.

Leading food-producing countries tend to turn inward to protect their populations from the impact. India has already banned sugar exports, primarily to contain inflation in the country.

Against this backdrop, the Essential Food Commodities Importers’ and Traders’ Association has informed Trade Minister Wasantha Samarasinghe that prices of imported food commodities are likely to rise by 20-25 percent due to weather-related factors and the Middle East crisis, according to an association spokesman.

The spokesman told the Daily Mirror that sugar was being imported from Brazil following India’s export ban, resulting in higher costs.

“The voyage is much longer. India is also considering restrictions on the export of big onions. If that happens, we may have to import from the Netherlands. It will then be more expensive due to freight charges and other costs. It takes at least one month for delivery,” he said.

“We have assured a steady supply of these items, but prices will rise,” he added.

Freight charges have also increased by 20-30 percent, according to him. Sri Lanka imports most of its essential food items, including pulses, sugar, some varieties of rice, certain fruits, spices, wheat and canned fish.

Sri Lanka has also begun to feel the impact of El Niño. President Anura Kumara Dissanayake has established a Special Cabinet Subcommittee and an Officials’ Committee to oversee El Niño preparedness.

El Niño is a periodic warming of the central and eastern Pacific Ocean. Scientists officially declare an El Niño event when a stretch of the ocean remains more than half a degree Celsius warmer than normal for three consecutive months.

According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), El Niño conditions are already present and are expected to strengthen through the remainder of 2026, with a 97 percent probability that they will persist into early 2027.

For Sri Lanka, however, the eventual impact will depend not only on El Niño in the Pacific but also on the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), and on how these two climate drivers interact with the monsoons and shorter-term weather systems. Forecasts indicate that a positive IOD may develop alongside El Niño, although there is currently uncertainty regarding its timing and strength, the FAO said.

This interaction could create a particularly complex risk for Sri Lanka. The Department of Meteorology has indicated that rainfall during July and August may be below normal, particularly in the Dry Zone. From October onward, however, El Niño years are generally associated with above-normal rainfall, while a positive IOD could prolong enhanced rainfall until December.

Sri Lanka must therefore prepare not only for drought but also for a possible sequence of water scarcity followed by excessive rainfall, flooding, waterlogging and landslides.

This matters because national rice availability depends heavily on the performance of individual cultivation seasons. Sri Lanka produced approximately 5.05 million tonnes of paddy in 2025, broadly sufficient to meet most domestic requirements. However, the country does not have an adequate buffer to absorb the failure of a major cultivation season without significant consequences.

A significant loss of the Maha or Yala production could quickly increase rice prices and import requirements. The persistence of El Niño into early 2027 also raises concerns about reservoir carry-over and the conditions under which the 2027 Yala season will commence.
