S&P rating exposes limits of Sri Lanka’s post-crisis recovery

Sri Lanka may have moved beyond the immediate threat of another debt or payment crisis; however, its sovereign credit profile remains highly vulnerable to the external shocks, with the pressure on the reserves, rupee and foreign inflows, leaving little room for policy error.

S&P Global Ratings this week affirmed Sri Lanka’s long and short-term sovereign credit ratings at ‘CCC+/C’, with a stable outlook, reflecting expectations that the economic growth and stronger government revenue will sustain fiscal repair over the next six to 12 months, despite the worsening external conditions.

Yet, the rating remains deep in speculative-grade territory, indicating that Sri Lanka’s ability to meet its obligations continues to depend on the favourable economic and financial conditions.

The stable outlook is therefore not a clean bill of health. S&P does not expect a material improvement in sovereign credit support over the next six to 12 months, as the risks to the external demand, inflation and financing conditions remain significant.

Sri Lanka’s vulnerability is also shifting from the budget to balance of payments.

The government revenue surged 34 percent in 2025 to 16.7 percent of gross domestic product (GDP), helping narrow the fiscal deficit to 2.3 percent of GDP and lift the primary surplus to a record 5.4 percent. Revenue rose a further 30 percent during the first five months of 2026, despite a high comparison base.

This has eased the debt-servicing burden. S&P expects the interest payments to decline from 64 percent of revenue in 2024 to 39 percent by 2029, supported by a continued revenue growth and the phasing out of the expensive debt incurred during the 2022 crisis.

Even then, nearly four of every 10 rupees collected by the government would be absorbed by the interest payments, constraining spending on infrastructure, healthcare, education

The government debt also remains elevated and S&P forecasts the net general government debt, including the guarantees to the state-owned enterprises, at about 92 percent of GDP in 2026, declining to around 83 percent by 2029.

The more immediate pressure, however, is external, as the fuel imports more than doubled year-on-year in rupee terms during April and May, while the rupee depreciated around 8 percent against the US dollar in the first half. The gross official reserves fell to US $ 6.45 billion in June, from US $ 6.88 billion in May.

S&P said it expects the current account to swing to a deficit of 1.7 percent of GDP in 2026, as the imports rise and uncertainty clouds the remittances and tourism, two of Sri Lanka’s main foreign-exchange earners. The Middle East accounts for around 40 percent of remittance inflows, while roughly one-third of tourists transit through the aviation hubs in the region. A prolonged conflict could therefore hit Sri Lanka through a higher energy bill, weaker remittances and disrupted tourist traffic.

Signs of moderation have already emerged. The remittance growth slowed to 9 percent in June, from more than 30 percent in May, while the tourist arrivals fell nearly 10 percent during the month. The gross external financing needs, measured against the current-account receipts and usable reserves, are projected to rise to around 110 percent in 2026, from 104 percent last year. Although below the pre-crisis average of more than 120 percent, the increase highlights Sri Lanka’s continued dependence on foreign inflows and official support. S&P expects about US $ 1.7 billion from the International Monetary Fund and other multilateral institutions this year, partly offsetting the weaker trade balance and helping the country meet its near-term funding requirements.

The authorities have also allowed the exchange rate to absorb part of the shock, instead of aggressively defending the rupee with reserves. The Central Bank raised its policy rate by 100 basis points in May, while the period allowed for converting the export proceeds was shortened.

While these measures could protect the reserves and anchor the inflation expectations, they also carry domestic costs through higher borrowing rates, imported inflation and weaker household purchasing power. Consumer inflation accelerated to 6.8 percent by end-June. Economic activity has nevertheless remained stronger than S&P previously expected. Real GDP expanded 4.8 percent in the fourth quarter of 2025 and 5.1 percent in the first quarter of 2026.

Growth is forecast to slow to 3.8 percent this year, as the higher energy and input costs weigh on the activity, before recovering to 4.2 percent in 2027, as the supply-chain conditions normalise.
