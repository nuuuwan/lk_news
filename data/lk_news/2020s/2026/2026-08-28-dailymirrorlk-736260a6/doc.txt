Enforced disappearances in Sri Lanka : Grieving families  of missing persons still  await closure

Families of disappeared persons at a demonstration seeking justice for their loved ones

Grieving families have been agitating on the streets, holding demonstrations with a unified voice to seek justice for their loved ones

The OMP was setup to investigate on enforced disappearances, provide relief to victims, and to investigate enforced disappearances

Office on Missing Persons has received a total of 42,000 complaints regarding missing persons

Families of individuals subjected to enforced disappearances have been engaged in a lifelong struggle to unearth what happened to their loved ones during different conflict periods in Sri Lanka’s history. From the 1971 JVP insurrections onwards to the end of the 30-year armed conflict to the recent prison riots, these families have been agitating on the streets, holding demonstrations with a unified voice to seek justice for their loved ones. As a result of these struggles and international interventions such as the Geneva UN Human Rights Council, the Office on Missing Persons (OMP) Act No. 14 of 2016 was passed in Parliament in 2016 and the office was subsequently established in 2018. At the time, enforced disappearances left a black mark on Sri Lanka. The OMP was setup to investigate on enforced disappearances, provide relief to victims, and to find out what happened to people who were subjected to enforced disappearances. Its main objective was to ensure the nonoccurrence of enforced disappearances in Sri Lanka. But to what extent this objective has been fulfilled remains a question.

Speaking at a special media briefing held recently in view of the International Day of Victims of Enforced Disappearances, OMP Chairman, attorney-at-law Mahesh Katulanda said that so far they have received a total of 42,000 complaints regarding missing persons. These complaints have been lodged by various multinational agencies, committees and Presidential commissions.

He said that some of them had been complaints about the same person. “Our main challenge was to filter these complaints. Back then we didn’t have a proper database. During the 30-year conflict, some people didn’t have identification cards, there were some who left their houses without any documents. During 1971, 1988/1989 technology wasn’t quite advanced. All records were handwritten on chits and books. There were practical issues in identifying individuals due to spelling errors. So we passed an era of manually recording all details,” he said.

Katulanda said that following painstaking efforts by OMP officers, they had managed to reduce the recurrence of multiple entries. As of today a list of over 23,352 missing persons has been included into their database.  “Amongst them we are maintaining files of 16,966 civilians who were subject to enforced disappearances. We are also investigating into the disappearances of military officers and police officers who had gone missing during the conflict. So far we have done one-on-one interviews with members of nearly 9700 families and have collected direct and indirect information related to the disappearances, the background that led to the disappearance and have provided support to these families as part of preliminary investigations,” he added.

He further said that the incumbent government is currently reviewing how a ‘revival payment’ could be disbursed to support the immediate needs of these families and that the Justice Ministry is working in this regard. “A sum of Rs. 375 million was provided to OMP to initiate preliminary proceedings. Therefore this government seems to be having a political willingness to resolve these issues. We have been given approval to recruit 69 officers to expedite investigations. These officers include lawyers, tracing experts, experts to provide psychosocial support. Therefore we are able to function as an independent body despite being a government institution. The revival payment has already been given to over 5700 individuals while nearly 2000 individuals have been recommended to receive the payment. They will receive it over the next one or two months. In future we plan to reduce the waiting lists and ensure that these families would be able to see some light at the end of the tunnel,” he affirmed.

In his remarks, OMP Commissioner Mirak Raheem said that there’s a misperception among the general public as to whether these families can move on. “The truth is, as the state, there’s a duty and an obligation based on an ample number of provisions in the Constitution as well as basic laws such as the Enforced Disappearances act to provide answers and other forms of redress including compensation to these families. So the OMP represents the commitment of the Sri Lankan state to provide these answers and that is a vision we are firmly committed to,” he told media representatives.

He further said that in 2016 a change was introduced where families of the disappeared were able to opt for a Certificate of Absence (CoA). “Many families were quite nervous about accepting a certificate of disappeared. They thought the minute they accept a certificate of disappeared the government would close the file and that they wouldn’t trace the individual. The CoA is therefore an attempt to try and provide some of the services you could access similarly to when you receive a certificate of death. But it is not expansive as a certificate of death. Based on OMP recommendations, 335 families have now obtained a certificate of absence,” he added.

According to Raheem another key task falling under OMP is the observation of mass grave cases. He said that since 2018 there have been 17 cases and out of that, 6 are currently ongoing. “This involves a series of tasks under the observation role including facilitating funding and making recommendations to the court if we feel that specific steps should be taken to assist investigations. In other cases it includes assisting investigators who sometimes feel they would like some additional technical expertise or training so that they can better investigate these cases. In each case the OMP has tried to advocate for a multidisciplinary approach. The OMP recognises that mass graves are complex and not as straightforward as normal homicides and it requires specific skills such as forensic archaeology, forensic anthropology and forensic genetics. In the short term the OMP is trying to come up with recommendations and measures to address these needs in ongoing cases and we are also working towards strengthening the capacity into the long term by working with different sectors in the state,” he added.

The Committee for Protecting Rights of Prisoners (CPRP) has lodged a formal complaint to the Human Rights Commission as well as the OMP regarding cases of at least three inmates at the Negombo prison who have been reported missing following the unrest which took place on July 6 and 7. CPRP claims that officials are not disclosing information regarding these inmates and that it is a violation of Section 3(1) of the International convention for the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearances Act No. 5 of 2018. This section states that any public officer or acting in an official capacity, or any person acting with the authorization, support or acquiescence of the State arrests, detains, wrongfully confines, abducts, kidnaps, or in any other form deprives any other person of such person’s liberty, any such person who refuses to acknowledge such arrest, detention, wrongful confinement, abduction, kidnapping, or deprivation of liberty; or conceals the fate of such other person; or fails or refuses to disclose or is unable without valid excuse to disclose the subsequent or present whereabouts of such other person, shall be guilty of the offense of enforced disappearance. Out of a list of six missing inmates, the whereabouts of at least four individuals are not yet known. But following complains prison authorities have confirmed that these individuals have been found, but their family members were not given an opportunity to speak to them. However, according to CPRP Executive Director, attorney-at-law Senaka Perera, Section 16(1) of the Act guarantees that a detained person’s relatives, legal representatives and attorneys-at-law have full access to official registers and records of persons deprived of their liberty. He said that concealing information regarding these inmates is similar to an enforced disappearance. “Their family members and close relatives have a right to know the whereabouts, health conditions and other information regarding their loved ones. We believe that the OMP could take a more proactive approach to investigate into this matter and confirm what has happened to these inmates,” Perera added. He further said that among the list of missing inmates was a Venezuelan national who was later found and that his relatives had been able to speak to him via video call. “The offense of concealing information by the Prison authorities cannot be rectified now as they have ignored their responsibilities. Prison officers are entitled to provide information regarding these inmates as per the Enforced Disappearances Act No. 5 of 2018. The relatives haven’t had any information regarding their loved ones for 27 days and it is a very unfortunate situation. Therefore we believe that existing mechanisms should be expedited to serve justice to these inmates and their relatives,” he underscored.

When asked about the cases of missing inmates, OMP Chairman Mahesh Katulanda told the Daily Mirror that the OMP is following these cases. “We have received a formal complaint and we are now in the process of calling their close relatives to obtain statements as part of the investigations,” he added.
