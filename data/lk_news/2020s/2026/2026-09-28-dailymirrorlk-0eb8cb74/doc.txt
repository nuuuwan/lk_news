Trust is the ultimate currency of every profession: Capital Maharaja Group CEO

From left: AAT Sri Lanka Chief Executive Officer Tishanga Kumarasinghe, President Dr. Chamara Bandara, Capital Maharaja Group CEO Suresh Srinivasan, Vice President Dayal Abhayasinghe, Chief Operating Officer Himali Dasanayake and Conference Committee Chairman and Council Member Isuru Kalhara

Trust built on integrity is the ultimate currency of every profession, Capital Maharaja Group CEO Suresh Srinivasan said, urging accounting professionals to hold firm to their values as technology reshapes their work.

Srinivasan was delivering the Chief Guest address at the AAT Sri Lanka Conference 2026, held under the theme ‘Precision to Power’ on 8 and 9 September at Waters Edge, Battaramulla.

The two-day conference brought together industry leaders, professionals and students for sessions on entrepreneurship, governance, data-driven decision-making and transformative leadership.

While the pace of technological change will only accelerate, he noted that the fundamentals of the profession remain constant: integrity, objectivity, professional competence, confidentiality and professional behaviour.

“These foundations build trust, which is the ultimate currency of every profession. That is how we move from precision to power,” he stressed.

Tracing his roots to a modest family in southern India, where his father served as a government employee for 40 years, Srinivasan recalled that his earliest lessons in ethics came from home rather than the classroom.

“Long before I learned about integrity through a professional code of ethics, I learned it by watching my father live,” he told delegates.

He also shared how a classmate outperformed him at the O-Level examinations, scoring 471 out of 500 against his own 417. “In a room full of accounting professionals, I don’t need to explain that a small transposition can have a material impact,” he quipped, drawing laughter from the audience. He went on to perform strongly at A-Level two years later, having “learned how to learn.”

A turning point came at Britannia, where he accepted an operational role others had declined, running packaging operations involving hundreds of employees and strong union representation.

“That one ‘yes’ changed my career and taught me that leadership begins when we are willing to own the outcomes,” he reflected.

At 40, while at CavinKare, a serious health challenge left him considering a university post at a fraction of his salary, until his boss persuaded him to stay and later entrusted him with the entire supply chain.

“At a time when I was losing confidence in myself, someone else still believed in me,” Srinivasan explained.

After joining E.I.D. Parry in 2014, his team revived a dormant sugar refining business, taking it from significant losses to break-even within 22 months before expanding into value-added products such as brown, low-calorie and low-GI sugar.

“At the end of our careers, we will all have a personal balance sheet showing assets and wealth, but there is one line no accounting standard will require us to disclose: how many people are better off because we were part of their journey?”
