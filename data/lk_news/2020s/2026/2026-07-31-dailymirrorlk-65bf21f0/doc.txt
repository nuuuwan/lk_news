Judges’ retirement age extension Lal Kantha asked me to keep silent:  Lal Wijenayake

To create a civilised country and produce responsible citizens, we need the independence of the judiciary

Senior lawyer Lal Wijenayake, who, as a member of the leadership council, the executive committee and the national operation committee of the ruling National People’s Power (NPP), raised objections to the government’s move to extend the retirement age of judges. In an interview with Daily Mirror, he detailed out reasons that led to his decision and implication of interference with the judiciary by any ruler. Excerpts:

Q. What are the circumstances that led to your dissent as a member of the Executive Committee and the Leadership Council of the National People’s Power (NPP)?

We have been fighting for judicial independence for a long time. Whenever there have been attacks on the independence of the judiciary, we have come forward and fought against them.

I have practised law for fifty-odd years. First, Felix Dias Bandaranaike attacked the judiciary, and we fought against that. Similarly, there have been several instances where the independence of the judiciary was challenged.

What we feel is that unless you have an independent judiciary, you cannot build a society where there is equality and democracy. To create a civilised country and produce responsible citizens, we need the independence of the judiciary.

There are three arms of government — the Executive, the Legislature and the Judiciary. The Executive and the Legislature have their own functions. Then you have the citizens. Citizens may come into conflict with the Legislature as well as the Executive at certain points in their dealings with society. It is the Judiciary that adjudicates those disputes.

Unless you have an independent judiciary, the Executive and the Legislature will dominate over citizens, and people will not have the right to challenge them even if their rights are violated, whether they are human rights or personal rights. Therefore, judicial independence is essential to establish a civilised country.

One of the pillars of judicial independence is that during the period of service of judges, their service conditions should not be interfered with. Otherwise, the Executive and the Legislature can influence the judiciary by either offering benefits or withdrawing existing benefits. They can use such measures as a threat to interfere with judicial independence.

They can say, “We will increase your salary if you do this, or reduce it otherwise.” Felix Dias Bandaranaike attempted something similar in 1972 or 1973 when he tried to withdraw the official vehicles provided to judges.

One important condition for protecting judicial independence is that the service conditions of judges should not be changed during their period of office. If a judge is appointed for four years, nobody should be able to remove that judge before the completion of those four years. This is one of the pillars that protects judicial independence, democracy and other rights.

For example, if I apply for a licence to build a house and my application is rejected in an unjustified manner, I should have access to a remedy. I should be able to approach the Judiciary. The Judiciary then decides the dispute between the State, which refuses to grant the permit, and me, and determines what is legally correct.

One way of interfering with the Judiciary is by interfering with the conditions of service of judges. In this case, they say they are going to extend the period of service. But the next government could say they are reducing the retirement age and remove some judges from office.

Who will then give orders against the State? The State can say, “We will reduce the retirement age to 55,” and some judges will have to leave. Therefore, one cardinal pillar of judicial independence is that the service conditions of judges should not be interfered with during their period of service.

Q. As a member of the Leadership Council of the NPP, when did you raise this matter with the party? When were you asked to keep quiet?

Actually, internally, I was discussing this matter with our members, though not officially. During those discussions, I said that this should not be done because it would be detrimental to the independence of the judiciary.

They must have come to know about it. One member gave me a call one day — a member of the Leadership Council, the Political Bureau and other bodies. He did not say anything like he was a dictator or anything of that nature. He simply said, “Why don’t you keep silent on this matter?”

Yes. Then I told him, “I have been fighting for the independence of the judiciary. So how can I not fight against something that affects it?”

Then they called for a meeting of the leaders of what they call the “National Operation Committee.” They summoned that meeting.

In the meantime, I wrote to the member who spoke to me. Over the telephone, I did not want to speak for long because I knew things could be misunderstood. So I wrote a letter explaining my position and sent copies to President Anura Kumara Dissanayake, as the leader of the party, and Secretary Dr. Nihal Abeysinghe, along with a few others.

Then they called a meeting. I attended that meeting. They asked me to say what I had to say. I referred to the letter I had sent and almost read it out.

Then President Anura Kumara Dissanayake explained his reasons for wanting this change. He gave his arguments and was critical of certain legal provisions as well as aspects of the legal system.

After that, I told him that what he said was true to a certain extent — perhaps even to a greater extent. But I said this was not the solution to the problem.

After that, they did not take a decision. They moved on to discuss other matters, such as the economy. The meeting ended there.

No decision was taken by any of those bodies — either the Leadership Council or the Executive Council. They never formally took a decision. Therefore, I assumed to a certain extent that they had given up the idea.

Later, I came to know that the proposal was going to be introduced. Therefore, I had to publicly state that I was against it.

Whenever people tried to interfere with the judiciary in the past, we openly criticised them and fought against such moves. I am against this as well. That is what happened.

Q. Previous governments made similar attempts. You are aware of the repercussions they faced. How do you relate the current situation to your past experiences?

The impeachment of former Chief Justice Shirani Bandaranayake by the Mahinda Rajapaksa government was one such instance where we fought against interference with the judiciary.

Felix Dias Bandaranaike was a very powerful person. It was difficult to find anyone who could challenge him. He was powerful and popular within his circles. We fought against him. Mrs. Bandaranaike intervened and, to some extent, tried to correct the situation.

At the next election, what happened? He lost his seat. After that, he could not return to politics.

Similarly, those who tried to interfere with the judiciary have not survived politically. It is the same story with Mr. Mahinda Rajapaksa. Every time a person in government attempts to interfere with the judiciary, they have not survived politically.

Q On this issue, you have dissented with the party. What will be your future participation in NPP activities?

No, I do not know because, so far, they have not asked me for any explanation or taken any action against me. There has been no official party decision.

They cannot find fault with me because they have not taken a formal decision. If they take a decision and say that I have violated party discipline by not following it, then they can find fault with me.

I also have to consider whether I can remain in the party if the party takes such a decision. But that has not happened so far.

Q You mentioned that a member of the NPP Politburo or the JVP contacted you. Is it possible for you to name him?

It is K.D. Lal Kantha (Minister of Agriculture). I sent him a letter. He is based in the Kandy area and is almost in charge of that area.

He is from the JVP, not from the NPP. He is the person who spoke to me and asked me to keep quiet.

They did it politely because they have treated me very well personally. They respect me so much that sometimes it is embarrassing. I have no personal problem with them at all.

This is a matter of principle. What I strongly believe is that, to live in a civilised society, there must be the rule of law. The rule of law can prevail only if there is an independent judiciary.

I want to live in a civilised society. I feel that we are too old, but the future generation should also have the right to live in a civilised society as responsible citizens. We have enjoyed that privilege for so long.

We do not want, at this stage of our lives, to become a party responsible for destroying that. Therefore, I have to do whatever is possible on my part to protect it.

Q So now, do you actually think the government will back down because a lot of pressure is being brought against it?

I doubt whether they will back down because of the pressure. If you look at the NPP, it consists of around 25 organisations. Out of those, only a very few are political parties — perhaps two or three. The others are trade union federations and various organisations, such as the Teachers’ Federation, Agricultural Workers’ Federation and others.

I have been in the Lanka Sama Samaja Party for about 50 years. I am a breakaway from the Sama Samaja Party because they decided to support Mahinda Rajapaksa, and that is how I separated from them. So, I have experience of working within the Sama Samaja Party and understand how decisions were taken in a traditional left party.

For example, in 1964, when we were at university, I was a member of the Sama Samaja Party. There was a proposal for the party to support Mrs. Bandaranaike’s coalition government. The party held a conference that continued for four days. Only after four days of discussion did they take a vote and decide that the party should join the coalition.

Those who opposed the decision, including myself and Professor Kumar David, who was also a faculty member at the university, expressed our views at that conference.

I am therefore used to a system where decisions are taken after wide-ranging discussions with all interested parties.

However, in the NPP, I do not see that kind of process. I doubt whether they will be influenced by outside pressure because decisions seem to be taken somewhere, but it is not clear where those decisions are actually being made. That is the problem.
