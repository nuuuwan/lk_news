Tobacco industry stands to lose from proposed tobacco-free generation – Dr. Bandara

Colombo, Oct. 7 (Daily Mirror) - The tobacco industry could be the biggest financial loser if Sri Lanka introduces the proposed tobacco-free generation policy, as the measure would gradually prevent tobacco companies from accessing new generations of consumers, Dr. Dineth Bandara has argued.

Responding to criticism of the proposed policy, Dr. Bandara said the measure should be viewed in the context of the long-term impact of tobacco use on Sri Lankan society, rather than through concerns about an immediate disruption to the tobacco market.

The proposal would prohibit the sale of tobacco products to anyone born on or after January 1, 2010.

Dr. Bandara said that the measure would place the responsibility on sellers and would not criminalise young people for possessing or attempting to obtain tobacco products.

He also pointed out that Sri Lanka already prohibits the sale of tobacco products to anyone below 21 under the National Authority on Tobacco and Alcohol Act.

Therefore, the 2010 birth cohort would not reach the current legal purchasing age until 2031, meaning the proposed policy would not immediately prevent existing adult smokers from legally purchasing cigarettes, he said.

According to Dr. Bandara, the policy would instead gradually reduce the pool of people legally eligible to purchase tobacco products as successive generations reach adulthood.

He described this as an attempt to prevent new smokers from replacing those who quit, become seriously ill or die from tobacco-related diseases.

Dr. Bandara cited estimates that tobacco kills around 22,000 Sri Lankans each year, arguing that the public-health consequences should remain central to the debate.

He compared the tobacco market to a bathtub, with existing smokers representing the water already in the tub and new smokers representing water entering through a tap.

While measures such as taxation, education and smoking cessation services could reduce the number of new smokers, he said a tobacco-free generation policy was intended to eventually “turn off” that source.

Dr. Bandara also rejected claims that the policy would necessarily result in a major increase in illicit tobacco sales.

He argued that examples from countries such as Bhutan and South Africa, where broader tobacco restrictions created different enforcement challenges, should not be directly compared with a generational sales restriction.

He said the proposed policy would not suddenly remove tobacco from existing adult smokers who are currently legally entitled to purchase it.

Dr. Bandara further argued that concerns over cigarette prices were irrelevant to the proposal because the policy itself would not increase the price of cigarettes.

He said illicit tobacco remained an issue requiring enforcement, but warned against using unverified or industry-generated estimates as the sole basis for assessing the potential impact of the proposed policy.

The proposal has also been defended by reference to international tobacco-control policies. Dr. Bandara pointed to countries including the Maldives and the United Kingdom, as well as Norway's tobacco-control strategy, which has included a goal of preventing tobacco and nicotine use among people born from 2010 onwards.

He said taxation, education, cessation services and enforcement should continue alongside a tobacco-free generation policy rather than being treated as alternatives.

According to Dr. Bandara, the central question was whether Sri Lanka was prepared to prevent future generations from becoming new consumers of a product responsible for a significant number of premature deaths.

He argued that if the policy succeeds, its long-term commercial impact would be felt by the tobacco industry because fewer young people would legally enter the tobacco market.

“Someone does stand to lose money,” Dr. Bandara said, arguing that the financial consequences of the policy would ultimately fall on an industry dependent on replacing smokers who quit or die.

The proposed tobacco-free generation policy remains subject to debate, with concerns also being raised over enforcement, illicit trade, individual choice and its possible unintended consequences.
