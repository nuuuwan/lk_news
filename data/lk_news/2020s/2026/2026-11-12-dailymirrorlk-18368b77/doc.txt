The fall  of a giant:  How the SLFP lost its recognition

Following a leadership dispute within the UNP in 1951, S.W.R.D. Bandaranaike broke away and founded the SLFP

Though largely ignored today and unknown to the younger generation, the Sri Lanka Freedom Party is an institution that left a massive imprint on Sri Lankan political history.

Decades earlier, in the early 1980s, the SLFP  faced another massive leadership tussle when former Minister Maithripala Senanayake challenged the leadership of Sirimavo Bandaranaike.

The Elections Commission has informed the factions fighting for the leadership of the Sri Lanka Freedom Party (SLFP) that the SLFP will no longer be considered a recognised political party, due to ongoing leadership disputes.

The Commission stated last Friday that the core reason for this decision is the failure of the party’s leaders to resolve their internal conflicts for a long time.

Once a dominant political force that played an important role as both the ruling party and the main opposition since 1951, the SLFP was almost wiped off the political landscape following the 2019 presidential election. Compounding its decline, infighting has escalated dramatically since 2022, leading to 18 lawsuits mutually being filed against various leaders of the party.

Former Minister Nimal Siripala de Silva is said to be the party leader now. He noted last January that only one out of 18 cases remained unresolved—the one filed against the party leadership by Dayasiri Jayasekara, the current Samagi Jana Balawegaya (SJB) MP who previously served as the SLFP General Secretary. Jayasekara filed the lawsuit claiming his removal from the General Secretary post was illegal.

Legally, only the General Secretary of a party is authorised to communicate officially with the Elections Commission on behalf of the party. However, communication between the Elections Commission and the SLFP became severely entangled because of this pending litigation, ultimately leading the Commission to suspend the party’s official recognition.

Remarkably, this development concerning a party that ruled the country for over 40 years did not become major news in the national media. Some media outlets chose not to publish it at all. The primary reason is that the party had already lost its political relevance years ago. Among the country’s younger generation, many might not even know that a party called the SLFP once governed Sri Lanka.

This obscurity can be attributed to the party not having contested any election under the standalone “SLFP” name as a major contender since the 1989 general election, 38 years ago. Following defeats in the 1988 presidential election and the 1989 general election, the SLFP contested and won the 1994 general and presidential elections under the banner of the People’s Alliance (PA), a coalition, led by Chandrika Kumaratunga. Several leftist parties were also part of that coalition.

Later, in 2003, the SLFP joined forces with the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP) to form the United People’s Freedom Alliance (UPFA). Back then the JVP’s  primary objective was to topple the incumbent United National Party (UNP) government, on the grounds that the UNP was granting excessive concessions to the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE) during peace talks. In the 2004 general election, the JVP contested under this UPFA alliance and won 41 seats. However, it withdrew from the coalition in 2005 after the Kumaratunga-led government accepted a joint tsunami relief mechanism involving the LTTE. Despite their exit, the SLFP, under the leadership of Mahinda Rajapaksa, continued to operate as the dominant entity within that alliance.

The true collapse of the SLFP — where it lost its status as one of the country’s two major political parties – happened when Maithripala Sirisena defected from the Mahinda Rajapaksa-led government to contest the upcoming presidential election against Rajapaksa.

Decades earlier, in the early 1980s, the SLFP faced another massive leadership tussle when former Minister Maithripala Senanayake challenged the leadership of Sirimavo Bandaranaike. Her son Anura Bandaranaike, also sided with Maithripala Senanayake. At one point, the SLFP headquarters on Darley Road in Colombo was even targeted in a bomb attack. However, both factions eventually resolved the dispute accepting the status quo in leadership.

Unlike that Maithripala, this Maithripala (Maithripala Sirisena) successfully challenged the party leadership, won the 2015 presidential election with the backing of UNP votes, defeating Mahinda Rajapaksa. Within a week of the election conclusion, Rajapaksa voluntarily handed over the SLFP leadership to Maithripala Sirisena and handed over the leadership of the UPFA as well within two months.

However, the vast majority of loyalists within the SLFP and the broader UPFA remained steadfast supporters of Rajapaksa. In 2016, when Basil Rajapaksa formed the Sri Lanka Podujana Peramuna (SLPP) under Mahinda Rajapaksa’s leadership, those loyalists also change party allegiance. Sirisena was left with only a handful of supporters and the empty nameplate of the “SLFP.”

Consequently, the SLFP name was barely visible on ballot papers after 1988. In the 2020 general election, only a tiny fraction of the SLFP contested at the 2020 general election, but Angajan Ramanathan who contested in Jaffna District was the only candidate to win under the party name; some other SLFP members contested under the symbol of Basil Rajapaksa’s new party (SLPP). When these members joined Gotabaya Rajapaksa’s cabinet in 2022, party leader Maithripala Sirisena attempted to take disciplinary action against them. They in turn retaliated with legal action against Sirisena. These internal power struggles led to the filing of the 18 mutual lawsuits. Though largely ignored today and unknown to the younger generation, the Sri Lanka Freedom Party is an institution that left a massive imprint on Sri Lankan political history. Around the time Sri Lanka gained independence, a new capitalist class emerged outside of the traditional landed elite class which was represented by the UNP. Following a leadership dispute within the UNP in 1951, S.W.R.D. Bandaranaike broke away and founded the SLFP. The newly emerged capitalist class immediately rallied behind him. As a result, the party secured a landslide victory in the 1956 general election, and Bandaranaike became Prime Minister. The UNP, which had been the ruling party until then, was reduced to just 8 seats.

The Bandaranaike government—and subsequently the government of his wife, Sirimavo Bandaranaike, who took power after his assassination—enacted several policies considered progressive at the time. A key milestone was S.W.R.D. Bandaranaike removing the British naval and air bases that had remained in Sri Lanka even after independence. Fifteen years later, Mrs. Bandaranaike severed all remaining administrative ties with Britain and declared Sri Lanka a Republic in 1972.

It was during S.W.R.D. Bandaranaike’s tenure that the Employees’ Provident Fund (EPF) was established for workers. May Day was declared a public holiday, private bus transport services were nationalised, and the Paddy Lands Act was passed to safeguard the welfare of agricultural tenants. Although he took the initial steps toward the devolution of power through the Bandaranaike-Chelvanayakam Pact, he later tore up the agreement due to fierce opposition from Buddhist monks.

Although she entered politics without any prior experience, Sirimavo Bandaranaike who became the world’s first female prime minister nationalised the oil companies, which had been controlled by powerful foreign multinationals in 1961. Similarly, during the 1962 Sino-Indian War, she took the initiative to mediate peace between the two nations with the assistance of five non-aligned countries. This earned her praise from both belligerent countries and the world at large. Today, however, these milestones remain nothing more than historical nostalgia for the SLFP. Stripped of support by the people and of its official recognition by the Elections Commission, the party now hangs precariously in political limbo.
