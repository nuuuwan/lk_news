Can ASUS crack the c-suite and state tenders amid influencer push?

​Taiwanese hardware major ASUS is seeking to convert its rapid retail momentum into lucrative enterprise and public sector accounts in Sri Lanka, though industry observers caution that breaking the institutional stranglehold of legacy commercial PC vendors will require far more than lifestyle marketing and consumer hype.

​While the brand has more than doubled its domestic PC market share from roughly 18 percent in the second quarter of last year to approximately 40 percent, its presence has largely been built on consumer retail channels, prosumers, and student buyers. Making meaningful inroads into corporate boardrooms, institutional fleets, and high-value government procurement cycles presents a fundamentally different test of institutional credibility.

​Globally, the commercial and C-suite fleet landscape remains dominated by the “Big Three”—Lenovo, HP, and Dell—which collectively command over 60 percent of global personal computer shipments and an even larger share of enterprise deployments. While ASUS accounts for roughly 7 percent of global PC shipments, its enterprise footprint remains modest compared to its consumer presence. Corporate Chief Information Officers (CIOs) and government tender boards routinely default to established enterprise ecosystems due to long-term lifecycle management, centralized IT fleet deployment infrastructure, and dedicated multi-year service-level agreements.

​To challenge these entrenched players, ASUS is deploying its dedicated commercial portfolio, spearheaded by the new ExpertBook Ultra alongside the expanded ExpertBook B and P series. The company is positioning these lightweight, neural-processing-equipped machines as modern alternatives for C-suite executives, MSME leaders, and mobile decision-makers.

​Addressing why ASUS continues to prioritize high-end silicon in Sri Lanka despite macro pressures, Dinesh Sharma, Vice President, Commercial PC Business, ASUS India, Sri Lanka and Nepal, emphasized that premium demand remains resilient:

​“Of course, the overall market capacity isn’t as large as that of our bigger neighbors. However, Sri Lanka has top-end users and technology enthusiasts who want the latest innovations. While we may not bring in massive quantities, we must make these products available to meet that demand. The needs and demands of Sri Lanka should not be neglected; every market is valid.”

​However, unit economics remain a steep barrier for broad corporate rollouts. Mainstream AI-enabled commercial models begin at Rs. 254,490, while top-tier executive ultrabooks and specialized high-performance workstations climb past Rs. 1,112,990.

​“Our entry-level pricing starts around Rs. 250,000, though pricing varies across different channels. Last year, costs were higher. This year, we’re using the same chips in both top-tier and entry-level devices. This strategy aims to expand our reach from the top of the market to the broader consumer base,” Sharma explained. He added that the company is actively investing in enterprise and user education: “While AI is still relatively new and can be somewhat expensive, we’re working to make it more affordable. With support from Qualcomm and our regional headquarters, we’re also providing training and educational resources to help users understand how to effectively use AI on their devices, beyond just using it for things like ChatGPT.”

​To build brand cachet outside traditional enterprise channels, ASUS has frequently leveraged digital creators and social media influencers across platforms like TikTok and Instagram. Yet, this reliance on paid lifestyle creators has drawn critical scrutiny across global tech markets, where regulators and industry watchdogs have repeatedly questioned the boundary between independent product validation and sponsored commercial endorsement. For conservative corporate boards, enterprise CIOs, and public sector procurement committees, promotional influencer campaigns carry little weight compared to rigorous enterprise security compliance, data protection, and guaranteed local hardware turnaround times.

​Sharma maintained that the creator and digital economy represents a legitimate and rapidly expanding segment of professional computing demand:

​“Beyond gamers, there’s also demand from creators and those who need powerful graphics for video editing and high-quality viewing experiences. The demand is increasing, driven by the growth of social media creators and influencers on platforms like TikTok and Instagram. We are paying attention to that demand.”

​The vendor is also setting its sights on larger institutional demand, including potential digital infrastructure requirements linked to major development hubs such as the Colombo Port City. But as public sector bodies and large corporations seek to balance digital transformation with stringent fiscal discipline, ASUS will need to prove that its commercial support infrastructure can deliver the round-the-clock enterprise reliability demanded by corporate fleets. Until then, winning over Sri Lanka’s C-suite may prove far more complex than dominating the retail shelf.
