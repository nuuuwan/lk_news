US war on Iran: ‘no war, no peace’ or ‘more war, less peace’

Pedestrians walk past a mural of Iran’s slain supreme leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, along a street in downtown Tehran on August 31. AFP

It is now as evident as the summer’s sun over the Hormuz Strait that the war between the United States and Iran will continue like the ‘forever war’—a term that has come back to haunt President Donald Trump. He once used it mockingly to slam his predecessors for the US wars in Iraq and Afghanistan.

When the Trump administration on August 24 imposed what it described as the harshest ever economic D-Day sanction, codenamed Operation Economic Outcast, some analysts saw it as the end of kinetic action and the US, instead, turning its focus to economically crippling Iran to force its surrender.

But renewed but measured US attacks on Iran this week, after a month’s pause, suggest the war has entered the ‘no war, no peace’ phase. The US strategy appears to be to continue the war as and when necessary, weaponising peace overtures from chosen mediators as a pretence.

Trump is untrustworthy. His words are soaked in narcissistic saliva. Canada trusted him and started trade talks, only to leave the negotiating table furious and disappointed. Trump is no respecter of international law. Just like an eccentric leader espoused by the Madman Theory in international relations, Trump keeps the world guessing about his next move.

He promotes himself as a man of peace, claiming he has solved at least eight world conflicts—a highly contested claim. Yet he keeps proving otherwise. He wants to annex Canada, Greenland, and Venezuela. He has war plans for Cuba. He is incapable of peace and justice. He agrees to Iran’s terms only to violate them before the ink dries. The Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding that the US signed with Iran, following painstaking mediation by Pakistan and Qatar, could have laid the foundation for permanent peace in West Asia. But the hawkish Trump administration scuttled it—not because it feared losing electoral advantage in the November mid‑terms, but because war—not peace—was its nature.

Trump’s incapacity for peace resembles the scorpion that entered into a pact with a frog to cross a lake. Midway, the dangerous passenger stung the frog. When the dying frog asked why, knowing both would perish, the scorpion replied, ‘Sorry, bro, that’s my nature’.

Incapable of peace, Trump, like a fish taking to water, took to the Iran war—one that previous presidents dared not touch even with a barge pole, no matter how hard Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu tried to trap them with deceptive talk.

Ignoring expert advice, the Trump administration plunged into Netanyahu’s war without wargaming exit strategies in the event of a setback or stalemate. As a result, he has now been pushed into playing the dangerous game of ‘no war, no peace’. He apparently believes he can continue the pattern of escalating and de‑escalating the war until the midterm elections in November while pretending to give peace a chance. This is meant to dupe the American working class, most of whom oppose the war and are fuming over rising fuel and essential prices.

Last week, Trump boasted of signing a historic US$65 billion deal with Venezuela. The biggest oil deal ever, he claimed, would bring down oil prices like never before. This is his election promise to American voters, especially Republicans, many of whom are largely unconcerned about the lack of ethics and morality in Trump’s foreign policy. But oil industry analysts say it will take anywhere from two to ten years before the Venezuelan deal produces a significant price drop.

With opinion polls favouring the Democrats to win the elections and control both the House and Senate, Trump’s gamble with oil prices is doomed to fail. If the November elections turn out to be, as surveys predict, a battering for the Republicans, Trump is likely to lash out at Iran like a wounded animal. The war is likely to escalate, with the risk of spreading region‑wide.

Iran seems prepared for it even as it calls for peace. In a quick response to US attacks on Larak Island on Monday and targets along Iran’s western coast overlooking the Strait of Hormuz—including a wedding reception—on Wednesday, Tehran launched missile and drone strikes on US bases in Jordan, Kuwait, the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain. These countries, along with the US, claimed the Iranian projectiles were intercepted and caused no harm. Similar claims during the early months of the war were proved to be strategic lies aimed at denying Iran a taste of victory.

On Wednesday, Trump ruled out the possibility of the renewed fighting lasting too long but said the US military is ready to strike the Islamic Republic “any time we want.” He added that he is not in a hurry to enter a peace deal, rejecting Iran’s proposals through mediators. This week, Iran’s President Masoud Pezeshkian, attending the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation summit in Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan, renewed Iran’s call to resurrect the Islamabad MoU.

But Trump, who has failed to achieve any of the war’s objectives, has upped the ante, believing—or miscalculating—that by combinedly increasing military and economic pressure he can force Iran to surrender. He claimed the US had de‑mined the Strait of Hormuz and that more ships are now moving in and out of the strait—a claim far from reality, as reports indicate two Saudi‑linked ships were hit by projectiles and only about five ships crossed the chokepoint on Wednesday.

As oil prices nudged the US$100 mark yesterday, in a severe blow to world economies—especially developing countries—Iran declared that the Strait of Hormuz could only be opened with its consent.

If the war escalates after the US midterm elections or before them, Iran seems confident it could inflict pain on the US military. It has so far strategically avoided targeting US warships and naval assets within its missile range. Moreover, Iran holds a drone advantage. Since the Iran War began, nations have learned that more than any conventional weapon, drones are the most effective in modern warfare. The tactic is simple: launch swarms of cheap but deadly drones, not primarily to hit targets but to exhaust costly anti‑drone and anti‑missile systems such as Patriots and THAAD. The US anti‑missile capability is now at an all‑time low, below the requirement for strategic readiness—a victory for Iran.

Amid these military setbacks, the US war party may be exploring new strategies, even as top US military chiefs—including the Army Secretary and his deputy—resigned following disagreements with War Secretary Pete Hegseth, who behaves more like an Islamophobic extremist than a pragmatist, realist or strategist.

The new US strategies may see Israel re‑entering the war or the US, together with Israel, engineering a false‑flag operation to trigger an Iran‑Saudi conflict—one likely to draw in Pakistan and Turkey under the recently signed Mecca Defence Agreement.

Bloodied by wars, the Middle East has been a powder keg for millennia and is likely to remain so until the end of time. The US war on Iran may appear to be a ‘no war, no peace’ conflict, but in reality it is more war than peace.
