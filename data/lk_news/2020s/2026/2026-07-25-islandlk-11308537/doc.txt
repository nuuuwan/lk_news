A Landmark Gathering of Minds: The Ceylon Journal Roundtable Revives the Spirit of Intellectual Dialogue

In an age when academic discourse is increasingly confined within disciplinary boundaries and universities rarely provide spaces for sustained interdisciplinary dialogue, The Ceylon Journal Roundtable marked a remarkable departure. Held recently at the serene Tulana Research Centre in Kelaniya, the inaugural Roundtable brought together more than fifty distinguished academics, researchers, professionals, clergy, writers, artists, architects, physicians, lawyers, journalists and independent scholars from across Sri Lanka for a full day of conversation under the theme History, Culture and Religion.

Conceived and principally organised by historian and Editor-in-Chief of The Ceylon Journal, Avishka Mario Senewiratne, the Roundtable represented months of careful planning and collaboration. Sponsored by The Ceylon Journal, the event sought not merely to present research papers, but to recreate a culture of civilised intellectual exchange that has become increasingly uncommon in Sri Lanka’s academic landscape.

Indeed, many participants remarked that it was difficult to recall a comparable interdisciplinary gathering organised independently outside the conventional university conference structure. In his virtual message played at the commencement of the event, eminent historian Professor C. R. de Silva (now domiciled in Virginia, USA) observed that the last comparable intellectual initiative had taken place during the late 1960s and early 1970s, inspired by the pioneering work of Professor Gananath Obeyesekere and a generation of scholars committed to rigorous public dialogue. Professor de Silva congratulated The Ceylon Journal for reviving this important tradition and expressed the hope that the Roundtable would become a permanent annual feature of Sri Lanka’s intellectual calendar.

The proceedings formally commenced with a welcome address by Avishka Mario Senewiratne, who reflected upon the philosophy behind the Roundtable. He noted that scholarship has never progressed through unanimity, but through evidence, conversation, respectful disagreement and the willingness to engage with ideas across disciplines. He emphasised that while journals preserve scholarship in print, meaningful conversation is what tests, refines and enriches knowledge.

Following the welcome, Dr. Praveen Weeratunga of The Ceylon Journal introduced the objectives of the Roundtable and outlined the format for the day’s discussions before inviting participants to embrace open yet respectful intellectual engagement.

The first session, chaired by Dr. Praveen Weeratunga, focused on History and Heritage. Dr. Buddhika Dassanayake delivered a compelling presentation examining the forgotten royal burial grounds of the Kandyan Kingdom and argued that the destruction of this sacred landscape amounted to a deliberate erasure of historical memory. He combined archaeological evidence, historical records and colonial documentation to reconstruct one of the island’s least appreciated heritage sites.

This was followed by Chryshane Mendis, who challenged conventional approaches to Sri Lankan archaeology by advocating greater scholarly attention to the Early Modern period. He argued that archaeology should not conclude with the ancient kingdoms but must continue into the colonial centuries, opening fresh avenues for research into Sri Lanka’s more recent past. An animated discussion followed, with participants debating the preservation of heritage, colonial memory and future archaeological priorities.

The second session on Art, Architecture and Shared Cultural Influences, chaired by architect Lakmith Fernando, highlighted the relationship between the built environment, culture and sustainability. Dr. Priyantha Udagedara explored the colonial transformation of Nuwara Eliya through paintings, travel accounts and visual culture, illustrating how British landscape aesthetics reshaped both physical space and cultural perceptions of the hill country. Together, the presentations generated considerable discussion on heritage conservation, identity and the relevance of traditional knowledge in the modern world. Architect Sunela Jayewardene delivered a thought-provoking address on Sri Lanka’s traditional architectural heritage, demonstrating how centuries-old indigenous design principles provide practical solutions to contemporary environmental challenges, climate resilience and sustainable living.

Following morning refreshments, the Roundtable resumed with its third session devoted to Religion and Society, chaired by Ralston Joseph. Professor G. P. V. Somaratna examined Dutch religious policy in maritime Sri Lanka, demonstrating how the Dutch East India Company balanced political pragmatism with commercial priorities in its relationships with Buddhism, Catholicism and Protestantism. Dr. Asoka Mendis de Zoysa complemented this perspective by exploring the religious and social transformations experienced by cinnamon-peeling communities under Portuguese, Dutch and British rule, revealing the complex interactions between colonial administration, caste, commerce and faith. The lively discussion that followed illustrated the enduring importance of religious history in understanding Sri Lanka’s multicultural past.

Participants were then introduced to the history and mission of the Tulana Research Centre by its Director, Fr. Dilan Perera SJ. Founded in 1974 by the internationally renowned theologian and Buddhist scholar late Fr. Aloysius Pieris SJ, Tulana has long served as one of Sri Lanka’s foremost centres for scholarship, interreligious encounter and dialogue. Described by many as a “mini university,” Tulana has welcomed generations of local and international scholars, while its remarkable libraries, research facilities and atmosphere of contemplation have made it one of the country’s most respected intellectual institutions. Participants expressed particular appreciation that such a gathering was hosted in a venue whose very philosophy embodies dialogue across cultures, religions and academic disciplines.

Following lunch, the fourth session turned to Literature and Language, under the chairmanship of Dr. Kanchanakesi Warnapala. Professor Sandagomi Coperahewa then explored Sri Lanka’s rich multilingual heritage, arguing that the island’s literary traditions historically fostered dialogue, coexistence and mutual understanding among Sinhala, Tamil, Pali and Sanskrit traditions. Participants reflected on the importance of multilingualism as an essential component of national reconciliation and intellectual openness. Dr. Dhanuka Bandara offered an original interpretation of Martin Wickramasinghe by examining the tensions between the celebrated author’s Buddhist metaphysics and his later conservative cultural positions.

The concluding academic session, chaired by Dr. Shane Halpe, shifted attention to contemporary society through discussions on Education, Compassion and Ethnic Harmony. Dr. Gauthami Kamalika Jayathilaka argued that compassion should become a central value within Sri Lanka’s state universities, presenting practical examples of cultivating empathy, social responsibility and harmony among students. Ralston Joseph followed with a stimulating presentation exploring ethnic harmony through an economic rather than purely political lens, suggesting that shared prosperity and market participation may provide common ground capable of reducing social divisions. The discussion that followed demonstrated how the Roundtable successfully connected historical inquiry with pressing contemporary challenges.

Unlike conventional academic conferences, every session reserved substantial time for open discussion, allowing participants from diverse disciplines to question speakers, compare perspectives and exchange ideas freely. Historians conversed with physicians, architects debated with linguists, artists engaged with economists, while clergy, lawyers and journalists contributed equally to the discussions. It was precisely this interdisciplinary exchange that distinguished the Roundtable and created an atmosphere of genuine intellectual curiosity rarely witnessed in contemporary academic gatherings.

The event attracted an impressive gathering of scholars and professionals representing universities, research institutions, professional organisations and independent scholarship throughout the country. Nearly every invited participant. As proceedings concluded, participants remained long after the formal programme had ended, continuing conversations over evening tea and establishing new collaborations that organisers hope will lead to future research, publications and public discussions.

The inaugural The Ceylon Journal Roundtable demonstrated that serious scholarship continues to flourish when provided with the right environment. More significantly, it reaffirmed that independent scholarly initiatives can successfully bring together some of Sri Lanka’s finest intellectuals outside traditional institutional structures.

Many participants described the Roundtable as a landmark achievement—one that few universities themselves have managed to accomplish in recent years. By reviving a culture of thoughtful interdisciplinary conversation, The Ceylon Journal has not merely organised another conference; it has initiated what many hope will become an enduring national tradition of intellectual dialogue.

The overwhelmingly positive response from participants reflected the success of the inaugural Roundtable. Independent scholar Chamikara Pilapitiya, well known for his work on Sri Lankan military history and heritage, described the gathering as “the best seminar I have attended in my living years,” praising The Ceylon Journal for creating a unique platform for serious intellectual engagement. Professor Enoka Corea, Chair Professor of Micro-Biology at the University of Colombo, observed that the Roundtable brought together “kindred souls” united by a shared love for Sri Lanka’s rich cultural inheritance. She expressed the hope that the discussions would become a regular feature, helping to fill important gaps in the country’s complex historical narrative. Veteran academic and former Executive Director of the United States – Sri Lanka Fulbright Commission Tissa Jayatilaka described the gathering as “a good gathering of minds,” noting that it had provided an opportunity to reconnect with long-standing colleagues while also meeting a younger generation of scholars. While praising the initiative, he observed that slightly stricter adherence to the allocated speaking times would have allowed even greater scope for audience discussion. Architect Aousten Aloysius of Jaffna remarked that the interdisciplinary discussions had broadened and deepened his own research interests and encouraged him to continue developing as a researcher. Sam Perera, co-founder of Perera-Hussein Publishing House, likewise commended the event as “an intellectually stimulating and thought-provoking day,” adding that he particularly appreciated “the sessions, the format and the overall smoothness of the operation.”

As proceedings concluded, participants lingered long after the formal programme had ended, continuing conversations over evening tea while exchanging ideas and establishing new collaborations. The inaugural The Ceylon Journal Roundtable demonstrated that serious scholarship continues to flourish when provided with the right environment. More significantly, it reaffirmed that an independent scholarly initiative could successfully assemble some of Sri Lanka’s finest academics, researchers, professionals and public intellectuals outside the traditional university framework.
