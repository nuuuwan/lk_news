Sri Lankan households earn more but afford less

Colombo, Nov. 26 (Daily Mirror) - Sri Lankan households at the midpoint of the income distribution lost nearly 40 percent of their purchasing power between 2019 and the first quarter of 2025, preliminary official figures show, exposing a deep deterioration in living standards despite higher rupee incomes.

The Department of Census and Statistics’ revised Household Income and Expenditure Survey bulletin showed monthly median household income rose to Rs.75,882 from Rs.53,333 in 2019. However, after adjusting for inflation, it fell 39.2 percent, indicating that income growth fell well short of compensating for higher living costs.

The findings place household purchasing power and consumption capacity at the centre of the economic policy challenge, with families receiving more in nominal rupees while having less capacity to purchase goods and services.

“The decline was greater for median household income than for mean household income,” the department said.

Median income divides households into two equal groups, with half receiving less and half receiving more. It provides a useful counterpoint to average income, which is influenced by households with very high earnings.

Average monthly household income increased to Rs.120,266 in the first quarter of 2025 from Rs.76,414 in 2019. Yet its inflation-adjusted value declined 32.8 percent over the same period.

Measured at constant 2016 prices, median monthly household income fell to Rs.28,444 from Rs.46,829 in 2019, while average income dropped to Rs.45,081 from Rs.67,096. Both were also below their 2016 levels.

The deterioration extended to household expenditure, providing evidence of weaker real consumption and raising questions about the strength of purchasing capacity available to support businesses dependent on domestic demand.

Average monthly household expenditure rose to Rs.116,191 from Rs.63,130 in 2019. However, at constant prices, it declined to Rs.43,554 from Rs.55,432, a fall of about 21.4 percent, calculated from the bulletin’s figures.

Median monthly expenditure similarly increased in nominal terms to Rs.94,057 from Rs.47,544. Adjusted for inflation, it fell approximately 15.5 percent to Rs.35,257 from Rs.41,746.

The distinction matters for businesses assessing consumer demand: higher expenditure in rupee terms can coexist with a decline in the goods and services households can afford.

The survey also showed an estimated reversal in the earlier decline in poverty.

The national poverty headcount ratio rose to 16 percent in the first quarter of 2025 from 14.3 percent in 2019, returning to the level recorded in 2016. Approximately 3.42 million people were estimated to be living below the official poverty line.

The monthly poverty threshold stood at Rs.16,417 per person, compared with Rs.6,966 in 2019, reflecting the higher cost of meeting a fixed minimum standard of food and essential non-food needs.

The threshold is based on expenditure sufficient to meet a daily dietary energy requirement of 2,030 kilocalories, together with an allowance for necessities such as housing, clothing, transport and healthcare. It measures absolute poverty against a defined basic-needs standard rather than the full range of contemporary household spending requirements.

Income disparities were substantial. The richest fifth of households received average monthly incomes of Rs.335,679, nearly 15 times the Rs.22,612 recorded for the poorest fifth.

The middle 60 percent of households recorded average monthly income of Rs.81,067, substantially below the national average of Rs.120,266.

Conditions also varied across residential sectors. Average monthly household income was highest in urban areas at Rs.178,455, followed by Rs.110,613 in rural areas and Rs.72,558 in estates.

Estate households recorded the highest estimated poverty rate at 23 percent, compared with 17.3 percent in rural areas and 7.8 percent in urban areas. Rural areas nevertheless accounted for the largest number of people below the poverty line, at approximately 2.93 million, or about 86 percent of the national total.

The department cautioned that urban and estate poverty estimates had greater sampling variability than the national and rural figures.

The bulletin provides the first-quarter findings of the 2025 survey, the first conducted since 2019. The survey originally scheduled for 2022 was postponed because of exceptional economic conditions and the need to conduct it after the 2024 population and housing census.

The full-year survey covered a nationally representative sample of approximately 25,000 households, but the bulletin’s estimates draw only on data collected between January and March 2025. They remain provisional pending cleaning and validation of the complete dataset. (SAA)
