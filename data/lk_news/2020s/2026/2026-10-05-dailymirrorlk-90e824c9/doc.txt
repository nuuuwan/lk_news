Laws that Shackle our Economy

The economic crisis in our country and the counter-revolution with tremendous repression after the great uprising in 2022, were seen as great opportunities by the International Financial Institutions backed by the West (Photo courtesy Asia Times).

Sri Lanka cannot recover from the long economic crisis, much less support its citizens’ economic aspirations, without repealing laws that shackle its economy

Economic policies in Sri Lanka are going to be the subject of much discussion over the next six months. The Government will present the budget next month. The four-year IMF programme will come to an end in March 2027. In this context, what is the role of laws, and how much space is there within the legal framework to shift the economic trajectory of the country?

During the last few years, particularly in 2023 and 2024, a series of laws were passed by the Wickremesinghe-Rajapaksa government. Among them were also a set of laws advocated by the IMF and the World Bank that have drastically changed the scope and character of macroeconomic policy making, and more broadly the functions and workings of the Ministry of Finance and the Central Bank. In this column, I address the challenges for our economy shackled by such regressive laws.

While we are taught to believe that laws empower us and the rule of law will lead to progress, in reality modern law, including international law, have for the most part been on the side of powerful repressive actors. Antony Anghie, an internationally known Sri Lankan legal theorist, in his seminal book titled ‘Imperialism, Sovereignty and the Making of International Law’, has exposed the legacy of colonial brutality and treaties in the formation of the international legal regime. He draws on the work of the political theorist Partha Chatterjee, particularly his conception of the “rule of colonial difference”. Chatterjee in his important work, ‘The Nation and Its Fragments’, clearly shows how colonial rule had two sets of laws, one for the colonisers and one for the colonised, and that is the case with international law as well.

While the West was forced to accept decolonisation, it continued to reject Third World sovereignty. The former colonisers who had justified colonial rule lasting centuries as necessary for “civilising” the rest of the world, sought every avenue to find fault with the newly formed sovereign states. Worse, they used their military power and intelligence agencies to assassinate and topple many progressive leaders and governments in Asia, Africa and Latin America, in order to ensure neo-colonial rule with comprador or puppet regimes aligned to the West.

Neo-colonialism is not just about political control, but even more focused on wealth extraction, ensuring the policies of postcolonial states are in line with the economic interests of the West. The promise of freedom and development with decolonisation that the people of the Third World yearned for, was shutdown with military power, political manoeuvres, dominant economic structures and legal regimes. Indeed, regressive laws, particularly related to economic matters, were far more effective in both masking neo-colonial interests and internalising global inequalities, particularly when so-called sovereign Third World states absorbed them into their law books.

I provide this historical context to illustrate that, what happened under the comprador Wickremesinghe-Rajapaksa regime of 2022 to 2024 has been happening in many parts of the world since decolonisation in the mid-twentieth century. The economic crisis in our country and the counter-revolution with tremendous repression after the great uprising in 2022, were seen as great opportunities by the International Financial Institutions backed by the West. The IMF and the World Bank shamelessly worked hand and glove with the delegitimised Wickremesinghe-Rajapaksa regime to pass tens of laws that constrain the country and its economy.

My focus here is on four laws that have tremendous impact on macroeconomic policymaking: Central Bank of Sri Lanka Act, No. 16 of 2023; Public Debt Management Act (PDMA), No. 33 of 2024; Public Financial Management Act (PFMA), No. 44 of 2024; and Economic Transformation Act (ETA), No. 45 of 2024. Unless these four laws – rushed through by the previous regime while it knew it was about to be thrown out – are repealed and a new set of laws that ensure sovereign economic policymaking are enshrined, there is little hope for economic rejuvenation.

Economic policymaking is in a straightjacket with the above mentioned four laws. The Central Bank can no longer be asked to provide domestic financing to grow the economy. So-called Central Bank independence is there now to guard the interests of global finance capital with inflation targeting by maintaining high interest rates to the detriment of economic growth in the country. Combined with the PDMA and the creation of the Debt Management Office, the Central Bank has washed its hands off financing necessary for debt management.

Even worse, the PFMA states that Government budget expenditure cannot exceed 13 percent of GDP. In what country and which moron would legislate the amount of expenditure in the national budget? This also restricts public expenditure to one of the lowest levels of any country in the world. Furthermore, the PFMA also puts limit on Treasury guarantees including for state enterprises at a total of 7.5 percent of GDP. The ETA is the peak of stupidity with all kinds of targets on economic growth, budgeting and employment; it sets a one percent of GDP limit on the current account deficit, a 2.3 percent of GDP target of primary budget surplus, and legislates into law that Female Labour Force Participation should be 40 percent by 2030.

These targets set in law are meant to most of all incapacitate policymaking. While current account deficits that are influenced by global trade, or, for that matter, women’s formal participation in the economy are influenced by a range of socio-economic factors, and cannot be determined by law, the goal of these laws are to shut down even alternative policy thinking. Indeed, the Central Bank’s sole monetary policy and inflation targeting mandate along with the Finance Ministry’s budgetary expenditure limits, Treasury guarantee limits and primary budget surplus requirements have constrained the thinking of the policymakers and  staff in both institutions.

The agenda of the IMF and World Bank in pushing for these neo-colonial laws is also the creation of an austerity mind-set with a singular focus on repaying the debt of external financiers. The consequences are that for example a public distribution system supported by Treasury guarantees, a trade policy with some flexibility in balance of payments with a current account deficit, an industrial policy to nurture new sectors supported by public financing, a public development bank with a concessionary financing window from the Central Bank etc., are all considered a non-starter by the bureaucracy, particularly if they believe this regressive legal regime is going to persist.

Sri Lanka cannot recover from the long economic crisis, much less support its citizens’ economic aspirations, without repealing laws that shackle its economy. There is also the urgency to repeal such laws as the unravelling global order and the attendant shocks require alternative thinking and more policy mechanisms and tools to manoeuvre through the difficult times ahead.

Neoliberalism itself is in crisis and neo-colonialism is shaken by the internal fractures and external pressures that are breaking the long consensus within the West. The Trump regime’s policies and the recent wars are undermining Western hegemony. In this global environment, the NPP government, in the months ahead, should muster the political will to repeal these regressive laws for the country’s economic survival. Changing the legal regime underlying economic policymaking is also essential for a progressive shift in the economic trajectory of the country.
