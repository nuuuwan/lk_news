Iran war: Intensified diplomacy raises hope for peace

Pakistan’s military chief Asim Munir holds talks with Iran’s President Masoud Pezeshkian in Tehran on Monday

Speculation is rife about a deal between the United States and Iran after a C‑17 US military aircraft carrying CIA Director John Ratcliffe landed in Moscow on Tuesday. There are numerous issues to be thrashed out between the two big powers that were superpower rivals during the five‑decade Cold War.

Some believe the talks concern the Ukraine war, which President Donald Trump had boasted he would end within his first week in office but failed to do. The war is not going well for Russia, with European powers enhancing their military and political support for Ukraine even as the US scales down its backing. The visit came amid an escalation in the Russia‑Ukraine conflict, with Ukraine demonstrating its newly gained military strength by striking strategic targets deep inside Russia. Meanwhile, Russia accused the Baltic states—Latvia, Estonia, and Lithuania—of allowing Ukraine to use their airspace to hit Russian targets, warning them and other NATO members, particularly Britain, that they would face consequences.

Analysts also believe Iran was high on the agenda during Ratcliffe’s four‑hour unscheduled visit for talks with his counterpart. On Wednesday, in a one‑minute exclusive interview with Al Jazeera, Trump declined to comment on the CIA director’s trip, citing the sensitivities involved. However, POLITICO reported that in a separate US radio interview, Trump denied speculation that Ratcliffe had travelled to Moscow to dissuade Putin from testing NATO’s resolve or attacking Britain, which this week vowed to double down on its support for Ukraine. Trump described the visit as “semi-routine” but acknowledged it was aimed at bringing the war to a close. “I hate to disappoint people. We would like to see the war with Ukraine end,” he said.

Some Western outlets, quoting sources familiar with the talks, characterised the trip—the first such visit since Trump assumed office in his second term—as a warning to Russia not to extend military and economic support to Iran, which has been hit this week with what Washington calls “Economic D‑Day” sanctions.

The CIA chief’s Russia visit came a day after US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent threatened to expand sanctions on countries refusing to cut economic ties with Iran—a warning analysts said was aimed particularly at Russia and China. Beyond defence and political cooperation, Russia and Iran maintain strong economic links through the Caspian Sea—a strictly no‑go zone for NATO.

The US “D‑Day” for Iran was on Monday. But there was no Iranian surrender. Instead, Tehran dismissed the US economic warfare, saying it had endured worse and was well accustomed to surviving under sanctions.

As the war launched by the US and Israel to destroy Iran’s nuclear programme and bring about regime change enters its seventh month today, Ratcliffe’s visit to Russia may not have been aimed at warning Moscow, as US media speculated. Russia is far from beholden to US dictates. If the trip was meant to warn Russia on its own soil, it was an ultimate insult.

A spymaster par excellence, President Vladimir Putin is not unaware of US and NATO schemes to destabilise his country. Given Russia’s strong defence ties with Iran, it is unlikely Moscow will heed any US warning—whether about Ukraine or Iran.

Nor has China shown any willingness to abide by US sanctions and abandon Iran, which accounts for 12–14 per cent of Beijing’s seaborne crude oil imports. In fact, China buys 80–90 per cent of Iran’s oil. In a statement, China’s foreign ministry denounced the US sanction threat, calling such measures illegal, and warned it would take “all necessary measures” to protect national interests.

Interestingly, in the initial list of sanctions on 60 individuals, entities, and vessels for alleged involvement in trade with Iran, no Chinese financial institutions appeared, despite their role in financing Iran’s oil trade. The omission was deliberate and reflected US caution. When asked, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent replied, “Why would I want to blow up the global financial system?”

Washington’s lack of political courage to sanction China for dealing with Iran has rendered the so‑called D‑Day sanction little more than a damp squib. The Trump administration’s decision to impose sanctions on Iran was akin to its decision to wage war on Iran. Both strategies lack foresight. If the US sanctions China for its dealings with Iran, Beijing could retaliate through financial markets or by restricting exports of critical minerals, including rare earths.

On Tuesday, Iran dismissed the sanctions as “gross lawlessness” and expressed confidence that many countries would refuse to join the pressure campaign.

The story about Ratcliffe reading the riot act in Russia may be a red herring. The real purpose of the visit may have been backdoor diplomacy aimed at persuading Moscow to use its influence to push Iran toward a peace deal.

The visit coincides with heightened diplomatic activity to revive the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding, signed in June. The deal collapsed within weeks, with the US imposing a blockade on vessels departing from or bound for Iranian ports, while Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz to ships unless they moved with its permission.

On Monday, the very day the Trump administration’s D‑Day threats limped into force, Pakistan’s military chief Asim Munir was in Tehran, carrying a message from Trump and holding top‑level meetings with senior Iranian security officials, including General Mohsen Rezaei, the representative of Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei and the newly appointed secretary of the Supreme National Security Council.

His visit came as differences between Iran’s political leadership and the military, including the Revolutionary Guard, became more evident, with President Masoud Pezeshkian pushing for talks while the latter insisted on prosecuting the war to consolidate Iran’s advantages. Pakistan’s military said “significant progress” had been made in Field Marshal Munir’s efforts to resume peace talks.

Iran’s political leadership expressed willingness to re‑enter the talks but voiced concern over the new round of sanctions. Pakistan assured Tehran that such concerns could be addressed during negotiations.

On Tuesday, Oman’s Foreign Minister Badr Albusaidi was in Tehran to finalise a bilateral deal on the Strait of Hormuz. The deal includes the setting up of a “joint temporary navigational corridor”, a plan to clear mines, and possibly a security fee. Yesterday, Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman bin Jassim Al Thani was expected in Tehran for talks on de‑escalating tensions and reviving dialogue between Iran and the US.

Meanwhile, US diplomats evacuated from the region are returning to their embassies, a sign that Washington will not resume the war, which most Americans oppose. Amid these positive signs, Trump’s unpredictability remains a concern. He raises hopes for peace, only to subvert them himself. Iran, meanwhile, would do well to heed President Pezeshkian’s advice to resume talks now that it has gained a position of strength.
