Quality of life:  Is it the state’s responsibility?

The state cannot take responsibility for the happiness of its citizens

The title of the NPP manifesto, “a Thriving Nation, a Beautiful Life,” suggests that those running the present government believe that the state has a responsibility to ensure a higher quality of life for the citizens. This issue was raised in a recent public discussion, in implicit criticism of my advocacy of reducing taxes and promoting exports as against actions to ensure a “rich life.”

The state cannot take responsibility for the happiness of its citizens. Its responsibility is to preserve the basic conditions needed for citizens to achieve their own happiness. Reduce the barriers, but do not seek to ensure the outcome.

The events leading up to the economic crisis on 2021-22 are illustrative. Thousands came to the streets to protest the Gotabaya government’s failure to ensure the supply of necessities such as cooking fuel, electricity, fuel for transport, and fertiliser and related items for farmers. They were not fighting for happiness (though a minority did raise related slogans), they were protesting the government’s failure to continue the supply of the basics needed to achieve their own versions of happiness, or at least to reduce their unhappiness.  What is a higher quality of life?

Is one man’s or woman’s happiness the same as that of another? Anyone from a normal family who has organised a family trip knows the answer. If it is so difficult to ensure that everyone’s happiness is optimised in a small group, how can the politico-bureaucratic machine known as the state do it for a much larger population?

Factors affecting happiness have a ratchet-like quality. The unhappiness caused by deprivation of a service is much larger than the happiness generated when first getting it. According to the 2024 census 98 percent of households use grid electricity as their source of lighting. This was a 13 percent increase over the 87 percent reported in 2012. Those who got connected after 2012 would have experienced some happiness when they did. However, the unhappiness caused by 13-hour power cuts in 2022 would have been an order of magnitude greater.

Happiness is subjective; to each his own. It is foolish to average these desires. De gustibus non disputandum est is an ancient aphorism, meaning “in matters of taste, there can be no disputes.” Personal likes and choices are different for everyone. You cannot prove a personal preference is right or wrong, so arguing about it is futile.

So, the ceiling is happiness but defining it and reaching for it is an individual’s responsibility, with a little help from their friends. The state can ensure that we have a firm footing as we reach for the stars (or the ceiling). When there is no electricity, when there is no water in the tap, when transport is crippled and roads are blocked, when there is no fuel to cook with, one is unable to reach for the ceiling. One must spend all one’s  energy trying to stop falling through the floor.  That which we cannot provide for ourselves

The floor that fell apart in 2021-22 was made of those elements mentioned above, especially for urban dwellers who powered the Aragalaya. According to the 2024 census 82.1 percent of urbanites use LPG gas for cooking (up 53 percent from 2012). While firewood is still common in rural and estate areas, gas use has been growing very fast from low bases in 2012-24: 202 percent increase to 35.1 percent of rural households and a 313 percent increase to 15.3 percent of estate households. No wonder the urban areas erupted against President Gotabaya’s mismanagement of the economy.

Electricity that is the primary source of illumination in almost all homes, safe water supplies, transport, and telecommunication are among the infrastructure services that make up the floor that people rely on. That is why service disruptions and price increases matter so much.

There are other things we cannot provide for ourselves. Credentials that tell others that we have certain competencies are one example. It is possible to become an autodidact like Martin Wickramasinghe. But knowing is not enough for most people. There must be some respected body certifying that one has the required knowledge or skills. In modern societies it is necessary for the state to provide us with the means to establish that we are who we claim to be, to authenticate our identities. If any of these hard or soft infrastructures are taken away, our lives will be poorer; our striving for happiness will be made harder.  That which we must get for ourselves

Suppose that one person is made happy by travel and another by the quiet life. If the basics are in place, the wanderer can achieve happiness by earning enough money to travel. The person wanting the quiet life will have less need for money and can trade that off for greater non-working time. One may be a film buff and will need to have the time and money for enjoying films. Another would make different choices regarding time and money.

It is simply not practical for a big, lumbering state to know, let alone supply, what makes different people happy. This is where decentralised economic forms such as markets are superior. If efforts are made to assign individuals for the jobs they are suited for (or was considered necessary for the society as a whole), as was attempted under central planning, the result would be generalised unhappiness. Issuance of food as determined by the government based on ration cards through cooperative shops as was the case before 1978 would not be acceptable today.

An interview about the new book “The time tax,” began thus: one of the biggest problems in US politics is that the very people who Democrats, liberals and socialists think should be the most supportive of big government programmes intended to help them are often the least excited — and  often vote for the party that wants to cut government spending.

When I was informally conversing with President Gotabaya’s advisors back in 2022 on how to face the onslaught of the coming crisis, we agreed that the poorest would bear the brunt and the government had to provide a safety net. The debate was whether it would be modelled on the pre-1978 rationing scheme where the authorities would decide what food items would be given out, or whether it should be cash transfers direct to the beneficiaries’ bank accounts. I was pleased we went with the latter option which is the Aswesuma programme currently in effect.

Aswesuma recognises that people were affected by a crisis they did not cause and provides relief. But how that money is to be spent is left to the recipients. The floor is provided, but reaching the ceiling is up to individuals
