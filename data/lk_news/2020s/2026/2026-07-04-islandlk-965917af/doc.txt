Pioneering the Introduction of Japanese-Style Management in Sri Lanka

LESSONS FROM MY CAREER: SYNTHESISING MANAGEMENT THEORY WITH PRACTICE – PART 39

Some experiences remain etched in one’s memory for a lifetime. Mine occurred in Japan in 1980 when, as a young Industrial Engineer following a course in Industrial and Systems Engineering, I witnessed a Quality Circle in action for the very first time. Little did I realise then that this single factory visit would profoundly influence my professional life and eventually lead to the introduction of Japanese-style Quality Circles to Sri Lanka.

One of the factories we visited belonged to NEC. There, we were invited to observe a Quality Circle presentation conducted entirely by shop-floor workers. This was long before the days of PowerPoint presentations. Instead, the team used colourful transparencies on an overhead projector. Yet, despite the absence of modern technology, it remains one of the most captivating presentations I have ever witnessed.

I was astonished by the enthusiasm, confidence and commitment displayed by these ordinary factory workers. Drawing entirely on their own practical experience and intimate knowledge of their work, they had followed a disciplined problem-solving process. They identified the root causes of a production issue, generated alternative solutions, evaluated each option carefully, selected the most appropriate remedy and presented it to management. Once approval was obtained, the circle itself implemented the solution and established new standards to ensure that the improvement would be sustained.

What impressed me most was not merely the technical excellence of their work but the genuine enjoyment they derived from the entire process. The presentation itself was a masterpiece of storytelling.

To illustrate the progress of their project, they projected the image of a ship sailing calmly across the sea. A cleverly designed cardboard overlay enabled the ship to appear to glide smoothly across the screen as the project advanced. Then came the inevitable setback. Differences of opinion emerged within the circle. Suddenly the calm sea transformed into a violent storm. The sky darkened, lightning flashed across the screen, and thunder echoed through the room. By making ingenious cuts in the cardboard and using aluminium foil, they recreated the sights and sounds of a raging tempest. Although entirely simple by today’s standards, the effect was remarkably dramatic.

Even after forty-six years, I can still recall almost every detail of that presentation. It was impossible not to admire the pride these workers took in solving problems that affected their own workplace. They were not simply carrying out instructions from management; they were thinking, analysing, creating and improving. They were fully engaged.

As I watched them, I remember saying to myself, “This is something we must introduce in Sri Lanka.”

Another memorable Quality Circle that we observed was in a completely different setting. Instead of a manufacturing environment, this one operated in a data-entry department. Six young women who entered data into computers faced a persistent problem. They frequently struggled to decipher the handwriting on the documents they received, resulting in costly errors.

Rather than accepting the situation as inevitable, they analysed the problem systematically. They measured the frequency of errors, identified which departments generated the problematic forms and categorised the types of handwriting mistakes that caused the greatest difficulties. Their investigations eventually led them to develop a standard manual prescribing how numerals and letters should be written on official forms. Management accepted the proposal and implemented it throughout the organisation. The result was a dramatic reduction in data-entry errors.

The lesson was unmistakable. Quality Circles were not confined to factories. They could improve any type of work where people were encouraged to think, analyse and solve problems together.

Armed with these inspiring experiences, I returned to Sri Lanka with tremendous enthusiasm and an overwhelming desire to introduce Quality Circles at the Sri Lanka Tyre Corporation, where I was then serving as the Industrial Engineer.

On my journey home, my Chairman requested that I stop over in the Philippines to visit the BF Goodrich tyre factory. At that time, the Sri Lanka Tyre Corporation had a technical collaboration agreement with BF Goodrich of the United States, making the visit particularly worthwhile.

The Philippine factory employed around 1,000 workers and produced approximately 2,000 tyres per day. By contrast, our own factory employed nearly 2,000 workers but produced only about 1,000 tyres. The figures spoke volumes about our productivity—or rather, our lack of it.

The factory also operated an employee suggestion scheme, but there was a significant difference. It was restricted to engineers and managerial staff, and each participant was expected to generate perhaps one worthwhile suggestion each year. Many of these suggestions produced considerable financial savings.

Quality Circles in Japan were designed specifically for shop-floor and frontline employees. The Japanese believed that the people performing the work every day possessed invaluable practical knowledge that managers could never fully acquire. Instead of relying solely on supervisors and engineers, they actively encouraged workers themselves to identify problems and devise improvements.

It was during this period that I began to appreciate the profound philosophical difference between Western and Japanese management.

The Japanese themselves often expressed it rather elegantly. They observed that traditional American management made use primarily of the “hands” of employees. Japanese management, on the other hand, sought to harness not only the hands but also the minds of every employee.

That simple philosophy explained much of Japan’s remarkable industrial success.

Filled with enthusiasm, I began introducing the concept on my return to Sri Lanka. Unfortunately, enthusiasm alone was not enough.

Many Heads of Divisions saw little reason to change established practices. From their perspective, everything appeared to be functioning satisfactorily. Why introduce a completely new management concept that encouraged workers to participate in decision-making?

Several senior colleagues quietly advised me to proceed with caution. They explained that our Chairman’s management style was generally regarded as autocratic and that worker participation initiatives might not be welcomed. Although they appreciated my enthusiasm, they feared I was moving too quickly.

Rather than abandoning the idea, I decided to persevere more discreetly. Experience has taught me that genuine organisational change often requires patience, persistence and careful timing. New ideas seldom encounter universal acceptance at the outset. Those proposing change must be prepared for disappointment before success eventually arrives.

Meanwhile, references to our plans for Quality Circles found their way into the minutes of our Steering Committee meetings. Since these minutes were regularly forwarded to BF Goodrich in the United States, they soon attracted attention there.

One day I received an unexpected parcel from the BF Goodrich head office.

Inside were numerous articles documenting both successful and unsuccessful Quality Circle implementations across Europe. The collection proved immensely valuable. It demonstrated that Quality Circles were by no means guaranteed to succeed. Many organisations had failed because they treated the concept as another management programme rather than as a long-term cultural transformation requiring patience, leadership and trust.

Looking back, I now realise how fortunate I was to receive them. They taught me as much from the failures as from the successes.

Interestingly, 1980 was also the year when worldwide enthusiasm for Quality Circles reached its peak. Across Europe, North America and Asia, organisations were experimenting with this revolutionary Japanese approach to employee participation. Many embraced it enthusiastically, while others misunderstood its underlying philosophy and abandoned it prematurely.

About a year after my return from Japan, I accepted a more senior appointment at another state-owned enterprise. Although considerably smaller than the Tyre Corporation, the organisation gave me much greater authority and freedom to introduce new ideas.

I immediately recognised an opportunity to put into practice everything I had learnt in Japan.

This time, however, I was determined not merely to copy the Japanese model. During my training I had attended an enlightening workshop on transferring foreign management concepts across cultures. The central message was both simple and profound: every management system contains culture-specific elements as well as universal principles. Success depends upon preserving the universal principles while carefully adapting the culture-specific aspects to local conditions.

Rather than transplanting the Japanese model wholesale, I redesigned it to suit Sri Lankan organisational culture.

Instead of focusing exclusively on improvements in quality and productivity, I placed equal emphasis on enriching the quality of employees’ working lives. My belief was that if people genuinely enjoyed participating, felt respected and found meaning in their work, improvements in quality and productivity would naturally follow.

I also modified the structure of the circles to better suit our own organisational environment and deliberately introduced an element of enjoyment into the meetings and presentations.

The Quality Circles became highly successful. Employees participated enthusiastically, managers began to recognise their value and improvements started appearing throughout the organisation.

For the first time, I became convinced that Quality Circles could indeed flourish in Sri Lanka—provided they were thoughtfully adapted rather than blindly copied.

That conviction would shape the next chapter of my professional life.
