2027 Budget: Red herring to neutralise public and relink Sri Lanka with new financial colonialism

Trade Unions and People’s Power, a coalition of 38 trade unions and civil society organisations, in a statement on Tuesday raised concerns that the 2027 Budget shifts the burden onto the public, with indirect taxes set to rise by Rs. 170 billion against just Rs. 30 billion in direct taxes, while subsidies and transfers are cut by Rs. 100 billion and corporate tax holidays continue.

The group said 85% of the expected increase in tax revenue would fall on the general public. It also accused the Government of deliberately underspending Budget allocations to create surpluses that would help Sri Lanka return to International Sovereign Bond (ISB) markets under the International Monetary Fund (IMF) program.

The statement is as follows: “Pro-Government entities and the neoliberal apologists broadly hail the 2027 Budget as a step in the right direction, claiming that it balances the cost-of-living concerns of the public with business investment aspirations. While Sri Lanka’s corporate sector benefits by maintaining enormous tax breaks, this mainstream view exaggerates the crumbs thrown to the general public through indirect tax relief on a few essentials. Reducing taxes on sprats, dry fish and exercise books—alongside nominal salary increases for Government servants—will do nothing to halt the worsening living costs or reverse the steep cuts in essential spending mandated by the 2027 proposals.

The neoliberal framing of the Budget hides its true menacing face: for instance, aggregate hyper-inflationary indirect taxes are set to escalate by Rs. 170 billion, while the increase in direct tax income will be negligible at Rs. 30 billion, worsening the ratio between the two components to around 80% from 78% this year. Subsidies and transfers are slashed brutally by a staggering Rs. 100 billion (dropping from Rs. 1.57 trillion to Rs. 1.47 trillion)—showing total disregard for mass poverty at 16% according to the Ministry of Health and malnutrition among school children as high as 25% (UNICEF Sri Lanka). Corporate income tax holidays, on the other hand, which cost a mammoth Rs. 190 billion in 2024/25 according to the Ministry of Finance Tax Expenditure Report, continue unabated. Meanwhile, 85% of the burden of the expected tax revenue increase falls squarely on the general public.

The burden of higher indirect taxes and soaring living costs also restricts productive investments. Higher living costs inevitably fuel wage demands, inflating production costs without raising real wages. This erodes the competitive power of domestic producers in both domestic and global markets, accelerating the flight of manufacturing investments, while driving speculative and foreign-exchange-draining capital into property and real estate and illicit capital flows to hedge against inflation.

Conversely, shifting towards a higher direct-to-indirect tax ratio by revoking corporate income tax breaks and enforcing strict tax compliance would ease the cost of living, curb inflation, and lift families out of poverty and child malnutrition, while improving fiscal stability (a recent IMF study shows government revenue can rise by as much as 1.9% of Gross Domestic Product merely through enforcing tax compliance and upgrading the technological infrastructure of tax collecting institutions).

At the same time, it would drive down interest rates and hence, Government debt-servicing costs, while stimulating real investments, creating a virtuous cycle that further expands direct tax revenue through rising aggregate incomes. Yet, the current tax policy remains entirely geared towards speculative and commercial capital that prefers shifting assets and surpluses abroad or into real estate rather than expanding industry. Consequently, the tax structure locks in economic backwardness. This is underscored by Sri Lanka’s Economic Complexity Index rank remaining stagnant at 83rd globally, with its value trapped in negative territory (-0.045) since the inception of the index in the mid-1990s. The 2027 Budget will ensure that this structural stagnation in the economy persists.

Examining the striking gap between the 2026 Budget’s expenditure proposals and actual Government spending so far this year reveals a clear playbook: essential public investments (excluding highways and expressways) are choked through relentless and deliberate underspending. This has become the trademark strategy of the current administration: neutralise public outrage through exaggerated spending figures during Budget speeches, and fulfil the targets of global speculative capital—represented by the International Monetary Fund (IMF)—by brutally underspending budgetary allocations to manufacture artificial budget surpluses.

This disingenuous strategy is laid bare by the current Government utilising only 8% of the funds allocated for Cyclone Ditwah victims by mid-2026, according to the World Bank. By overachieving revenue targets and underspending 2026 budgetary allocations by roughly Rs. 1 trillion compared to the proposed Rs. 7 trillion, the Government has manufactured a surplus of Rs. 22 billion on the back of neglected public services.

A glaring preview of what to expect in 2027 is visible in the first half of 2026. While the 2026 Budget allocated a combined public investment of Rs. 425 billion to healthcare (Rs. 120 billion), education (Rs. 99 billion), water supply and sanitation (Rs. 95 billion), public transport (Rs. 67 billion) and housing (Rs. 44 billion), the Mid-Year Fiscal Position Report shows that only Rs. 55 billion of these commitments were actually met in the first six months. Similarly, out of the allocated sum of Rs. 33.6 billion for providing meals to school children (reduced to Rs. 33.5 billion in 2027), only Rs. 12.7 billion was utilised in the first half of 2026, even as child malnutrition across the country continues to rise. Therefore, nominal increases for healthcare and education in the 2027 Budget are empty rhetoric destined to fall through the cracks.

The primary goal of the current regime is to manufacture budget surpluses to appease international speculative finance and regain the capacity to issue new International Sovereign Bonds (ISBs) under IMF mandates. The fifth and sixth IMF reviews under the External Fund Facility (EFF) for Sri Lanka clearly project $ 3 billion in new ISB borrowings for 2027 and 2028. Slashing Government spending and raising regressive taxes are manoeuvres designed solely to lower the risk premiums demanded by international finance capital.

Ever since the 2008/09 Western financial collapse, global speculative finance has aggressively targeted the Global South as a high-interest dumping ground for surplus capital, disregarding and violating prudent lending practices, and relying on the IMF to extract repayments through restructuring deals favourable to itself—such as the one signed onto by Sri Lanka in 2024—when third world debt bubbles ultimately burst. This marks the new wave of financial colonisation of the Global South.

Sri Lanka has remained decoupled from this cycle for the past five years due to the 2022 economic collapse, but the IMF’s primary mission today is to forcefully reintegrate Sri Lanka into global financial markets as a vehicle for surplus extraction. This is precisely why the IMF opposes exchange controls in the third world. These measures will enable the region to function independently of global speculative capital, delivering a lethal blow to this new form of dependency and surplus extraction towards the core from the periphery.

Operating within this framework, the incumbent administration systematically starves essential public services and relies on indirect taxation to maintain surpluses, using popular Budget rhetoric merely as a red herring. Furthermore, the regime’s alliance with a broadly unproductive, hyper-consumerist business elite prevents it from imposing exchange controls, halting illicit capital outflows to boost national savings destroyed through hyper-consumerism and resolving the balance of payments (BoP) crisis without resorting to speculative foreign financing through ISBs (rising BoP issues are indicated by the deficit in the current account recording over $ 400 million in the first nine months of 2026).

Finally, we emphasise that the horrendously repressive Protection of the State from Terrorism Act (PSTA) and Prevention of Organised Crimes Bill (POCA) have been deliberately introduced by the National People’s Power (NPP) regime to stamp out future public dissent, silence opposing voices against the increasingly unbearable living costs and crumbling state of essential services, and secure the interests of local and global speculative capital.
