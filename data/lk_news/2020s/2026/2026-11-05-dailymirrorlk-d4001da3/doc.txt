Dignity at work: Confronting the harassment of women in Sri Lanka’s workplaces

Every morning, hundreds of thousands of Sri Lankan women set out for work. They board crowded buses to garment factories in Katunayake and Biyagama, take their places at bank counters and hospital wards, teach in our schools, pluck tea on our estates and staff the offices of both the public and private sectors. They are, without exaggeration, the backbone of our economy. Yet, far too many of them carry an additional burden that their male colleagues seldom share, which is the fear, and too often the reality, of harassment at the very place where they go to earn an honest living.

Workplace harassment is not a minor matter of bad manners. It ranges from unwelcome remarks, persistent advances and humiliating ‘jokes’ to intimidation, demands for sexual favours in return for promotion or job security, and, at its worst, physical assault. Its consequences are serious as they range from lost confidence, anxiety and depression, ruined careers, and for many women, the decision simply to leave work altogether. When that happens, the loss is not hers alone; it is the nation’s.

Ask almost any working woman in this country, and she will have a story, either her own or that of a friend, sister or colleague. Yet, very few such stories ever reach a formal complaint, let alone a courtroom. The reasons are well known. Women fear losing their jobs, particularly in sectors where employment is precarious. They fear not being believed. They fear the gossip, the damage to their reputation and the pressure from family members who would rather the matter were quietly forgotten. Above all, many have no confidence that the system will protect them if they speak.

This silence is often mistaken for the absence of a problem. It is nothing of the sort. Where reporting channels are weak, where the alleged harasser is a supervisor with power over pay and promotion, and where the culture of an institution tolerates such conduct, silence is the rational response of a person with everything to lose. Our task, as legislators, employers and citizens, is to change that calculation.

It would be wrong to say that Sri Lanka has done nothing. Section 345 of the Penal Code, as amended by Act No. 22 of 1995, made sexual harassment a criminal offence, including harassment committed in the workplace. Our Supreme Court has, in several fundamental rights cases, recognised that sexual harassment at work violates a woman’s constitutional right to equality, and that employers, including the State, carry obligations to prevent it and to respond properly when it occurs. These are important foundations, and the judges who laid them deserve credit.

Earlier this year, Sri Lanka also ratified the International Labour Organisation’s (ILO’s) Violence and Harassment Convention, 2019 (No. 190), the first international treaty dedicated to eliminating violence and harassment, including gender-based harassment, in the world of work. In doing so, the Government committed itself to introducing the domestic legal provisions needed to give the Convention effect. I welcome that ratification wholeheartedly.

But let us be candid. Ratification is a promise, not a protection. Our existing framework remains scattered across the criminal law, labour statutes and case law. It is largely reactive, coming into play only after harm has been done, and it offers little to the many women who work in the informal sector, in domestic service or on short-term contracts. A criminal prosecution, with its high standard of proof and its long delays, is rarely the remedy a frightened employee needs. What she needs is a workplace that prevents harassment in the first place and deals with it swiftly, fairly and confidentially when it does occur.

We need not look to distant shores for answers. Several of our Asian neighbours, with legal traditions and social pressures not unlike our own, have already travelled this road. Their experience offers both encouragement and caution.

India is the obvious starting point. In 1997, in Vishaka v State of Rajasthan, the Indian Supreme Court laid down binding guidelines on workplace sexual harassment in the absence of legislation, much as our own Courts have sought to do. Parliament followed with the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act of 2013, widely known as the POSH Act. Its central feature is the Internal Committee, which every employer with 10 or more employees must constitute, presided over by a senior woman and including an external member drawn from a non-governmental organisation or someone familiar with the issues. Crucially, for women in small establishments, in domestic service or in the informal economy, the Act provides for Local Committees at the district level. Yet India also offers a warning. In 2023, its Supreme Court found that the law was being poorly implemented, with many committees either not constituted at all or not constituted properly and issued fresh directions. In August 2024, the Court considered requiring dashboards with information on the constitution and members of Internal Committees. The binding order came in December 2024. It directed SHe-Box complaint portals in all government departments, district officers in every district, Local Committees by 31 January 2025, and committee details to be placed on the SHe-Box portal. Since then, the Court has directed that district officers and Local Committees be appointed across the country and that committee details be published on the government’s SHe-Box portal. The lesson is plain; it is that a good law on paper must be matched by monitoring and transparency.

Pakistan enacted its Protection against Harassment of Women at the Workplace Act in 2010 and, notably, established a Federal Ombudsperson to whom women may complain directly, alongside inquiry committees within organisations. In 2022, Parliament widened the law considerably, extending it to the informal sector, to domestic workers and to students, and broadening the definition of harassment to include discrimination based on gender, whether or not it is sexual in nature. An independent, specialised ombudsperson gives a woman an alternative to an internal process she may not trust; that is a model worth our close attention.

The Philippines has gone further still in defining employers’ duties. Its Safe Spaces Act of 2019 requires employers to adopt a code of conduct prohibiting gender-based sexual harassment, developed in consultation with workers, and to establish an independent committee to investigate complaints. That committee must be headed by a woman, at least half of its members must be women, and it must resolve complaints within 10 days, with due process. The Act recognises that harassment may come from peers and even subordinates, not only from superiors, and that it may take place through text messages and email. Employers who fail in these duties may themselves be held liable.

Malaysia, for its part, passed the Anti-Sexual Harassment Act in 2022 and, in March 2024, brought into operation a dedicated Tribunal for Anti-Sexual Harassment. Its design deserves study. A complaint can be lodged for a filing fee of just 30 ringgit; lawyers are generally not permitted, which keeps proceedings simple and inexpensive; and the Tribunal may order an apology, compensation of up to RM250,000 or attendance at an appropriate programme. Recent reports indicate that the Tribunal has resolved most of its complaints within 60 days of its first hearing, and complaints may now be filed online. A low-cost, quick and accessible forum of this kind may achieve far more for an ordinary worker than a criminal prosecution that drags on for years.

Elsewhere in the region, Japan has for many years required employers to take preventive measures against sexual harassment, extended that duty to so-called ‘power harassment’ through amendments passed in 2019, and, since October 2026, also obliges employers to protect staff from harassment by customers and to prevent the harassment of job applicants.

Singapore’s Workplace Fairness Act, passed in 2025 and to be enacted end of 2027, will require employers to put in place proper grievance-handling processes for harassment and discrimination, to keep complainants’ identities confidential as far as possible and to refrain from any retaliation against those who come forward.

Also in South Asia, Bangladesh’s High Court issued binding guidelines in 2009 requiring complaints committees in workplaces and educational institutions, an example of judicial leadership filling a legislative gap.

From these experiences, a set of best practices emerges clearly. First, every workplace, however small, should have a route to redress, whether an internal committee or an external body at the district level. Second, committees should be led by women, include independent members and work to strict time limits. Third, there should be an independent external avenue, such as an ombudsperson or a tribunal, that is cheap, quick and does not require a lawyer. Fourth, the law must reach the informal sector and domestic workers. Fifth, harassment through digital channels and by third parties, such as customers, must be covered. Sixth, retaliation must be expressly prohibited. And finally, compliance must be monitored and published, so that the public can see whether the law is actually working.

First, Parliament should enact a dedicated Workplace Anti-Harassment Act, aligned fully with Convention No. 190. Such a law should define harassment clearly, apply to all workers in both the formal and informal sectors, and cover not only the office or factory floor but also work-related travel, accommodation, commuting and online communication.

Second, every employer above a reasonable size should be legally required to adopt a written anti-harassment policy, to establish an internal complaints committee that includes women and at least one independent member, and to investigate complaints within fixed time limits. Smaller employers and informal workers should have access to an external mechanism at the district level, as India’s Local Committees provide, so that no woman is left without a door to knock on. Alongside this, we should create an independent, low-cost forum, whether an ombudsperson on the Pakistani model or a specialised tribunal on the Malaysian one, where complaints can be heard swiftly and without the expense of lawyers.

Third, complainants and witnesses must be protected from retaliation. A woman who reports harassment and is then transferred, demoted or dismissed has been punished twice. The law should presume that adverse action taken against a complainant within a defined period is retaliatory unless the employer can prove otherwise.

Fourth, remedies must be meaningful. That means compensation for victims, proportionate disciplinary consequences for offenders and real penalties for employers who ignore their duties. A law without enforcement is a decoration, not a safeguard.

Fifth, and perhaps most importantly, we must invest in prevention. Regular training for managers and staff, awareness programmes in the language people actually speak, and clear leadership from the top will do more to change behaviour than any number of court judgments. When senior management makes it plain that harassment will not be tolerated, and acts accordingly, the culture of an institution changes.

It is tempting to treat workplace harassment as a ‘women’s issue’, to be left to women’s organisations and gender desks. That would be a mistake. Harassment drives talented people out of the workforce, depresses productivity, breeds mistrust and exposes institutions to legal and reputational damage. At a time when Sri Lanka is working hard to rebuild its economy, we simply cannot afford to lose the skills and energy of women who would otherwise work, lead and innovate. Increasing women’s participation in the labour force is an economic necessity, and safe workplaces are a precondition for it.

Men, too, have a vital part to play. Most men are not harassers, but silence from decent colleagues allows a minority to behave with impunity. Every man who challenges an offensive remark, supports a colleague who comes forward or insists that his organisation take complaints seriously makes our workplaces safer for his mother, his sister, his wife and his daughter.

At its heart, this is about dignity. Our Constitution guarantees equality before the law and freedom from discrimination on the ground of sex. Those guarantees ring hollow if a woman must choose between her livelihood and her self-respect. No woman should have to endure humiliation as the unspoken price of a pay packet.

We have taken the first step by joining the international community in committing to a world of work free from violence and harassment. The next step is ours to take at home. I urge the Government to bring forward comprehensive legislation without delay, and I call on employers, trade unions and civil society to work together to make it effective in practice. I, for my part, will support every sincere effort to that end.

The women of Sri Lanka have given this country their labour, their talent and their resilience. The very least we owe them in return is a workplace in which they are safe.

(Faiszer Musthapha is a President’s Counsel and a Member of Parliament)
