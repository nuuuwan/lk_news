Frequent eye rubbing could damage cornea; keratoconus detected in children as young as seven

Colombo, November 11 (Daily Mirror) - Frequent eye rubbing, particularly among children and young people suffering from eye allergies, could contribute to the development or progression of keratoconus, a progressive eye condition that can cause serious vision problems, a specialist has warned.

Marking World Keratoconus Day, yesterday, Consultant Cornea Surgeon Dr. Kusum Ratnayaka urged people with itchy eyes to seek medical advice rather than repeatedly rubbing them.

Keratoconus is increasingly being detected among Sri Lankans, including schoolchildren, with doctors now identifying cases in children as young as seven, the National Eye Hospital has cautioned.

“People who frequently rub their eyes or suffer from allergic eye conditions should be alert to the possibility of keratoconus,” Dr. Ratnayaka said.

The condition mainly affects young people between the ages of 15 and 25 and commonly develops during adolescence and early adulthood. However, its detection in children as young as seven or eight has raised concerns among specialists.

Keratoconus occurs when the cornea, the clear, dome-shaped front surface of the eye, gradually becomes thinner and bulges outwards into a cone-like shape. Normally, the cornea has a smooth, rounded surface that focuses light onto the retina. When it becomes cone-shaped, light is scattered, resulting in blurred and distorted vision.

In advanced cases, the condition can make everyday activities such as reading and driving difficult.

Dr. Ratnayaka said frequent changes in spectacle prescriptions could be an early warning sign, particularly among children. Parents should be alert if their children repeatedly need new spectacles or continue to experience vision problems despite wearing them.

Other symptoms include blurred vision, difficulty seeing at night, halos around lights and differences in vision between the two eyes.

People experiencing itchy eyes due to allergies or dry-eye symptoms should seek treatment to control the irritation and avoid repeatedly rubbing their eyes, he advised.

Doctors have also stressed the importance of school medical and vision-screening programmes, particularly examinations conducted among children in Grades 1, 4 and 7, to identify the condition at an early stage.

Early detection is important as keratoconus can progressively damage vision if left untreated. Spectacles may help correct vision during the early stages, while specialised contact lenses may be required as the condition advances.

Corneal collagen cross-linking is another treatment that strengthens the cornea and helps stop or slow the progression of the condition. Patients with advanced keratoconus may require corneal transplantation.

Specialists have urged parents to pay attention to frequent eye rubbing, repeated changes in spectacle prescriptions and difficulties in seeing clearly, and to arrange an eye examination if such symptoms are noticed.
