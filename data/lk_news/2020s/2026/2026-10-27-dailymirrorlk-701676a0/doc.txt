The politics of anti-terrorism laws

Both the ruling National People’s Power (NPP) and the Opposition parties -- especially the Samagi Jana Balawegaya (SJB), Sri Lanka Podujana Peramuna (SLPP) and the United National Party (UNP) -- are in a dilemma over the Protection of the State from Terrorism Bill (PSTB) that is presented in parliament.

The NPP’s predicament is that it was a party that fought tooth and nail against the Prevention of Terrorism Act (PTA) from the beginning of that law, arguing that the offences cited under it can be handled with  existing  laws, such as the Penal Code.

From a moral perspective, the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP), the prime constituent of the NPP, was the party in the country that suffered most under the PTA. Although the party was proscribed under the emergence regulations by the UNP government in 1983 under the false pretext of inciting anti-Tamil pogrom, the subsequent arrests and detention of the its members and supporters were carried out under the PTA. Most people so arrested or surrendered to the authorities never returned. The number of those who went missing those days is said to be over 60,000.

Nevertheless, the NPP suddenly woke up to realise that a law like the PTA is necessary to detain a suspect for prolonged questioning when a few suspects were arrested for allegedly threatening   Israeli tourists in Arugam Bay right after they assumed office in 2024. In fact, the people, including the Opposition,  were also dumbfounded when the police under a government headed by the JVP arrested those youths under the PTA.

The NPP’s problem here is that it realised some of its slogans were impractical only after coming to power, or once its victory in the 2024 election became inevitable. For instance, from the beginning they were demonising the International Monetary Fund (IMF) until they sensed in mid-2024 that they would no longer remain in the Opposition. They then sensed the danger of rejecting the IMF at a time when foreign reserves were running low. Thus, they began to adopt a milder approach towards the global lender.

It was a similar scenario that changed the NPP’s approach towards the PTA when the law enforcement authorities informed them of the necessity to detain suspects for a longer period, given the nature of the charges and their potential international implications. These might have been difficult decisions for the NPP leadership.

On the other hand, the negative stand held by the Opposition parties over the current PSTB represents sheer opportunism, given their past actions with regard to terrorism. It was the UNP that passed the PTA 47 years ago,  and it never agreed to repeal it until   international players pressed them to replace it with another law in par with international standards, during the so-called Yahapalana government.

Accordingly, a Bill named “Counter Terrorism Bill” (CTB) was presented in 2018 but only to be withdrawn in the light of protests by the human rights organisations here and abroad. It must be noted that the SJB is an offshoot of that UNP. Then, the UNP with the help of the SLPP presented almost the same Bill with a different title, “Anti-Terrorism Bill” in 2023.

The NPP government claims that the PSTB (PSTA once it is passed by the Parliament), unlike the past terrorism-related Acts and Bills, would not be a threat to activists who protest over economic or any other sociopolitical issues. One has to appreciate that the new law, unlike the previous laws on terrorism,  contains a section exempting actions such as “engaging in any protest, advocacy or dissent or engaging in any strike, lockout or other industrial action” from the list of offences that were deemed to be terrorism.

However, the proof of the pudding is in the eating -- the letter of the law and its practical application may diverge upon implementation. If you are arrested unfairly on terrorism charges under this law, you will eventually be released. However, the burden is on you to prove your innocence, during which time you will languish in a police cell or prison.
