How Sri Lanka can turn resources into riches

Colombo, Aug. 28 (Daily Mirror) - Switzerland, a landlocked nation devoid of precious metals or crude oil, stands as one of the world's richest economies. It thrives on precision machinery, pharmaceuticals, and high-end consumer goods like chocolate and watches, supported by the world's most trusted banking system. In stark contrast, Sri Lanka an island blessed with fertile soil, a surrounding ocean, valuable gems, and a highly educated populace remains a developing nation still grappling with economic turmoil. The question is not why Sri Lanka struggles, but why Switzerland succeeds, and how Sri Lanka can follow suit.

Switzerland’s success is a masterclass in value addition. With limited natural resources, it imports raw materials like gold, platinum, and cocoa, transforming them into high-value exports that command global prices. The Swiss model relies on a highly skilled workforce, institutional stability, and a sharp focus on niche, high end manufacturing and finance. It is an economy built on precision, trust, and education, where companies like Nestlé or Rolex generate more revenue than entire resource-rich nations.

Sri Lanka possesses almost every advantage Switzerland lacks. It has a strategic maritime location, a rich agricultural base (tea, rubber, coconut and spices), and a treasure trove of gems like blue sapphire and padparadsha Sapphire and minerals. Yet, the country has faced crippling economic crises, including a sovereign default in 2022 that led to shortages of fuel, food, and medicine.

While Sri Lanka exports high quality garments, tea, and rubber, it often exports them with low value addition. The country ships raw materials and semi-processed goods, missing the opportunity to build a manufacturing ecosystem that could command Swiss like premiums.

While economists point to fiscal mismanagement and trade deficits, a deeper pathology exists. Sri Lanka suffers from a "third deficit" an accountability deficit. Unlike Switzerland's decentralized, consensus driven governance, Sri Lanka's political system centralizes power, eroding institutional checks and balances. The inability of Parliament, the Central Bank, and the civil service to challenge reckless policy decisions such as sudden tax cuts and the ban on chemical fertilizers has repeatedly sabotaged the economy. This concentration of power, leading to policy reversals and a lack of long-term planning, deters the very foreign investment and stability that Switzerland offers.

Sri Lanka’s recovery requires a deliberate shift away from dependency on primary exports towards a value-added, export-oriented economy. The National Export Development Plan (NEDP) 2026–2030 provides a blueprint, targeting $36 billion in export revenue by focusing on sectors like auto components, rubber, minerals, and processed food.

Instead of exporting raw tea or coconut, Sri Lanka must focus on branded, processed, and packaged goods. The government is pushing to turn the coconut and food industry into a $3 billion sector by encouraging higher-value production.

Policy consistency is non-negotiable. The recent economic collapse was triggered by a combination of monetary errors, tax cuts, and global shocks. Sri Lanka must maintain the fiscal discipline required by the IMF while ensuring that reforms such as modernizing land and labour laws are implemented to improve the business climate.

To emulate Switzerland’s stability, Sri Lanka must rebuild the independence of its institutions. This means restoring the autonomy of the Central Bank, ensuring Parliament functions as a genuine check on executive power, and reverting to a professional, merit-based civil service. The "absentee Finance Minister" model and politically motivated policies must end.

Despite high female literacy, female labour force participation in Sri Lanka is only 31.6%, significantly hampering productivity. Investing in childcare, flexible work arrangements, and digital infrastructure would mobilize this underutilized workforce. Furthermore, the country must leverage its educated youth for the digital economy and business process outsourcing (BPO) sector, rather than solely relying on physical exports.

Switzerland transformed from a poor, rural, and resource poor nation into one of the world's richest countries in less than 200 years through strategic industrialization, political decentralization, and an intense focus on high-value niche manufacturing.

Switzerland proves that natural resources are not a prerequisite for wealth. Conversely, Sri Lanka proves that natural resources are not sufficient for prosperity. The downfall of Sri Lanka stems not from a lack of resources or talent, Sri Lanka does have deeply rooted legal and financial frameworks, blending colonial history with modern evolution. Main reason is failure of good governance and a lack of strategic value creation. To overcome this situation, Sri Lanka must "think like the Swiss."

Sri Lanka must adopt a Swiss mindset prioritising precision, stability, and value addition in every sector of the economy. This means shifting from reactive, short-term fixes to strategic, long-term thinking that builds resilience and global competitiveness.

But a national transformation of this scale cannot succeed without confronting the deep seated accountability deficits that have held the country back for decades. Weak governance, opaque decision-making, and a culture of impunity have eroded public trust and stifled progress.

To truly move forward, Sri Lanka must embed transparency, meritocracy, and rule of law at every level of public and private life. Only then can precision and stability take root and only then can the country add genuine value, not just for export markets, but for its own people.
