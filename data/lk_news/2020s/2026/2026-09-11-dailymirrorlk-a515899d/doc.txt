No imminent demise of US dollar despite diversification push

The US dollar is unlikely to lose its dominant position in the global reserve system anytime soon, despite increasing moves by investors and reserve managers to diversify their portfolios, Domenico Nardelli said.

Nardelli cautioned against drawing premature conclusions from concerns over US government debt, rising debt-servicing costs and questions surrounding the traditional risk-free status of US Treasuries. “It is not realistic to imagine an imminent demise of the dollar,” he said.

Nardelli acknowledged a series of developments that warranted attention, including the loss of the United States’ coveted AAA sovereign credit rating, the rapid expansion of US government debt and mounting debt-servicing costs.

“All of these are alarm bells for the US Treasury market. We, all of us, many of us in this room, as major holders of US Treasuries, should pay attention. These are important dynamics,” he said.

However, he stressed that the US dollar remained the world’s dominant reserve currency, while US Treasury markets continued to rank among the deepest and most liquid globally.

The international financial system also remained heavily anchored in dollar liquidity, he said.

Nardelli noted that the dollar’s share of global official foreign exchange reserves, while declining, still stood at 57 percent in the first quarter of 2026, according to International Monetary Fund data.

Total official foreign exchange reserves were approximately US$ 13 trillion, requiring a substantial portion of those funds to be placed in liquid assets.

Rather than abandoning dollar assets, Nardelli said global investors were increasingly seeking diversification across both asset classes and jurisdictions as geopolitical risks intensified.

“Global investors, instead of rushing to abandon US dollar assets, are looking to improve one key feature in their portfolios, and this is diversification, much more these days than it’s ever been,” he said.

Growing geopolitical fragmentation strengthened the case for multi-currency portfolios to spread exposure across jurisdictions rather than tying assets to a single market.

“In theory, the more fragmented the geopolitical environment becomes, the stronger the case for more diversification in a typical multi-currency portfolio, like a treasury or official institution portfolio,” Nardelli said.

He cautioned, however, that diversification was not without costs, with greater jurisdictional and product diversification potentially increasing operational complexity and portfolio maintenance requirements.
