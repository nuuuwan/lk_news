JVP and Presidential Pardons - A Chequered History

Almost all past presidential pardons in Sri Lanka except for general amnesties routinely granted to prisoners on major holidays like Vesak have been driven by political motives.

There have even been instances where prison officials smuggled the names of their associates onto general amnesty lists to secure their release during important holidays

The week-long protest organised by Buddhist monks demanding the release of Ven. Galagoda Aththe Gnanasara  — who is serving a prison sentence for contempt of court — concluded last Sunday without their demands being met.

The protesters announced they were suspending their struggle following the advice of the Mahanayaka Theras of the three main Nikayas (Buddhist chapters), who have stated they would hold talks with the government regarding Gnanasara Thera’s release.

The agitation originally began in Kandy with the demand that Gnanasara  be allowed to wear his saffron robes when being escorted out of prison. Later, this evolved into a direct demand for his acquittal and release.

In 2018, the Court of Appeal sentenced Ven. Gnanasara  to 19 years in prison, to be served concurrently over 6 years, for addressing the Homagama Magistrate’s court without permission and thereby committing contempt of court. After serving 9 months of the sentence, he was released on May 23, 2019, through a presidential pardon granted by then-President Maithripala Sirisena.

However, ruling on a petition challenging this decision, the Supreme Court declared on September10 that the pardon granted to him was unlawful, leading to his re-incarceration.

The Supreme Court decision or the resultant re-incarceration initially caused no public tension, but a major controversy arose later when the monk was brought to Colombo High Court on October 6 for another case wearing prison clothes instead of his saffron robes. This wardrobe issue has now snowballed into a campaign for his acquittal and release and mutated into a broader political conflict.

Opposition parties are unified in demanding an unconditional pardon for Ven. Gnanasara. Interestingly, among them are  those who are affiliated to the United National Party (UNP), the party that was in power when he was originally sentenced. Back then, they justified the decision which made Ven. Gnanasara  wear prison clothing. Video and audio clips of such instances are still being circulating on social media. Yet, during the recent robe controversy, they argued that monks should be allowed out of prison wearing their saffron robes.

Even those who traditionally advocate for the separation of religion and politics are also now demanding his pardon, seemingly supporting the Satyagraha (peaceful protest) held last week at Vihara Maha Devi Park. Some radical leftist parties opposing the government also seem to share this stance.

Conversely, government supporters fiercely criticised the protesting monks. While they justified presenting Ven. Gnanasara  in prison clothes in court, they also supported a government-appointed committee’s recommendation that monks should step out of prison in their saffron robes. Accusing others of using the saffron robe for political gain, they carried out mass demonstrations across Colombo and other regions along with the monks who support them. Whatever the merits or demerits of the demands made on behalf of Ven. Gnanasara may be, history shows that the executive power of the presidential pardon has long been abused. Almost all past presidential pardons in Sri Lanka—except for general amnesties routinely granted to prisoners on major holidays like Vesak—have been driven by political motives.

For instance, President J.R. Jayewardene pardoned Gonawala Sunil, a notorious loyalist of the UNP convicted of sexual assault in 1981 and President Mahinda Rajapaksa in 2009 pardoned  former minister Milroy Fernando’s wife, who was facing the death penalty for murder. President Maithripala Sirisena pardoned Jude Shramantha, who was convicted of murdering  a young woman, while President Gotabaya Rajapaksa pardoned Sunil Ratnayake, a soldier sentenced to death for the Mirusuvil massacre.

These unacceptable pardons have continuously triggered public outcry. Hence, people tend to look at Presidential pardons always with suspicion.

Some protesters question the adamancy of the leaders of the current government regarding their demands involving Ven. Gnanasara,   arguing that the same leaders enjoyed  Presidential pardons in the past after they were incarcerated for taking up arms against the government. However, the claim that past presidents simply “pardoned” those insurgents is historically inaccurate.

Following a brutal state oppression against Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP), the party launched an armed insurrection in 1971 against the government led by Sirimao Bandaranaike. The rebellion failed, and nearly 20,000 people, including JVP leaders, were arrested and detained under emergency regulations.

Meanwhile, during Sirimao Bandaranaike’s administration, the country faced a catastrophic economic crisis and severe food shortages. Serving rice was banned in restaurants on Tuesdays and Fridays, and transporting more than two measures (kilos) of rice across district lines was made illegal, among other drastic measures.

As public anger mounted, the Lanka Sama Samaja Party (LSSP) broke away from the government coalition in 1975. By early 1977, the LSSP prepared a no-confidence motion against the administration. Despite the ruling Sri Lanka Freedom Party (SLFP) holding a parliamentary majority, there were fears that some ruling party members might vote in favor of the motion due to the economic devastation. Also, fearing that the debate would damage her prospects in the upcoming general elections, Mrs. Bandaranaike prorogued the National State Assembly (Parliament) indefinitely on February 10, 1977. The emergency regulations enacted during the 1971 insurrection had been extended each month by parliament. However, because the parliamentary sessions were prorogued, that state of emergency automatically lapsed on February 15, leading to the release of thousands of detained insurgents the very next day. It was not a pardon.

However, JVP leaders and some others who had already been tried and sentenced under a specific new law—the Criminal Justice Commissions (CJC) Act—were not released. A few businessmen aligned with the UNP who were convicted under the same Act for foreign exchange fraud also remained jailed.

Following the UNP’s landslide victory in the 1977 general election, newly appointed Prime Minister J.R. Jayewardene immediately repealed the Criminal Justice Commissions Act to free his party allies. Then he declared a general amnesty for everyone convicted under the CJC Act on November 2, 1977, which consequentially freed the JVP leaders alongside Jayewardne’s associates. That was a pardon, but not particularly aimed at the JVP leaders.

The JVP was banned again under the false pretext of inciting 1983 “Black July” riots.  The JVP in turn took up arms again by 1986 due to the prolonged unfair proscription of it and the subsequent arrest and killing of its members and supporters. However, the UNP government lifted the ban at the peak of the conflict in May1988 following negotiations with a hitherto unknown person called K.C. Senanayake, who falsely posed as a representative for the JVP.  Even when Senanayake’s role was exposed in a few days, the ban was not reinstated. On the other hand, despite the JVP leadership being completely decimated in 1989 along with another 60,000 people, the lifting of the ban allowed the JVP to easily re-enter mainstream democratic politics after the UNP’s defeat in 1994. Therefore, their historic reintegration was a consequence of a regime change and a mistaken legal repeal, not a presidential pardon.
