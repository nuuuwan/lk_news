Unresolved VAT disputes could prolong prosecution risk for businesses: EY

The Sri Lankan businesses facing unresolved Value Added Tax (VAT) disputes could remain exposed to prosecution for an extended period under the new amendments that significantly widen the country’s tax enforcement framework, according to professional services firm EY.

The Value Added Tax (Amendment) Act, No. 14 of 2026, allows the criminal proceedings for the VAT offences to be instituted within 12 years of the date an offence was committed. Where the offence involves a failure to perform a statutory obligation, the period begins from the date on which that obligation was due.

However, the exposure could extend further in cases involving non-disclosure or misrepresentation affecting a taxpayer’s VAT liability. In such instances, the prosecution period begins only from the date on which the correct liability is finally determined.

EY said this could prolong the period of exposure where the audits, disputes, reviews or appeals remain unresolved, raising the stakes for the businesses with historical VAT matters.

The provision could require the companies to pay closer attention to legacy transactions, supporting documents and record retention, particularly where the VAT assessments remain under challenge.

The amendments also authorise the Attorney-General or an authorised person to prosecute the VAT offences, confirming that such violations may proceed through a formal criminal process where warranted.

The maximum monetary penalty for a range of VAT offences has meanwhile increased fortyfold. The offences committed before October 1, 2025 carry a maximum fine of Rs.25,000, imprisonment of up to six months or both. For the offences committed on or after that date, the maximum fine rises to Rs.1 million, while the possible prison term remains at six months.

The offences include failing to register for VAT, file returns, maintain proper books and records or issue valid tax invoices. Providing incorrect information affecting a person’s VAT liability, obstructing the Inland Revenue officials and issuing invoices without being registered for VAT can also attract penalties.

Failure to provide valid tax invoices, customs goods declarations or other required documents may similarly lead to a fine of up to Rs.1 million, imprisonment of up to six months or both.

The amended law also makes it an offence to obtain or attempt to obtain a VAT refund through fraud, misrepresentation, false or misleading information, fraudulent documents or the concealment of material facts.

The separate amendments introduced through the Inland Revenue (Amendment) Act, No. 11 of 2026, expand enforcement beyond administrative penalties by allowing prosecution for several forms of tax non-compliance.

These include failures to register with the Commissioner General of Inland Revenue, file income tax returns, furnish specified tax returns, submit annual statements or appear before the Commissioner General following a statutory notice.

Prosecution will not begin automatically. The Inland Revenue Department must first issue a written notice and give the taxpayer an opportunity to rectify the default. A conviction may result in a fine of up to Rs.400,000, imprisonment of up to six months or both.

The amendments also allow the department to recover the unpaid taxes through the Magistrate’s Court proceedings, where the amount in default may be treated as a court-imposed fine. This mechanism will not apply while a review or appeal remains pending.

EY cautioned that the Inland Revenue Department’s enhanced enforcement powers should be matched by greater responsibility, particularly because of the evidentiary weight attached to a certificate issued by the Commissioner General.

It said the assessments should be based on proper facts and law, with due process, proper notice and administrative fairness maintained as enforcement is strengthened.

The amendments mark a shift towards a more prosecution-driven tax compliance regime, increasing the importance of accurate filings, documentation and the timely resolution of outstanding disputes.

The two laws were enacted in June and July 2026, according to Sri Lanka’s official register of acts of 2026.
