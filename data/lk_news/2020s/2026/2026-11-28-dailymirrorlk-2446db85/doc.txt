Small-scale but attractive: Geopolitical factors shaping  Sri Lanka’s critical minerals prospects

The country’s strategic location in the Indian Ocean, combined with the competing interests of India and China, places it in a delicate position

Sri Lanka’s mineral industry is small-scale, centred on a handful of products such as premium vein graphite and mineral sands. Limited capital, technological gaps, and resistance from environmental activists have prevented the sector from scaling into a large, lucrative industry. However, the National Peoples’ Power (NPP) government headed by President Anura Kumara Dissanayake hopes to develop the sector and find a place in the global critical minerals market.

Hasarel Gallage of King’s College London examines the island nation’s prospects in the expanding global critical minerals sector in a paper entitled “Sri Lanka’s Critical Minerals: Navigating a Contested Geopolitical Landscape,” published in www.factum.lk

Sri Lanka has a small critical minerals sector. And yet, it is attracting the attention of both the government and foreign investors. The newly-launched critical minerals policy situates the domestic industry within broader geopolitical and geoeconomic calculations.

The country’s strategic location in the Indian Ocean, combined with the competing interests of India and China, places it in a delicate position. India and the United States have voiced concern over expanding Chinese projects and maritime reconnaissance activity. In 2023, the docking of a Chinese research vessel in Colombo prompted strong opposition from both capitals, after which Sri Lanka imposed a one-year moratorium on foreign research vessels entering its waters.

Despite these anxieties, the government intends to develop the mineral industry and integrate it into global supply chains.

Historically left-leaning and wary of regional intervention, the NPP has adopted a pragmatic stance since taking office and has welcomed foreign investors. On 31 August, President Dissanayake chaired a meeting with mineral investors and the Geological Survey and Mines Bureau, urging faster exploration and licensing to attract capital.

India–Sri Lanka economic relations have long mixed aspiration with inertia. Efforts to expand the 1998 Free Trade Agreement into a Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement stalled amid domestic opposition, as did the later Economic and Technology Cooperation Agreement (ETCA). The Adani Group’s proposed US$1 billion wind project in Mannar received initial approval and a 20-year power-purchase agreement, only for the NPP government to revoke it in January 2025 amid tariff disputes and environmental protests; Adani withdrew the following month.

Public sentiment towards India has nevertheless improved. The Spring 2026 Pew Research Centre Global Attitudes Survey recorded that more than 79 percent of Sri Lankans hold a favourable view of India, a shift attributed in part to $ 4.5 billion Indian support during Sri Lanka’s recent economic crisis. This improved climate forms the backdrop against which potential mineral partnerships should be assessed.

India’s critical-mineral motivations are driven by clean-energy targets - 50 percent non-fossil electricity capacity by 2030 and net-zero emissions by 2070. Domestic electric-vehicle sales are rising rapidly. The World Resources Institute projects that demand for seven core minerals, including aluminium, copper and manganese, will double or triple by 2030.

India remains 100 per cent import-dependent for lithium, cobalt and nickel, and roughly 75 percent of its EV lithium-ion batteries come from China. This dependence exposes its automotive, electronics and clean-energy sectors to supply disruptions, price volatility and export restrictions.

Therefore, India’s National Critical Mineral Mission seeks to acquire 50 foreign mineral assets by 2031, focusing mainly on cobalt, copper and lithium in Africa, Latin America and Australia.

Its interest in Sri Lanka centres on vein graphite and mineral sands. Current volumes remain modest: In 2025, India imported an estimated 53,900 tonnes of natural graphite, with Tanzania, Madagascar and China supplying the bulk; Sri Lanka’s share was only 0.1 percent.

Among Sri Lanka’s own graphite exports in 2025, India ranked outside the top five destinations, taking just 3 percent, while Japan took 44 percent.

Most of India’s imports are flake graphite, which is cheaper and easier to extract from surface deposits. Sri Lankan vein graphite, by contrast, is prized for its exceptional purity (the carbon content often exceeding 95 percent) but is mined underground by labour-intensive methods, limiting scale and raising costs. Talks involving the Indian state-owned enterprise Khanij Bidesh India Limited (KABIL) on possible investment in the vein-graphite industry offer hope.

A further complication in the India-Sri Lanka relationship is the unresolved status of the Afanasy Nikitin Seamount (ANS), an undersea ridge roughly 1,050 km southeast of Sri Lanka that contains cobalt, nickel and manganese deposits. Sri Lanka’s 2009 submission to the UN Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf seeking to extend its maritime boundary, remains suspended because of India’s formal protest lodged in 2022.

India’s own 2024 application to the International Seabed Authority for an exploration licence is likewise frozen.

Gallage argues that an India-Sri Lanka negotiation on ANS is the only realistic path forward. She points out that Sri Lanka possesses leverage -- without its cooperation, India’s application cannot proceed. Sri Lanka could also use that leverage to seek capital and technology transfer from India for mining capacity.

But any India-Sri Lanka arrangement would also have to navigate China’s deeper economic presence and rising cobalt demand.

China’s approach differs fundamentally from India’s. While India seeks resource acquisition, China aims to preserve its market dominance and processing strength. However, because China already controls substantial overseas assets and possesses abundant domestic resources, including all 17 rare-earth elements, it is unlikely to prioritise Sri Lanka’s comparatively modest deposits.

Trade figures reflect limited Sino-Sri Lankan interdependence in graphite. China ranked only seventh among destinations for Sri Lankan graphite in 2024, taking 1.8 percent of exports. But mineral sands tell a different story. China imported roughly 1,300 tonnes of titanium ore and concentrates, natural quartz and related products from Sri Lanka in 2024 and absorbed 24.5 percent of Sri Lanka’s titanium-ore exports (second only to India’s 54.6 percent).

Chinese capital is also present indirectly through partnerships such as that between Titanium Sands Limited and UK-based Global Mining Synergies, which seeks Chinese offtake and financing. Broader Belt and Road infrastructure—particularly the Chinese-operated Hambantota Port industrial park and the South Container Terminal in Colombo—could facilitate midstream processing or efficient export logistics, even if mining itself is not a core BRI focus, Gallage observes.

Western interest in critical minerals is driven by a derisking logic aimed at reducing dependence on China. Initiatives such as the US Minerals Security Partnership, the European Union’s Critical Raw Materials Act, and the 2025 Quad Critical Minerals Initiative (backed by US$20 billion in funding) channel capital toward diversified supply.

But Sri Lanka does not feature prominently in these plans. It lacks large-scale deposits of the lithium and cobalt most sought by Western industry. Clearly, Western governments have shown willingness to accept higher security risk elsewhere in exchange for volume.

However, Western and allied markets remain the principal destination for Sri Lankan graphite: In 2025 the top five export markets—led by Japan, the United States and Germany—accounted for approximately 85 percent of volume, while India and China together took less than 6 percent.

Gallage says that Sri Lanka’s most viable strategy is poly-alignment. Existing instruments such as the India–Sri Lanka Free Trade Agreement and the South Asian Free Trade Agreement can be leveraged to deepen regional mineral partnerships. Accelerating negotiations on the Economic and Technology Cooperation Agreement with India would improve access to Indian capital and processing capacity.

Membership of the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) would simultaneously strengthen ties with China and open automatic links to other mineral-producing economies in Southeast Asia.

Connecting Sri Lankan graphite more tightly with India’s expanding processing industry and with Japan’s industrial demand would allow the island to occupy a specialised, high-value niche rather than attempting to compete on volume alone.

In short, Sri Lanka possesses high-quality but limited mineral resources. But Gallage says that success will depend on the government’s ability to attract technology and capital while carefully managing the geopolitical pressures that inevitably accompany critical-mineral development in the Indian Ocean.
