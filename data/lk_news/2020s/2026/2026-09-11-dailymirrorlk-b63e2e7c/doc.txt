Sri Lanka should consider follow-on IMF programme beyond 2027: Krishan Balendra

Reform continuity could pave way for rating upgrades and lower borrowing costs

Para-tariff removal only “the beginning” of trade reform, says Balendra

Sri Lanka should consider entering into a follow-on International Monetary Fund (IMF) supported programme or an equivalent framework once the current extended fund facility lapses in April 2027 to preserve reform momentum and maintain policy credibility, Ceylon Chamber of Commerce (CCC) Chairman Krishan Balendra said.

Addressing the Sri Lanka Retail Forum 2026, organised by the Sri Lanka Retailers Association (SLRA) in Colombo this week, Balendra stressed that while the country has achieved notable macroeconomic stability following the 2022 economic crisis, the true test lies in converting that stability into durable, long-term expansion.

“As we look beyond stabilisation, Sri Lanka should consider continuing its stability story through a follow-on IMF supported programme once the current programme lapses in April 2027, or a similar framework that preserves reform momentum beyond the current programme period,” Balendra stated.

He pointed out that maintaining such an institutional anchor would provide strong reassurance to the investor community while insulating the economy against external and domestic shocks.

“Such an approach would provide confidence to investors, support sustainable economic growth, strengthen resilience against both external and domestic shocks, and reinforce policy credibility. Importantly, sustained reform continuity can pave the way for further improvements in Sri Lanka’s sovereign credit rating,” he noted, adding that an improved credit profile would significantly lower borrowing costs, enhance access to international capital and facilitate private sector investment.

Emphasising that policy predictability is paramount for both large retail networks and small enterprises making capital commitments, Balendra cautioned that sudden and frequent policy shifts create friction, escalate costs and discourage business expansion.

“For retailers and businesses across the country, policy certainty remains one of the most valuable forms of economic infrastructure,” he remarked.

Turning to external trade, the Chamber chief underscored that Sri Lanka’s relatively small domestic market necessitates deeper integration with regional and global supply chains. He welcomed the government’s ongoing dismantling of para-tariffs as an encouraging move toward liberalisation, but stressed that it must be part of a much wider structural push.

“The ongoing removal of para-tariffs is a notable and welcome first step. It sends an important signal regarding Sri Lanka’s commitment to improving competitiveness and becoming more integrated with global markets. However, this should be viewed as the beginning rather than the culmination of our reform journey,” he said, calling for comprehensive reforms in trade facilitation, logistics efficiency, customs modernisation and bilateral trade agreements.

Meanwhile, Balendra expressed strong optimism about Sri Lanka’s strategic position in the Indian Ocean, comparing the current regional dynamic to the boom experienced by China’s neighbours two decades ago. He said proximity to India, which he described as the fastest-growing major economy globally, is already delivering dividends. He pointed to Indian tourist arrivals rising from around 150,000 a decade ago to over 500,000 last year, and to the Port of Colombo, where roughly 85 percent of volumes are transshipment cargo predominantly serving India.

He highlighted that domestic retailers and small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) form the bedrock of the economy, urging policymakers and financial institutions to place special emphasis on scaling medium enterprises into the next generation of national champions through improved access to finance, digital adoption and market access.
