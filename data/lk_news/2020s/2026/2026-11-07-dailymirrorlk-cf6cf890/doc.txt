A question  is part of  the care

A patient leaves a clinic with instructions but does not fully understand the next step. The document is complete; the assistance is not.

In Sri Lanka, where families often help one another navigate appointments and everyday obligations, we should remember that a person who asks for clarification is not wasting anyone’s time. A clear explanation may prevent confusion long after the consultation ends.

My Christian faith takes me to Jesus’ question to a man in need: “What do you want me to do for you?” He listened before acting. The lesson is not to assume that we understand another person’s difficulty simply because we have seen the paperwork.

Health workers deserve respect and adequate support; families deserve accurate information. Neither can replace the other. Clinics and public services should give people a practical way to raise a second question without humiliation.

A peaceful community is one where asking for help does not make a person feel smaller. That is a standard all neighbours can defend, whatever their beliefs.
