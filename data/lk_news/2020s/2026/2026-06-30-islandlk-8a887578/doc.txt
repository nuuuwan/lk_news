Mangrove restoration seen as creating jobs and promoting rural economic vibrancy

Mangrove restoration is emerging as a powerful rural economic development model, generating income, strengthening community enterprises, creating village savings schemes and opening new opportunities in nature-based tourism, according to leading marine scientist Professor Sevvandi Jayakody.

Speaking to The Island Financial Review, Professor Jayakody said ecosystem restoration should no longer be viewed solely as an environmental exercise but as a long-term investment that delivers measurable economic and social returns while safeguarding Sri Lanka’s natural capital.

“Restoration is helping the community in multiple ways. Let us go into the finances first, because money matters to everyone, Professor Jayakody said.

Using the Anawilundawa mangrove restoration initiative as an example, she explained that every visitor to the area contributes directly to the local economy. Community members prepare meals, provide services and receive income, while part of the earnings is channeled into a community-managed revolving fund.

“Like you are here today, and you enjoyed their food. That is an income for the community. It also builds community pride because everyone loves to see visitors enjoying what they have to offer, she said.

Beyond immediate earnings, the project has become an engine for rural entrepreneurship. A percentage of every payment made by visitors is saved collectively, enabling villagers to build a community fund that finances small loans, purchases equipment and supports local enterprises.

“Every time we have visitors, the community provides the food and saves part of the income. That has now become their community fund. They are using it to provide loans to members, buy equipment, from cutlery to pottery, and organise themselves much better, she explained.

Jayakody said the initiative demonstrates that environmental restoration can become an economic asset when local communities are positioned as partners rather than spectators.

Equally important is the investment in human capital. Through education and continuous engagement, villagers have acquired scientific knowledge that enables them to identify mangrove species, monitor flowering and fruiting cycles, collect propagules responsibly and participate actively in ecosystem management.

“Nature-based tourism is about working with nature and ensuring that it is in a better condition than when we started. It is not simply using nature for tourism. The goal is to continuously improve the ecosystem while creating sustainable economic opportunities for local communities, she said.
