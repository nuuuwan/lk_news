Can Budget 2027 help Sri Lanka keep its talent?

By Yohan Perera Lecturer, Faculty of Management and Finance, University of Colombo [email protected]

Colombo, Nov. 28 (Daily Mirror) - When a young Sri Lankan becomes a doctor, engineer, researcher or university lecturer, that achievement is rarely theirs alone. Families have made sacrifices, teachers have invested years of effort, and public institutions have often contributed to their education. But then comes a difficult question. Will they build a career in Sri Lanka or look elsewhere?

There is nothing wrong with seeking opportunities abroad. Many Sri Lankans have improved their families' lives by doing so. But for a country investing heavily in education and professional training, the movement of skilled people raises an important policy question. Are we creating enough opportunities for them to use their knowledge here? The proposed 2027 Budget gives us a reason to look at this question more closely.

According to the Ministry of Finance's Citizen Budget, the proposed education-sector allocation for 2027 is Rs. 749 billion, compared with Rs. 708 billion in the 2026 Citizen Budget. The 2027 figure includes Rs. 562.7 billion for school education, Rs. 148.4 billion for university education and Rs. 25.7 billion for vocational education. The overall proposed allocation is about 5.8% higher than the 2026 figure, nominally. Of the broad education allocation, about Rs. 645.4 billion is recurrent expenditure and Rs. 104 billion is capital expenditure. Paying teachers and running institutions are essential, although such spending is different from building new facilities and improving equipment.

The trend in university education is worth examining. The corresponding allocations were Rs.104.5 billion in 2024, Rs. 135.5 billion in 2025 and Rs. 135.8 billion in 2026. The proposed Rs. 148.4 billion for 2027 continues that upward movement. These are budget allocations expressed in current rupees, not figures adjusted for inflation, and the scope of spending categories may change over time. Bigger allocations do not necessarily mean an equal improvement in facilities or results.

The Budget Speech also envisages Rs. 11 billion in capital investment for vocational education and training, including updated facilities and curricula. Separately, it identifies Rs. 23.8 billion for research and development programmes in 2027. It proposes supporting local researchers and helping universities turn research into products and services useful to society and industry. These plans deserve attention. A researcher needs more than a qualification. Laboratories, equipment, funding, institutional support and the freedom to pursue worthwhile work all influence whether a research career can grow at home.

The budget also recognises the financial concerns of some professionals. It proposes increasing university academics' monthly allowance by Rs. 25,000, with half the increase starting in July 2027 and the balance in July 2028. It proposes raising the DAT allowance for medical officers from Rs. 70,000 to Rs. 95,000, also in stages. Payments to visiting university lecturers would increase from Rs. 1,500 to Rs. 4,500 an hour. These measures acknowledge that people who teach, research and provide essential services need adequate financial support.

But deciding whether to stay or leave usually involves more than the next pay increase.

Concerns about professional migration are more than anecdotal. Speaking at the World Health Assembly in May 2025, Health Minister Nalinda Jayatissa reported that 726 specialist consultants, 1,116 medical officers and nearly 2,800 nursing officers had gone abroad between 2022 and the time of his statement in 2025. These figures refer to health professionals, not all graduates or knowledge workers. They show the pressure that departures can place on services requiring specialist skills.

The Sri Lanka Bureau of Foreign Employment recorded 311,223 registrations for foreign employment in 2025, compared with 314,670 in 2024. These totals cover many kinds of occupations, and we must not mistake them for the number of highly educated people permanently leaving the country. They show how important overseas employment remains to Sri Lankan households.

Migration also brings considerable benefits. Central Bank figures show workers' remittances rising from about US$6.58 billion in 2024 to US$8.08 billion in 2025. That is an increase of around 22.8%. For many families, overseas earnings support housing, education and everyday needs. For the country, they provide valuable foreign exchange. It would be unfair to describe every departure as a national loss.

The important question is whether Sri Lankans have a genuine choice. If a skilled professional wants to remain, can they expect a fair income, worthwhile work, opportunities to progress and institutions that value their contribution? An allowance increase can help, especially when living costs are high. But it cannot solve a shortage of modern equipment, limited promotion prospects, heavy workloads or a lack of opportunities to apply specialised skills.

For those of us working in human resource management, training people is only one part of the task. Helping them grow professionally and making good use of their abilities matter just as much. A university can produce qualified graduates, but it cannot create a healthy job market on its own. Employers, public institutions and economic policy all have a part to play.

To make the 2027 investments worthwhile, the government should report clearly on what it delivers. For universities, technical colleges and research programmes, this means more than announcing allocations. People should be able to see whether facilities were completed, research programmes began, and graduates found suitable opportunities. These results take time to emerge, but planning to measure them should begin when programs are funded.

Pay and allowances matter, but employers of highly trained people should also improve everyday career conditions. Hospitals need workable staffing arrangements and professional development. Universities need functioning research facilities, transparent career paths and opportunities to collaborate beyond their campuses. Businesses need reasons to create skilled jobs. None of this can be delivered by one budget speech, but the funding decisions made now can support that direction.

The budget's proposals concerning Sri Lankans overseas should also lead to something more than citizenship documents. The speech proposes a Global Sri Lankan Heritage Citizen programme and wider dual-citizenship benefits for eligible family members and descendants. These may help maintain ties with the country. The budget also proposes Rs. 1 billion in government support for an interest-subsidised housing loan scheme for migrant workers. That is a form of support for overseas workers, although it is not specifically a programme for engaging knowledge professionals. There is room to go further. Overseas doctors, researchers, academics and other professionals could mentor, teach, advise and work with Sri Lankan institutions through short visits and online collaboration. That would allow people to contribute without returning permanently.

The 2027 Budget makes important commitments to education, research and professional development. These initiatives deserve recognition, but their contribution to retaining skilled Sri Lankans will depend on how effectively they are implemented. Financial incentives alone cannot address the wider concerns surrounding career development, working conditions and professional opportunities. In parallel, Sri Lanka should establish practical ways for professionals working overseas to contribute their knowledge and experience to national development. The success of these budget commitments should therefore be assessed not merely through financial allocations, but through the opportunities they create for Sri Lankans to develop and apply their skills, both at home and abroad.
