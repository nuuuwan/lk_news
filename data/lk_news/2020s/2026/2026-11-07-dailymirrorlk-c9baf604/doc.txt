Elephants carry kilos of bullets in their bodies in Sri Lanka

With hundreds killed annually, gunshot wounds, improvised explosive devices and shrinking habitats continue to threaten the country’s wild elephant population

Sri Lanka’s wild elephants are carrying large amounts of metal fragments inside their bodies after being shot by poachers, with some suffering from serious injuries even years after being attacked.

Environmental activist Nayanaka Ranwella has raised concerns, claiming that elephant Agbo had at least 75 kilograms of lead from bullets inside its body, while the famous blind Galgamuwa tusker had more than 25 kilograms following gunshot injuries.

He accused successive governments and wildlife authorities of failing to take effective action against poachers and illegal activities inside forests.

According to Ranwella, poachers and some farmers use locally made firearms known as ‘Gal Katas’, containing ammunition made from iron balls, bucket hooks and scrap metal.

When fired, these fragments penetrate different parts of an elephant’s body, causing multiple injuries.

“Countless gunshot wounds can be seen all over the body of the famous wild tusker Agbo. At least 75 gunshot wounds can be seen within a single square-foot area of its body. Some of the wounds have healed, but the iron fragments remain embedded inside the body. Others are still open and have not healed,” he said.

A senior veterinary association official explained that metal fragments could remain inside elephants for years, causing chronic inflammation, recurring infections, abscesses, persistent pain and difficulty walking.

Removing fragments lodged deep inside the body or near vital organs is not always possible. Affected elephants may require prolonged veterinary treatment, while some suffer permanent damage.

Ranwella questioned why people allegedly manufacturing explosive baits, possessing illegal firearms and setting traps had not been arrested despite information being available to officials.

He claimed to have raised concerns about elephant poaching for nearly 15 years without effective action.

Ranwella said 14 routes used by poachers had been identified inside Yala National Park and questioned why drones and hidden cameras were not being deployed to monitor them.

He also referred to the killing of an elephant named Neela in the Kala Oya area, claiming that information about the suspected killer had been submitted to wildlife authorities without any arrest being made six months later.

Ranwella raised concerns over a proposed rice mill in Ampara, claiming it could obstruct an elephant corridor used by between 200 and 300 elephants.

He said radio-collar tracking of two elephants, Udani and Sugala, had identified the proposed site as part of an important elephant route.

According to Ranwella, the migration map was presented to former President Mahinda Rajapaksa, who reportedly expressed concern about construction along the corridor. However, the land was subsequently allocated for the proposed rice mill.

Ranwella warned that blocking the corridor connecting the Ampara Sanctuary with Buddhangala could force elephants towards Ampara town, increasing human-elephant conflict.

“If even a single elephant entering the town causes such a commotion, imagine what happens when 200 or 300 arrive!” he said. He also demanded an investigation into Agbo’s injuries and the release of Government Analyst reports concerning the incident.

Department of Wildlife Conservation statistics show that 289 elephants died between January 1 and October 23, 2026, with the cause of death unidentified in 95 cases.

Gunshot injuries accounted for 42 deaths, while electrocution and improvised explosive devices known as ‘hakka patas’ caused another 34 deaths each.

Another 23 elephants were recorded after their bodies had become putrefied, while infected wounds caused 11 deaths. Other causes included accidents, drowning, poisoning and worm infestations.

The Eastern Wildlife Region recorded the highest number of deaths at 60, followed by Anuradhapura with 57 and Polonnaruwa with 42.

Between 2010 and October 23, 2026, Sri Lanka recorded 5,397 elephant deaths.

Polonnaruwa accounted for 1,181 deaths, followed by the Eastern Wildlife Region with 1,023 and Anuradhapura with 799.

The highest annual death toll was recorded in 2023, when 488 elephants died, followed by 455 in 2025 and 439 in 2022. From the slaughter of the Panamure Tusker to today’s indiscriminate poaching.

Sri Lanka has a long history of elephant exploitation. the Panamure Tusker, killed on August 10, 1950, during the country’s last elephant kraal, remains a symbol of resistance against the capture of wild elephants.

The operation, organised by Sir Francis Molamure, involved driving elephants into a wooden enclosure. One powerful Tusker repeatedly resisted capture and was eventually shot.

Its killing triggered public outrage and parliamentary debate, contributing to the eventual end of elephant kraals.

During British colonial rule, elephant hunting was encouraged to clear plantation land. Major Thomas William Rogers, a colonial officer in Badulla, was estimated to have killed between 1,400 and 2,000 elephants.

He died after reportedly being struck by lightning in Haputale in June 1845, an incident that became part of local folklore.

Sri Lanka is home to some of the largest Asian elephants, with adult males reaching around 10 feet in height. The late tusker Kavantissa, measuring approximately 11 feet, was considered among the country’s tallest.

However, habitat destruction, poaching and human-elephant conflict continue to threaten their survival.

Amid growing concerns, the Government launched the ‘Hasthi Sena’ (Elephant Force) operation in Galkiriyagama on November 2.

The islandwide operation is jointly implemented by the Ministry of Environment, Armed Forces and Department of Wildlife Conservation.

Environment Minister Dhammika Patabendi said the initial phase would focus on protecting 17 elephants facing threats.

However, its success will depend on addressing illegal firearms, explosive traps, electrocution and the destruction of elephant corridors.

Ranwella said he had continued his campaign under four governments and would keep speaking out until effective action was taken.

For a country that takes pride in its elephants, the challenge is not only preventing their deaths but protecting survivors from years of suffering caused by bullets and metal fragments lodged inside their bodies.
