Pakistan becomes more Middle Eastern than South Asian

Ties with the Islamic world deepened during the 1970s under Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, when Gulf labour migration began (Photo courtesy of Chehra Digital)

On August 22, 2025, US President Donald Trump announced on Truth Social that he was appointing his close aide, Sergio Gor, as the next US Ambassador to India and Special Envoy for South and Central Asian Affairs. Seen against the historical background of India’s sensitivity about being clubbed together (being “hyphenated” with Pakistan), this might have been unwelcome in New Delhi.

In July 2025, the World Bank removed Pakistan from the South Asia region and placed it in the Middle East, North Africa, Afghanistan and Pakistan (MENAAP) region. In the World Bank’s new scheme, the South Asia region now comprises only India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan and the Maldives. The reclassification reflects the emerging geopolitical landscape.

On June 16, 2026, the US renamed its “Indo-Pacific Command” as the “Pacific Command”, removing “Indo” from it.

These designatory changes stemmed from emerging geopolitical realities, shifts in the distribution of power, and the Western world’s changing interests.

The World Bank’s decision to shift Pakistan from South Asia to the Middle East followed Pakistan’s  reorienting its foreign policy towards the Middle East, principally to Iran, Saudi Arabia and Turkiye. Islamabad had joined the US in its bid to get a deal with Iran to end the war. Pakistan gets a lot of aid from Saudi Arabia and armaments from Turkey. On top of it all, Pakistan is now a key partner in the politico-military “Mecca Pact” with Saudi Arabia and Turkiye.

Over the years, Pakistan loosened its ties with the rest of South Asia. As of today, it has very little trade, economic relations, or people-to-people ties with any country in South Asia. Lately, it has been making attempts to build bridges with post-Hasina Bangladesh. But these have been minimal and tentative.

And SAARC, to which Pakistan still belongs, is not active due to the India-Pakistan conflict over Kashmir. If today South Asia is relevent  for Pakistan, it is only because of its conflict with India over Kashmir. Pakistan’s civil and military leadership is fully engaged in cultivating ties with the US and the Middle Eastern Islamic regimes, while keeping its ties with China intact.

The World Bank says that the change in the classification is for analytical purposes only. It is partly in recognition of the fact that Pakistan’s economic and strategic centre of gravity has shifted to the Middle East and partly because it is logical to put countries which share development challenges, economic structures, and governance patterns. This enables meaningful research, lending programmes, and policy advice.

Pakistan received US$ 38.3 billion in remittances from the Middle East in fiscal year 2025, a figure that exceeded its entire IMF loan programme and represented a 27% increase on the previous year. More than half of that came from Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) States. Saudi Arabia alone sent $739.6 million in January 2026, the UAE $694.2 million in the same month. Remittances are now Pakistan’s single largest non-debt external inflow.  Pakistan imports liquefied natural gas from Qatar and has structured industrial policy around Gulf sovereign wealth fund investment, says Aishwarya Sanjukta Roy Proma of Rajshahi University, Bangladesh.

Historically too, Pakistan has had a Middle Eastern orientation. In the 1950s, it became part of the US-led CENTO ( Central Treaty Organisation), a politico-military group of Islamic nations meant to resist the Communist USSR. Ties with the Islamic world deepened during the 1970s under Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, when Gulf labour migration began, and Pakistan became a significant exporter of construction and service workers to Saudi Arabia and the UAE. Ties with the Middle East deepened further under Zia ul-Haq in the 1980s, when Pakistan’s role as a frontline state in the Afghan jihad made it structurally dependent on Gulf financing and American military assistance. In 2013 itself, the IMF had created the MENAP ( Middle East, North Africa, Afghanistan, and Pakistan), Roy Proma recalls.

In World Bank’s MENAAP group, the working-age population of approximately 380 million is projected to grow by 40%  in the next 25 years. But economic growth across the region is insufficient to generate adequate employment for this kind of growth in the population. Economist Muhammad Usman Hashmi says that the World Bank’s shift reflects structural economic similarities between the MENAAP countries.

Pakistan now shares with MENA economies high demographic pressures, conflict exposure, labour market strains, and lack of private sector dynamism. Pakistan is currently negotiating a US$ 40 billion Country Partnership Framework with the World Bank for fiscal years 2026 through 2035, centred on human capital development, private sector growth, and economic resilience.

On June 16 ,2026, the Pentagon decided to drop “Indo” from the title of the Indo-Pacific Command (USINDOPACOM). Though described as  a housekeeping matter, the change revealed the effect of strategic realities and geopolitical shifts in US interests in the Indian and Pacific Ocean region.

Way back in 2018, in President Trump’s first term, Defence Secretary James Mattis announced that the “Pacific Command” would henceforth be known as the “US Indo-Pacific Command”, reflecting a profound shift in American strategic thinking. By inserting “Indo” into the title, Washington formally acknowledged that the Indian and Pacific Oceans were a single, interconnected strategic theatre, hosting trade routes, supply chains, naval deployments and geopolitical competition between the US and China.

The inclusion of “Indo” recognised India’s growing importance in American foreign policy and defence planning vis-à-vis the common rival, China. Both countries were opposed to China’s Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), which they said was a hegemonic instrument. The term Indo-Pacific also neatly fitted into another US-led grouping, QUAD, comprising the US, Japan, Australia and India with an aim to contain China’s influence.

In the course of time, the term “Indo” was dropped from the Pacific command’s name as India-US relations deteriorated after Trump’s weaponisation of sanctions and tariffs. Alienated India began to patch up with China. It needed  to build its industries with Chinese inputs and investment and also to bridge the yawning trade gap with China. India and China also started talks to manage the border conflict.

Meanwhile, there were murmurs in the US (as per an article by Dr.Ashley Tellis in “Foreign Affairs”) that India was not accommodative to the US even as it received assistance from the US.

Days before Trump’s 50% Trump tariff hit Indian exports on 27 August, the US President announced that his  close aide and MAGA loyalist, Sergio Gor, would be the next Ambassador to India cum Special Envoy for South and Central Asian Affairs. Indian diplomatic commentators made no secret of their uneasiness as concurrent accreditation was not considered normal where India and Pakistan were concerned.

When President Obama appointed Richard Holbrooke as Special envoy for the Afghan-Pakistan (Af-Pak) region, New Delhi objected to India being added to his brief. Holbrooke believed that a solution of the Afghanistan-Pakistan tension could lead to a settlement of the Kashmir issue also. But India put its foot down and ensured that it was kept out of Holbrooke’s brief.

Given the uneasiness in Indo-US relations now,  the Modi government could not oppose the “‘hyphenation” of India with Pakistan, Bangladesh and Afghanistan in Gor’s job description.

But US analyst Michael Kugelman speculated that the US and President Trump could be signalling the importance of ties with India by having the special envoy for two key regions posted in New Delhi, rather than in some other capital.
