Thema Collection opens Deewara, plans silent-safari retreat to ease Yala crowding

Thema Collection has opened Deewara, its newest property on the Mawella coast, and is advancing a silent-safari retreat near Yala designed to ease crowding at the wildlife park, alongside new resort concepts for Kalpitiya and Ella, the family-owned hotel group’s leadership revealed.

Speaking at a media gathering held in Colombo last Thursday, Thema Collection Founder and Chairman Chandra Wickramasinghe described Deewara as offering guests an entirely different experience on an untouched stretch of coastline beyond Tangalle. Acquired this year, the restored fisherman’s cottage draws its character from the island’s maritime heritage and features in the first episode of the relaunched Rendez-vous with Yasmin and Kumar, airing on 1 October. The Yala project addresses growing concern over congestion at the country’s most visited national park. Some 2,000 hotel rooms ring its main entrance, the chairman pointed out, and around 100 jeeps a day pour in through it, a scene he likened to entering a city.

By contrast, fewer than eight jeeps a day use a quieter entrance which the government has already invested in opening, but which remains underused for want of accommodation nearby.

Thema plans to fill that gap with tree-house accommodation and silent safaris in electric vehicles, with tightly limited entries, while rehabilitating the surrounding buffer land.

However, although the lease is now secured, the project must still obtain clearances from as many as 23 state institutions before construction can begin, alongside consultations with village societies and temple committees.

“They collect 23 institutions again and again. This is the problem,” he lamented, suggesting such approvals be completed within around six months, depending on the sensitivity of the land.

Even so, he affirmed the group’s resolve to see the project through, acknowledging that environmentally sensitive areas under strain from over-tourism, such as Yala, warranted careful scrutiny.

“In a way it’s good, you protect the environment before I even put a brick down there,” he added.

Meanwhile, in Kalpitiya, the group is shaping a beachfront concept that seeks to recast a destination long synonymous with kitesurfing and island excursions as one where travellers linger by the sea.

“No one is going there for five, six days just to stay on the beach,” Thema Collection Director of Marketing and Business Development Maxime Wickramasinghe noted.

In Ella, the group is crafting a resort inspired by the Ravana legend woven into the surrounding hills, drawing on temple architecture and traditional havelis, a theme Maxime Wickramasinghe stressed would be interpreted with due reverence. Both projects remain at the design stage.

The new projects will join a portfolio of 17 properties and 362 rooms stretching from the Cultural Triangle and the tea country to the east coast and down south, 14 of which the group owns. The chairman explained that the group favours long-term leases for new sites and is advancing its pipeline in step with financing conditions and construction costs. Sustainability initiatives are funded project by project, Maxime Wickramasinghe noted. The group has drawn on support from programmes such as the Market Development Facility, which assisted in quantifying its carbon emissions, though most initiatives are self-financed as many grant schemes target SMEs. The group traces its roots to Connaissance De Ceylan, the inbound travel agency the chairman co-founded in 1982, before venturing into hotels in 1994. According to Maxime Wickramasinghe, Maalu Maalu in Pasikudah, built after the end of the conflict, went on to define the group’s conceptual approach, and each property since has been conceived around a story of its own. That storytelling is now central to how the group markets itself.

“It’s more clips of storytelling, that’s how it sells now,” the chairman explained.

Amba Yaalu in Kandalama, Sri Lanka’s first hotel run entirely by women, and Wild Glamping Gal Oya, created with the Vedda community of Rathugala, both won Gold at the recent ICRT Indian Subcontinent Responsible Tourism Awards. Both now advance to the Global Responsible Tourism Awards in London in November. The group’s carbon emissions are independently verified by Control Union.

Turning to the wider industry, Maxime Wickramasinghe remarked that user-generated content and a younger generation of travellers were drawing guests beyond the classic circuit to places such as Sigiriya, Gal Oya and Wasgamuwa.

“They want to go and explore, they want experiences, they want different things,” he explained.

Sri Lankans account for over 30 percent of the group’s clientele, he revealed, followed by the UK, France and Australia. He credited a diverse marketing mix with lifting India from about 0.5 percent to around 6 percent of group occupancy.

The chairman identified air connectivity as key to the industry’s next phase of growth.

“We need more airline seats,” he emphasised, welcoming French Bee’s planned Paris, Malé and Colombo service from December.

He also urged the private sector to take the lead in promoting the island.

“We can’t wait until government or tourist board or somebody is going to market us, so we have to market it ourselves,” he asserted.
