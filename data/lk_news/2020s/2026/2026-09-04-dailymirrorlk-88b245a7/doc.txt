Banking: The Thief Within

The alleged fraudulent transfer of more than US$1 billion through banks in Sri Lanka should not be dismissed as merely another isolated case of fraud. It raises a far more disturbing question: where were the people and the systems entrusted with protecting the banks?

In my view, this is fundamentally a failure in the management of the human element. Fraud is not new to banking. It has happened in leading financial institutions around the world, and it will happen again wherever management becomes complacent, controls are weak, and personal trust replaces professional vigilance.

Banking is built on trust, but blind trust can be dangerous. In many institutions, long years of working together create a friendly atmosphere of shared meals, drinks and camaraderie. Familiarity can gradually weaken suspicion. A trusted colleague becomes “one of us,” and the normal safeguards and scrutiny expected in banking may slowly lose their sharpness.

History provides many painful lessons.  The lesson is clear: reputation is not a control mechanism. Friendship is not an audit. Trust is not a substitute for verification.The alleged transfer of such an enormous sum through Sri Lankan banks must therefore serve as a serious warning.

Banks must learn to trust their employees—but never to the extent of abandoning scrutiny. No employee, however senior, popular, respected or long-serving, should be beyond effective supervision.

If the lessons of banking history are ignored, the next scandal is not a question of whether it will happen, but when.
