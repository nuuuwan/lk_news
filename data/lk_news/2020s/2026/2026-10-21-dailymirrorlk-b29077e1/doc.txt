Public Investment and Idle Assets in Crisis Times

Sri Lanka has yet to recover from its long economic downturn. The central challenges facing the country are avenues to increase employment and livelihoods, and ensure income streams to meet the rising cost of living. Therefore, there is the need to increase sustainable production, and fair distribution of the returns on such production to the working people. Furthermore, given the drastic rise in wealth inequality in the country, there is also the need for redistribution of wealth. Public investment is one key instrument for the Government to begin addressing these economic challenges.

The past record of Sri Lanka’s public investment has not been encouraging. Mega infrastructure projects funded by high finance did not provide adequate returns and only increased the levels of debt to bilateral creditors, multi-lateral agencies and the commercial financiers. Indeed, Sri Lanka’s debt crisis and eventual default were in part the consequences of such external borrowing that did not translate into sustainable returns, much less foreign earnings, and were also channelled into luxurious consumption of imported goods.

In this context, what is needed is an alternative approach towards public investment that can multiply employment and livelihoods. In a time when resources are scarce, it is also critical that such investment stimulate idle assets into production.

Development finance in recent decades has become investments secured often at high interest costs that are supposed to contribute to economic growth. Once Sri Lanka was considered a middle income country, it is no longer qualified for concessionary loans from even the International Financial Institutions. Worse, the high cost capital has made the country prone to accumulating high interest debt. When such sustainable external financing is absent or too costly, domestic financing is considered an option. However, domestic financing also should be channelled towards investments that lead to increased production with income streams ensuring the welfare of the working people; otherwise domestic finance can also lead to dispossession of people’s assets.

About two and a half decades ago, the neoliberal Peruvian economist, Hernando de Soto, claimed tremendous amount wealth can be unleashed if the informal land owned by the working poor can be titled, so that they can use it as collateral for financing small businesses. His views captured the imagination of the global elite with the idea that there is tremendous amount of dead capital to become the source for economic development. Similarly in Sri Lanka, neoliberal economists have been calling for repealing the Paddy Lands Act so that farmers can use them including as collateral for loans. While it would be enticing for the farmers to have freeholding title over their land, using it as collateral for loans pose the danger of losing the land altogether.

The concern here is, finance or fictitious capital as Karl Marx called it, can do the very opposite that we expect of investment and lead to dispossession rather than accumulation linked to the economic wellbeing of the people. Indeed, dispossession has been the consequence of microfinance schemes, which policymakers for decades considered the avenue for uplifting marginalised sections of our society, whom the commercial banks consider credit unworthy.

In the Northern Province where I work, the post-war development initiatives with investment in infrastructure and the expansion of finance in the form of banks, finance companies and then later microfinance programs alerted us to not just the failure of that development vision but also dispossession of the war-torn people. We observed that many families that had managed to safeguard their gold jewellery – the emergency liquid assets of most low income households – end up losing them in the post-war years through pawning provided by banks.

It is in this broader context of the failure of post-war reconstruction and in fact dispossession that we sought the co-operative movement as an alternative. In August 2019, we formed the Northern Co-operative Development Bank (NCDB) – registered as the federation of all the co-operatives in the Northern Province – to provide affordable credit and other services necessary for sustainable production by co-operative industries and small-holder co-operative members.

One of the core initiatives of NCDB has been the activation of idle assets. There are numerous small factories, machinery, vehicles and farms, which had either survived the war or were even invested in after the war. These assets are sitting idle due to lack of planning, mobilisation of labour necessary for production and small investments necessary for their reactivation. We have found that supporting the local level co-operatives with the small investments along with technological and marketing support can activate assets over ten times the value of the small investments made by the federation. Furthermore, with local industrial production, a demand pull is created for local livelihoods.

Over last few years, a number of ventures such as rice and rice flour mills, small dairy processing plants, and coconut oil mills have been activated. Furthermore, numerous vehicles and machines sitting idle have been repaired and redeployed, sometimes by renting them at concessionary rates to other co-operatives that can effectively utilise them. An interesting development last year, of the idea of addressing idle assets, was the activation of the state-owned Cey-Nor boat yard, a multi-billion rupee asset in Karainagar, which is now supplying fibre glass boats to local fisher-folk. In fact, some of the first orders for the boat yard came from fisheries co-operative societies.

This approach, however, is very different from what Hernando de Soto and, for that matter, even our local officials have in mind when they claim the private sector can activate such idle assets or dead capital. When such assets are approached with a finance mind-set, it often does not lead to production. Rather, it can lead to “asset stripping” with corporate firms taking over an undervalued asset and selling it off part by part to make a profit. The consequence is that the idle asset does not lead to production nor increased employment. In fact, I am aware of a few examples of idle co-operative assets including a dairy plant and a rice mill, which local officials pushed to be leased to private firms, which have now been returned in disrepair. At the national level, this is also what we have seen for example with the plantation sector; where since privatisation in the 1990s, there has been little replanting, as the companies have just been extracting resulting also in declining employment in the sector.

There are a few key lessons for Sri Lanka trying to emerge out of the long downturn from the local level experience in the North. First, Sri Lanka’s capacity utilisation including of industries has declined from 81 percent in 2019 to 65 percent in 2025, and a low hanging fruit would be to provide the support through public investment to increase production and hence employment. In this context, a focused revival of idle industries including SOEs, for example, linked to our public distribution system would be timely given the food crisis that we are also likely to face.

Second, we have become very comfortable with taking any project from a development agency and investing the funds without considering its sustainability or for that matter employment generation. As the country emerges out of a long crisis, we need to consider the balance of payments constraints and further ensure whether investments from external actors will not just provide adequate returns to meet the high interest costs, but also generate foreign exchange earnings necessary to repay foreign loans. We have gotten comfortable with borrowing in dollars and investing in roads and buildings that could be done with domestic finances. Sustainable investments whether from external or domestic financing requires industrial policies and the formation of a public development bank to ensure funds are channelled into productive investments; including the import of machinery if it is with external funds to ensure investments contribute to exports and foreign earning to repay foreign loans.

Third, public investments with a focus on production that multiplies employment needs to be the focus. For example, housing development can lead to increased employment that in turn puts money in the hands of working people, who are also involved in other rural livelihoods such as agriculture, leading to stabilisation of the farms that their households own. Government expenditure in subsidies such as the national school meal program should link small farmers and fisher folk to demand for food and hence stimulate local livelihoods.

Sri Lanka’s economic policies should focus on what resources are available and how to multiply them to serve the needs of the working people, who continue to meet the brunt of the long economic depression. We need a radical rethinking of our development approach and priorities for public investment if we are to wade through the crisis prone times ahead.
