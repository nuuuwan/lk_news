Over the past three years, ever since Russia invaded Ukraine, a third world war has been predicted. Accompanying this is another dire forecast, that a worldwide economic depression along the lines of the Great Depression of 1929 would happen.

The Russian invasion of Ukraine began four years and nine months ago. World War II lasted six years and a day. It was ended by two nuclear bombs. Historians argue that Japan could have been defeated without the use of nuclear bombs. However, the war may have dragged on for months more if nuclear bombs were not used.

Millions of Americans lost their job in the Great Depression of 1929 (photo credit Delhi Mind Clinic)

Fears and predictions that a the Russia-Ukraine conflict would escalate into a world war with the use of nuclear weapons have so far been unfounded, though both the US and key NATO allies such as Germany and Sweden provided arms including tanks, missiles and aircraft to Ukraine and continue their military aid.

This war is now stale news, though it’s still being waged ferociously. Casualty figures have increased significantly over the past year, and Russian casualties alone since the war began in 2022 now amount to 1.6 million according to estimates.

The Israeli invasion of Gaza and the US-Iran war replaced Ukraine as prime news, and raised new fears of a world war and economic crash. Now, with a dramatic change in the American political landscape with US midterm elections, one finds a de-escalation of the conflict, i.e. a cessation of major US attacks though minor attacks on Iranian targets continue. But that war, too, is far from over.

For this to happen, the US economy has to crash. This is what happened in 1929. As of October 2026, the direct military cost of the U.S. war with Iran has reached US$47.1 billion according to AP News and Pentagon figures reported via The New York Times.

Direct military expenditures are estimated at approximately $47.1 billion as of mid-to-late October 2026, growing at a rate of roughly $2 billion to $3 billion per month. If that $2-3 billion is added to Sri Lanka’s economy, (which has a nominal GDP of roughly $110.68 billion), that would go a long way to stabilise it. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) Extended Fund Facility (EFF) amounts to just $2.9 billion, and the entire population is paying dearly for this ‘largesse.’

The Soviet Union collapsed due to excessive military spending coupled with a consumer economy too small to sustain it. At the time of its collapse in 1991, by various estimates the Soviet economy was roughly the second-largest in the world after the United States, with a GNP that hovered around 50% to 52% of the U.S. level in the 1980s,  with per capita GNP at roughly 35% to 38% of the U.S. level.

But these figures don’t tell the whole story. While American-made consumer goods had large export markets, the Soviet consumer products were largely for the domestic market and Eastern Europe (China, learning from this, did not repeat that mistake when it liberalised its economy).

Between 1989 and 1992, Soviet GDP per capita fell by approximately 40% as the command system fractured.  By 1991, the consolidated budget deficit of the USSR and the Russian Federation soared to about 31% of GDP, and the country dissolved with an external debt of roughly $66 billion alongside minimal foreign exchange and gold reserves.

Also, the  Soviet Union did not recover much of the credit owed to it from Cold War military sales mainly to Middle Eastern countries. Following the USSR’s collapse in 1991, the Russian Federation inherited these foreign claims and eventually forgave or wrote off tens of billions of dollars in bad debt in exchange for new commercial contracts, geopolitical influence, or partial settlements.

Despite its current, massive military spending, the US economy is in better shape. But it has a huge budget deficit.  For fiscal year ending  September 30, 2026, the U.S. federal budget deficit totalled $1.99 trillion (widely rounded to $2.0 trillion), representing 6.2% of GDP.

Does this mean another US economic crash along the lines of 1929? Prophesy is a dangerous thing. All we can do is to examine why it happened, in peace time, eleven years after World War I ended. One doesn’t need to be an economist to figure out that almost all major financial crises of the 20th and 21st centuries took place when no powerful nations were at war with each other.

Throughout the 1920s till the crash, the US economy prospered. Under three Republican presidents – Harding, Calvin Coolidge, and Herbert C. Hoover, the economy boomed, though with widening inequalities. Then in October 1929 came the Wall Street Crash, resulting in the Great Depression which affected not just the US but the rest of the world, too.

In November 1932, Democrat Franklin D. Roosevelt was elected president, and he worked energetically to stabilise the situation. While the Republican presidents had allowed private enterprise a free hand during the boom years, curbing government regulation, Roosevelt brought back government supervision.

What is notable is that World War I paved the way to a great expansion of American industry and the US was able to overtake her industrial rivals Germany and Britain. US consumer industries grew rapidly. In 1918, there were seven million cars in the US. By 1929, there were 24 million.

To protect American industries, Republican presidents introduced heavy tariffs on imports. This is a familiar tale, from Ronald Regan, another Republican, introducing heavy tariffs on Japanese products to Donald Trump doing the same today not only on giant rivals such as China but also on small, vulnerable countries like Sri Lanka.

None of this would directly cause an economic crash. High oil prices cause domestic dissatisfaction and hardships in the US, but  not even the massive oil price hike of 1973, when oil prices quadrupled,  did not lead to a worldwide economic crash. It caused a sharp global recession rather than a permanent worldwide depression, accompanied by a rare economic phenomenon known as stagflation (high inflation combined with high unemployment and sluggish growth).

So, it may be that we could be spared the worst, from worldwide economic depression to a devastating third world war. But, as noted earlier, prophesy is dangerous.
