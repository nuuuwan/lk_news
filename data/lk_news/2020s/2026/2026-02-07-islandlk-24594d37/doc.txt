Wetlands:Our Silent Lifelines

Senior Professor, Department of Zoology and Environmental Sciences, University of ColomboChair, Climate Change Sub-committee, Wildlife and Nature Protection Society of Sri Lanka

On a quiet morning, when mist lifts gently over paddy fields and birds begin their songs, few of us pause to think about the water beneath our feet. Yet that water, so easily taken for granted, often comes from wetlands—marshes, village tanks, rivers and floodplains that quietly sustain everyday life. They feed our fields, protect our homes, support wildlife, and shape the cultures we inherit.

This article is part of a month-long public awareness campaign led by the Wildlife and Nature Protection Society of Sri Lanka (WNPS) throughout February. The initiative aims to educate citizens on the critical importance of wetlands and includes island-wide school outreach programmes, guided field visits to wetland sites, and a short video competition supported by Seylan Bank to encourage young people to creatively express why wetlands matter.

Every year on February 2, the world observes World Wetlands Day, marking the adoption of the Ramsar Convention on Wetlands in 1971. The 2026 theme, “Wetlands and traditional knowledge: Celebrating cultural heritage,” reminds us that wetlands are not merely ecosystems defined by science. They are living landscapes shaped by centuries of human knowledge, care, and coexistence.

Across the world, communities have learned to live with wetlands rather than against them—through floodplain farming, mangrove stewardship, indigenous fishing practices, and shared water-use traditions. Refined over generations, these practices have protected wetlands while sustaining livelihoods.

Wetlands regulate water flows, reduce floods and droughts, store carbon, purify water, and support biodiversity. Although they cover only about six percent of the Earth’s land surface, they support nearly 40% of global plant and animal species, making them among the most productive ecosystems on the planet.

Yet the global picture is alarming. Wetlands are disappearing up to three times faster than forests, driven by land conversion, pollution, and climate change. Nearly 90% of the world’s wetlands have been degraded since the 1700s. With them, we lose natural flood protection, clean water, food security, and resilience to climate extremes.

Wetlands are more than statistics. They are places of memory, identity, and belonging. Recognising this connection is essential if conservation efforts are to succeed.

For Sri Lanka, this theme is not new—it reflects a way of life practised for centuries. Between about 200 BC and 1200 AD, the island developed a remarkable water-based civilisation. In the dry zone, water scarcity was overcome through an intricate network of village tanks, feeder canals, and cascading reservoirs.

This was more than a technical achievement. It was an eco-civilisation in which environmental stewardship, social organisation, and spiritual values were deeply interconnected. Water was treated not merely as a resource, but as a life-giving force deserving care and respect.

Ancient villages were carefully planned around integrated systems of tanks, wetlands, streams, forests, and fields, where each element supported the others. Rules governing water sharing and tank maintenance were upheld through strong community responsibility. Wetlands played a central role—absorbing excess water during heavy rains and releasing it gradually in dry periods, ensuring year-round agricultural stability.

Wetlands at work – filtering water, supporting livelihoods and sustaining ecosystems far beyond their boundaries

Long before the term “wise use of wetlands” entered international policy, Sri Lanka was already practising it.

Modern science now confirms what traditional knowledge always understood: wetlands are indispensable for sustainable development. They filter pollutants, recharge groundwater, sustain fisheries, and store carbon. In urban areas they function as natural infrastructure, absorbing storm water, cooling cities, and improving air and water quality.

Yet many of Sri Lanka’s wetlands are under threat from land filling, pollution and unplanned development. When wetlands are lost, floods worsen, droughts intensify, water quality declines and biodiversity disappears.

The ongoing WNPS campaign this February seeks to reconnect communities—especially young people—with these realities. Through school programmes and field visits, students are being encouraged to experience wetlands first-hand and understand the services they quietly provide every day.

Protecting wetlands is not a foreign idea for Sri Lanka. It is a return to deeply rooted traditions of respecting water and nature.
