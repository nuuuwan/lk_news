To foster sustainable economic development, national security, democratic accountability, and investor confidence should not be viewed as competing interests; they are essential pillars for building a prosperous future.

Sri Lanka finds itself at a crucial juncture in its journey towards economic stability and democratic resilience. After enduring a severe economic crisis, sovereign debt default, political turbulence, and a period of public uncertainty, the country now focuses on restoring international trust, attracting foreign direct investment (FDI), generating employment opportunities, and securing a viable economic future for younger generations.

In light of this context, the proposed Protection of the State from Terrorism Bill (PSTA), aimed at replacing the Prevention of Terrorism Act (PTA), as well as the Prevention of Organised Crimes Bill (POCB), must be examined with due diligence.

The primary inquiry here is not whether Sri Lanka requires effective legislation to address terrorism and organised crime—clearly, it does. A responsible Government has an obligation to safeguard national security, maintain public order, and ensure the safety of its citizens. The more pressing concern, however, lies in determining whether this responsibility should be managed through legislation that may inadvertently consolidate significant power within the Executive, potentially impacting individual liberties, judicial independence, and democratic accountability.

For a developing economy seeking to attract international investment, this matter transcends mere constitutional debate; it encompasses critical economic considerations as well.

While this determination warrants respect, it is vital to contextualise it within the broader constitutional framework. A judicial ruling on constitutional validity does not inherently validate that legislation aligns with sound public policy, meets international human rights standards, or possesses democratic legitimacy.

Sri Lanka’s constitutional history offers pertinent lessons. The 18th Amendment, while receiving judicial approval, was later met with criticism for centralising presidential power and eroding institutional checks and balances. Its principal components were subsequently reversed by the 19th Amendment, which then led to the reinstatement of significant executive powers through the 20th Amendment, and further curtailments in the 21st Amendment.

These experiences clearly demonstrate that constitutional permissibility and effective governance are not synonymous.

The responsibility for enacting sound legislation ultimately lies with Parliament, which must weigh not only what the Constitution permits but also what is necessary for the nation, its citizens, and its democratic institutions.

The Bar Association of Sri Lanka (BASL), led by President Rajeev Amarasuriya, has expressed serious reservations about both proposed Bills. These objections are not rooted in a general opposition to national security legislation but rather focus on the extent of executive authority, the potential weakening of judicial oversight, and the potential implications for fundamental freedoms.

The BASL has noted that the gazetted PSTA varies significantly from the earlier consultative draft, specifically regarding aspects of detention, bail, and judicial supervision. They have called for a re-evaluation of the Bill in its current form, publication of the committee report and dissenting views, and a thorough explanation from the Government regarding the changes introduced.

Concerns regarding the POCB are equally profound, particularly regarding its treatment of offenses connected to organised criminal groups, which may broaden criminal liability markedly. Criticism has also been levelled at the admissibility of certain confessions obtained by senior police officers and the constraints placed on judicial discretion concerning bail and remand.

The Human Rights Commission of Sri Lanka has independently recommended that the POCB be withdrawn and substantially revised, highlighting concerns about its compatibility with constitutional protections and international human rights obligations.

These concerns reflect significant institutional objections that deserve thoughtful consideration and reasoned responses rather than political dismissal.

United Nations human rights experts, in a statement from November 9, 2026, commended the Government’s intention to repeal the PTA and acknowledged that the latest PSTA draft represents a notable improvement over previous versions. Nonetheless, they underscored the need for additional safeguards, including effective judicial oversight over detention, robust protections against torture, safeguarding journalists’ rights and their sources, as well as ensuring accountability for officials who violate the law.

Organisations such as Amnesty International have also raised concerns regarding the extensive nature of counterterrorism powers and their potential effects on freedoms of expression, association, and peaceful dissent.

Additionally, the European Union’s GSP+ framework is a crucial consideration, as Sri Lanka’s preferential access to this market is contingent upon upholding international conventions, including commitments related to human rights. Noncompliance with these obligations could have repercussions extending far beyond the legal and political realms.

Thus, the Government should view international scrutiny not as an infringement on national sovereignty but as an opportunity to strengthen it. Observance of voluntarily undertaken international obligations is a hallmark of responsible governance.

One of the most critical yet often overlooked dimensions of this discourse is its economic significance.

Sri Lanka urgently requires sustainable FDI to enhance its productive capabilities, diversify its exports, facilitate technology transfer, generate employment opportunities, and improve foreign exchange earnings.

The Board of Investment has reported that FDI inflows reached approximately $ 1057 billion in 2025, signifying a 72% increase from the previous year. While this trend is encouraging, it should not obscure the substantial gap that remains between current investment levels and the ambitious development objectives of the country.

Foreign investors weigh their decisions on multiple factors beyond tax incentives, labor costs, geographical advantages, or political assurances. They seek predictability in legislation, consistency in taxation, transparency in procurement processes, reliable enforcement of contracts, independence of regulatory institutions, and access to effective legal remedies.

The U.S. Department of State and the U.S. International Trade Administration have identified challenges facing Sri Lanka, including regulatory unpredictability, bureaucratic inefficiencies, inconsistent policy implementation, and uncertainty surrounding investment approvals.

These challenges are particularly significant for investments in renewable energy, infrastructure, manufacturing, tourism, logistics, and technology, where substantial capital commitments are often made over extended periods.

A potential investor contemplating a long-term commitment requires assurance that commercial disputes will be resolved impartially, that regulations will not shift arbitrarily, and that public authorities will exercise their powers within clear legal frameworks. The rule of law is not merely an abstract concept; it represents an essential economic asset.

Indeed, the principles that protect citizens from arbitrary detention simultaneously safeguard business owners from arbitrary administrative actions. Institutional accountability ultimately benefits all stakeholders.

The principle of separation of powers, articulated by Montesquieu in his 1748 work The Spirit of the Laws, underscores a fundamental tenet: liberty is jeopardised when legislative, executive, and judicial powers are concentrated in a single entity.

The legislature creates laws, the executive enforces them, and the judiciary independently interprets and applies them. Each of these branches must operate within its constitutional boundaries while maintaining checks and balances to prevent arbitrary governance.

This is especially pertinent in the context of national security legislation, which inherently grants the State considerable authority over individual liberties. Consequently, independent judicial supervision is paramount.

Though a Government may hold a democratic mandate with a substantial parliamentary majority, that authority does not translate into unchecked power to diminish constitutional safeguards. Democracy encompasses not only majority rule but also protection for minority rights, respect for dissent, institutional independence, and equal treatment under the law.

Sri Lanka has faced considerable challenges due to the previous misuse of extraordinary State powers. The PTA, initially introduced in 1979 as temporary legislation, became entrenched in the legal framework for decades. Its legacy illustrates how powers enacted under the guise of national security can extend beyond their original intent.

This ongoing discourse around the proposed Bills is rooted in real concerns, not theoretical apprehensions. Legal commentator Kishali Pinto Jayawardene highlights significant issues regarding the management of confidential information and the protection of whistleblowers. She argues that broadly defined national security restrictions may deter journalists, public-interest investigators, and whistleblowers from carrying out their vital roles in serving the public interest.

This consideration warrants serious attention. Accountability hinges on the capacity to question public authorities, expose misconduct, and scrutinise decisions made in the name of national security.

A nation cannot cultivate a credible investment environment while simultaneously fostering uncertainty around the legitimate exercise of freedom of expression and the right to access information.

The Government should approach calls for re-evaluation not as a challenge to its development objectives or to national security but as an opportunity to embrace a more sophisticated and inclusive governance approach.

First, the PSTA and POCB should undergo thorough consultations with the BASL, the Human Rights Commission, civil society, independent legal experts, and relevant international bodies.

Second, the Government should articulate the rationale behind deviations from earlier consultative drafts and clarify how the proposed provisions satisfy both constitutional and international human rights standards.

Third, powers related to detention, bail restrictions, surveillance, and investigative procedures should be paired with effective judicial oversight, independent reviews, and accessible remedies to prevent abuse.

Fourth, the legislation should ensure sufficient protections for journalists, whistleblowers, lawyers, researchers, and individuals engaged in lawful civic activities.

Lastly, any organised crime legislation must be carefully crafted to distinguish genuine criminal organisations from legitimate trade unions, professional associations, peaceful demonstrations, and ordinary collective activities.

There exists no inherent conflict in maintaining robust national security legislation while also respecting these essential safeguards.

For Sri Lanka’s young entrepreneurs, professionals, lawyers, academics, and future leaders, this dialogue holds significant relevance. They are the generation tasked with establishing businesses, attracting investments, generating employment, advancing technology, and rejuvenating a competitive economy.

They require a nation that enables the free expression of ideas, where commercial agreements are honoured, where public institutions are held accountable, and where the law is applied fairly to all.

A thriving economy cannot flourish amid uncertainty, just as a democratic society cannot thrive in an atmosphere of fear.

The Government has a unique opportunity to demonstrate that its commitment to economic recovery is paralleled by a strong dedication to democratic principles.

Investors seek not only a favourable investment climate but also a country characterised by predictability, credibility, and accountability in governance.

Moreover, it is crucial to remember that national security exists to protect the citizens of the State, not to make them vulnerable to the State’s authority.

Ultimately, the true measure of a nation’s strength lies not in the extent of its Government’s power over its people but in how judiciously that power is wielded within the confines of the law.

Sri Lanka deserves security, investment, economic development, and political stability, but these cannot be sustained at the expense of liberty, justice, and public trust.

The Government should listen to the concerns raised by the legal community, independent institutions, international partners, and, most importantly, its citizens. Withdrawing and thoughtfully redrafting these Bills would not signify a retreat from governance; rather, it would showcase the maturity and confidence expected from a Government entrusted with the responsibility of nation-building.

The real test of leadership lies not merely in the ability to pass legislation with a parliamentary majority but in the capacity to enact laws that inspire trust and confidence among the populace.
