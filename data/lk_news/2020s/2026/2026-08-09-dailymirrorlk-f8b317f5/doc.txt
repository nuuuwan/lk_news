Stop letting the rain go to waste

Sri Lanka is once again caught between two extremes. In one part of the year, families queue for water bowsers, farmers watch their crops fail, and entire provinces struggle through months of drought. Then, almost without warning, the skies open. Within hours, roads disappear, homes are flooded, and lives are lost.

Just days ago, heavy rains triggered floods and landslides that killed people and forced thousands to evacuate in the central highlands. Nearly 3,500 people were displaced and hundreds of homes damaged in a matter of hours. That devastation came on the heels of last year’s catastrophic Cyclone Ditwah. And yet, every time the rain comes, we react as if it is unexpected. The deeper truth is uncomfortable. Sri Lanka does not have a water shortage. It has a water management crisis.

We are a country that receives significant rainfall, particularly in the wet zone, where annual averages exceed 2,500 millimetres. But that rain is uneven. Large parts of the dry zone receive far less, sometimes as low as 600 millimetres a year, concentrated over just a few months. The rest of the year is dry, harsh, and unforgiving.

Experts are warning that the ongoing El Niño pattern will disrupt rainfall even further. Forecasts already suggest below average rainfall through key months, with hotter conditions accelerating reservoir depletion. In simple terms, Sri Lanka will become drier when it cannot afford to be, and wetter when it is least prepared. Walk through Colombo after a heavy downpour. Drains overflow within minutes. Streets flood. Traffic collapses. In a matter of hours, millions of litres of freshwater are lost. The same water that could have been stored, filtered, and reused during drought months disappears into the ocean.

This is not just inefficient. It is irresponsible. Rainwater harvesting is not a new or complex idea. It is one this country was built on. Ancient kings understood that survival depended on capturing water when it was available. Today, we have replaced that wisdom with short-term thinking. There is no serious national push to integrate rainwater harvesting into urban planning. Instead, we continue to spend billions reacting to disasters after they happen.

The Ministry of Urban Development, local councils, and provincial authorities must take responsibility here. Drainage systems in Colombo and other urban centres remain outdated and poorly maintained. Flood mitigation cannot continue to be treated as a seasonal emergency response. It must become a year-round strategy.

At the same time, the Ministry of Water Supply and the Ministry of Agriculture cannot operate in silos. Farmers in drought-prone districts like Anuradhapura, Polonnaruwa, and Moneragala continue to depend on unpredictable rainfall. This disconnect is costing the country dearly, not just in economic terms, but in human suffering.

Rainwater harvesting systems can be introduced at multiple levels. At the household level, rooftop collection systems can store thousands of litres of water during a single rainy period. At the community level, storage tanks and small reservoirs can support agriculture during dry months. At the urban level, large-scale stormwater management systems can capture and redirect excess water instead of allowing it to flood streets.

But all of this requires political will. There are global firms and development agencies that specialise in sustainable water management. Public-private partnerships could be introduced to build large-scale rainwater harvesting systems, modernise drainage networks, and introduce smart water storage technologies. These are not just environmental projects. They are economic investments that will save billions in disaster recovery costs over time.

However, policy and investment alone are not enough. Public awareness is just as critical. Water conservation must become part of everyday thinking. Because the reality is this. Sri Lanka’s climate is changing faster than its policies. The rain will come. That is certain. What is uncertain is whether we will finally learn to use it wisely.

If we continue to waste it, we will keep swinging between drought and disaster. Sri Lanka does not need more rain.
