The Majority’s Moon

Sharni Jayawardena’s film ‘Just a Phase’ – on the Holidays Act, No. 17 of 1965 – treats the forgotten statute as what it always was: a quarrel, waged through politics, nationalism, and religiosity, over who gets to define the nation’s sense of time, and on what basis. The Act did two stark things. It stripped Sunday of its inherited legal status, and made all four Poya Days each month (not just the full moon day) both a public and bank holiday – a regime that came into force on January 1, 1966. Beyond administrative tidying, this was the state reordering public life around a vaulting Sinhala-Buddhist nationalism’s underlying faith-based rhythm, which the film grasps from its first frames.

The craft on display is considerable, though anyone who has followed Jayawardena’s earlier work would expect no less. The photography, and location work move between intimate interiors, and the framing of human subjects to more open landscapes that hold the country’s uneasy inheritance in a single frame. The archival research is patient, multidisciplinary, and beautifully presented: newspaper clippings in Sinhala, Tamil and English, ephemera like the Elephant Beedi calendar, legal gazettes, parliamentary debates and oral testimony stitched together with a care that speaks to months, perhaps years, of work.

The film surfaces Dharmapala’s 1898 daily code advising women to treat sil not as burden but as regular practice, which is a small detail saying a great deal about the gendered moral architecture the Act was quietly importing. Sound mixing rarely gets the credit it deserves in documentary, and the immersive mix here is exceptional – I watched with headphones, which certainly helped. The background music, and sound effects, field recordings, archival clips, and the voices of those who lived through the period sit in a balance that rarely draws attention to itself. That restraint is the craft.

On the politics, the film doesn’t hedge. Its reading of Dudley Senanayake’s 1965 coalition as an accommodation with a resurgent Buddhist lobby holds up against the country’s vexed, and violent political history. The parliamentary voices quoted bear witness to the country’s divisive contemporaneous politics. A senator calls the bill indefensible and hypocritical, and an attempt to drag the country back to medieval times under the guise of restoration. A Muslim politician worries, practically, and with some prescience, about what happens when a Poya falls on Eid. The inclusion of this multi-ethnic, and multi-religious texture exposes the Act’s claim to embody an indigenous national rhythm as precisely what it was – a rhythm for some, and an imposition on the rest. Muslims, and Catholics appear not as abstract minorities whose lives needed rearrangement, but as participants (and citizens) in a richer civic fabric the Act actively thinned. A Catholic priest recalling Vatican II (The Second Ecumenical Council of the Vatican) arriving almost simultaneously with the lunar calendar’s introduction – leaving the Church internally equipped to adapt while state-imposed time still disrupted family and school life – shows how carefully Jayawardena has listened beyond the majority faith community. Likewise, a Muslim remembering Friday prayers on the so-called P1 and P2 days, the private sector forced to mirror international time while the state slept (a point woven into the film by featuring the renowned sci-fi writer Arthur C Clarke), the uncertainty of a P6 week are all fragments that accumulate into an argument the film never needs to more explicitly frame or state. The social, religious and familial practices of Sri Lanka’s communities constituted a genuine multi-ethnic and multi-religious richness that the Act diminished, and sought to erase. Witnesses who lived through those years, and those who grew up on the stories of parents who did, convey this almost without meaning to.

One insight the film surfaces, and which deserves more weight than it gets, is that the popular practice of Buddhism during this period carried on largely independent of its political instrumentalisation. The lunar calendar was the creation of populist politicians, and a narrow clerical establishment pressing electoral advantage towards majoritarian rule. Sil, and everyday observance belonged to a different register, one that did not need a statute to function. This gap between lived faith, and politicised (or weaponised) faith is the quiet centre of the film, and where it most clearly speaks to a policymaking pathology that still bedevils the country, and how! The primacy of Buddhism in the 1972 constitution, the majoritarian reflexes that shape public holidays, language policy, everyday exchanges, and electoral rhetoric alike belongs to the trajectory the 1965 Act set, and its repeal did not fully end.

I would have loved to hear, and see dissenting voices from within the Buddhist clergy to the Act – or, from the perspective of 2026, the idea of the Act. The film hints, without quite saying so, that not every monk agreed with the Act or its outcomes. Clergy who offered critical reflection at the time, or who could now speak to its failure – a failure Sirimavo Bandaranaike herself acknowledged in Parliament in 1971 – are missing from the frame. Their inclusion would have complicated the story usefully and resisted the impression that the sangha spoke with one voice. There is a single subject towards the end of the film who admits to knowing nothing of this period, and I wished Jayawardena pursued this ignorance more.

There are surely so many others. What do younger Sri Lankans, encountering this history now for the first time, make of a state that once tried to legislate the weekend, and by extension, the work week, around the moon’s phases? That conversation would have pulled the past into the present, where it belongs.

The Act was essentially statecraft informed by, and seeking to influence labour, ritual and an electoral arithmetic. The film is strongest when it remembers this, as it does when it returns to the 1971 repeal. The Holidays Act, No. 29 passed that year retained only the Full Moon Poya Day alongside Sunday, and the muted public response suggests little popular attachment to the earlier arrangement. What stays after watching ‘Just a Phase’ is the film’s account of the damage done when a state mistakes symbolic majoritarianism for shared national meaning, and social cohesion.

Jayawardena has taken a statute most Sri Lankans have never heard of, and turned it into a story about home, identity, belonging, school, mosque, church, office and temple. It is a serious piece of filmmaking on a subject most have allowed to slip out of view. The scholarship on the Act is thin enough that Jayawardena’s film will now be one of the first places people go to understand what the state once tried to do with time, and why it failed. It’s a remarkable, rare, rewarding contribution to a fuller historical record.
