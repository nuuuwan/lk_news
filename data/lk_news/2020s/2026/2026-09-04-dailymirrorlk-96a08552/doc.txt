El Nino: what is in store?

El Niño has once again begun to figure prominently in international weather and climate discussions. For Sri Lanka, however, it should not be treated merely as another international weather phenomenon. It could have direct consequences for agriculture, water availability, electricity generation, food prices and the livelihoods of thousands of people.

According to forecasts, El Niño conditions are already present and are expected to strengthen in the coming months. The World Meteorological Organisation has indicated an exceptionally high likelihood that El Niño will persist through February 2027. The important point is that preparedness should start before the effects become serious. El Nino  effects on Sri Lanka may not necessarily be uniform. Changes in rainfall patterns can affect different regions in different ways. Some areas could experience prolonged dry conditions, while others could face unusual rainfall. Therefore, it would be unwise to assume that El Niño will automatically result in a nationwide drought. But the uncertainty itself makes preparedness more important.

Agriculture should be one of the first areas to receive attention.

A prolonged dry spell can have serious consequences for paddy and other food crops. Farmers who depend heavily on rainfall are particularly vulnerable. A reduction in agricultural production could eventually translate into higher food prices. The Government should therefore ensure that farmers receive timely information on expected weather conditions. Agricultural authorities should advise farmers on planting schedules, crop selection, irrigation and water conservation. Where necessary, contingency arrangements should be made for areas likely to face water shortages.

Livestock farming also deserves attention. A prolonged dry spell can reduce the availability of grass and drinking water, affecting milk production. Water management will be equally important. Sri Lanka’s reservoirs have to serve several competing purposes. Water is required for agriculture, drinking purposes and electricity generation. During a prolonged dry period, these competing demands can become a serious problem.

This is why decisions on the management of reservoirs cannot be postponed until water levels have already fallen dangerously low. Authorities should assess water availability in advance and establish priorities for different sectors.

Hydropower remains an important part of Sri Lanka’s electricity generation system. A prolonged reduction in rainfall could therefore reduce hydropower generation and increase dependence on thermal power. That would have implications not only for electricity supply but also for the country’s fuel import bill. The Government should consequently assess possible hydropower shortfalls and make contingency plans  in advance. The rapid expansion of solar and other renewable energy sources provides Sri Lanka with an opportunity to reduce this vulnerability.  The lesson is that climate preparedness should not be confined to the Disaster Management Centre or the Meteorological Department. It requires coordination among the agencies responsible for agriculture, irrigation, electricity, water supply, health, transport and finance.

There is also a need to improve the country’s early-warning mechanisms. Warnings are useful only when they reach the people who need them in a form they can understand and act upon. A forecast issued by a government department is not enough. Farmers, fishermen, local authorities and communities should receive timely and practical information about forecasts.  The Government should also avoid creating unnecessary alarm. El Niño is a natural climate phenomenon, and its effects vary from one region to another. Not every forecast of a strong El Niño will result in a major crisis in Sri Lanka. Therefore, the response should be based on scientific evidence  rather than sensational predictions. At the same time, uncertainty should not become an excuse for doing nothing.

Sri Lanka’s recent economic crisis has demonstrated the cost of being unprepared for shocks. The country has limited fiscal space to respond to emergencies through expensive imports or large-scale relief programmes. Prevention and early planning are therefore likely to be far cheaper than crisis management later. There is another important dimension to this issue. Climate change is making extreme weather events and climatic uncertainties an increasingly important policy concern. El Niño itself is not caused by climate change, but its interaction with a warming climate can complicate the effects of weather events. Sri Lanka must consequently move towards a more systematic approach to climate-risk management.
