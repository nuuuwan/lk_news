Sri Lanka risks losing export ground to rivals: ADB cautions

Global value chain trade accounted for 31% of Sri Lanka’s gross trade in 2024, against 63% in Vietnam

Says exports, investment and productivity must drive next phase of recovery

Asserts wider market access must be matched by reforms to cut trade costs and improve the investment climate

Sri Lanka risks losing ground in key export markets as competitors secure preferential access through trade deals, Asian Development Bank (ADB) warned yesterday, calling for deeper trade integration and domestic reforms to help local firms compete.

ADB Country Director for Sri Lanka Shannon Cowlin, while citing the India-European Union free trade negotiations as an example of how other countries are moving to secure market access, noted Sri Lankan exporters also face higher requirements on quality, traceability and sustainability.

She shared her views while addressing the Serendipity Knowledge Program forum on ‘From Trade Reform to Export Growth: Enabling Sri Lankan Firms to Compete Globally.’

“And as competitors gain preferential access to key markets, Sri Lankan exporters risk losing ground unless the country pursues deeper trade integration and improved competitiveness,” Cowlin said.

The gap with Vietnam illustrates the challenge. Global value chain trade accounted for approximately 31 percent of Sri Lanka’s gross trade in 2024, compared with 63 percent in Vietnam. Cowlin said Sri Lanka’s export performance had lagged that of regional peers for decades, with merchandise exports concentrated in a few traditional sectors and foreign direct investment remaining low.

High import protection, policy uncertainty and regulatory and institutional constraints had discouraged export diversification, she said.

“But macroeconomic stability, while important, is not sufficient to deliver sustained growth and prosperity,” Cowlin said.

She said exports, investment and productivity must increasingly drive the next phase of the recovery. For a country with significant external debt, exports earn foreign exchange without adding to the debt burden, strengthen external buffers and support productive employment.

Sri Lanka’s efforts to join the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) could open further opportunities in the region, Cowlin said. Turning that access into export growth would require domestic reforms to lower trade costs, improve the investment climate and help firms innovate, diversify and raise productivity.

The National Export Development Plan for 2026–2030, launched earlier this year with ADB support, provided a framework for diversifying exports. She said the value of the initiatives would ultimately be measured by whether they delivered tangible gains in exports, investment and productivity.
