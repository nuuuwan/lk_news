The NPP and economic democracy

Needless to say, none of these hapless parents and children are from affluent families

The brutal assault and abandonment of a four-year-old girl in Wattegama by her mother last week is part of a broader, recurring pattern of abuse rather than an isolated incident.

According to reports, the mother of the child along with her paramour had brutally assaulted the girl at home, inflicted her with burn injuries with a lighter, and subjected her to repeated cruelty before abandoning her on a side road in Wattegama.

Dropping a child of only four years old in a place 36 kilometers away from her home, so that she could never find her way home shows the depth of the mother’s cruel mindset. Such crimes against children are not limited to a particular area or a race or a religion and this is not the first of its kind. Also given the pattern of the incidents, this could also not be the last such incident.

Another mother in Weligama had left her one year old infant with her fourteen-year-old sister-in-law a few years ago under the pretext of visiting her husband and fled the area. In the same year, a disabled man had left his two sons aged four and five years at the Mahanama Bridge in Matara and had allegedly attempted to commit suicide by jumping into the Nilawala Ganga. According a similar report, two children were thrown into Kalu Ganga by their mother and were rescued by three army personnel on duty nearby.

A sharp observer of child cruelty in the country would provide a long list of incidents of child abuse already published in the media, before they are forgotten.

Needless to say, none of these hapless parents and children are from affluent families, pointing the underlying cause of these incidents –  poverty. Whatever the figures about growth rates, per capita income or other achievements recorded in the reports prepared by  concerned authorities under various governments have been, these stories are a testimony of the real situation that prevails in the country for the past several decades.

In its latest classification of countries, the World Bank last month had elevated Sri Lanka from a lower middle-income country to an upper middle-income country, noting that Sri Lanka recorded real economic growth of 5% last year. Sri Lanka has crossed the threshold of per capita Gross National Income (GNI) of $ 4,636, though narrowly, to become upper middle-income country. The country has come out of bankruptcy, surpassing all macroeconomic goals set out by the International Monetary Fund (IMF) such as targets from exports, taxes and foreign employment.

However, there is still a segment of population that is  forced even to abandon their children or destroy them, not out of hatred but out of desperation. Poverty in the country is a chronic issue. Experts point out that “Sri Lanka is not inherently poor, but it faces severe economic stagnation and high poverty rates—with over a quarter of the population affected—largely due to the devastating 2022 debt and foreign currency crisis, decades of heavy borrowing, poorly managed state enterprises, and a heavy reliance on vulnerable traditional exports.”

On the other hand, the income gap tends to maintain the poverty despite  macroeconomic achievements. According to the Centre for Poverty Analysis (CEPA), “more than half the total household income of the country is enjoyed by the richest 20% in Sri Lanka while the bottom decile (poorest 20%) gets only 5%, with the share of household income being just 1.6% for the poorest 10%.” Also “regional disparities between urban and rural areas, unequal access to quality education and well-paying jobs, an over-reliance on lower-wage agriculture, and structural economic shocks that disproportionately affect vulnerable households” are blamed for this income gap.

Underscoring its responsibility to address this issue the National People’s Power (NPP) in its manifesto “A thriving Nation, A Beautiful Life” envisages economic democracy with a plan “to ensure equitable opportunities in economic activities, participation in the economic decision-making process with an equal say, and a fair share of economic beneﬁts.”

Yet, we do not see decisive action on the part of the NPP government towards the economic democracy it promised.
