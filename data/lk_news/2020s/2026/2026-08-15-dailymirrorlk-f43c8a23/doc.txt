A solution to the drug-fuelled prison system How the rehabilitation of 12,000 LTTE cadres can show the way

Sri Lanka undertook a mass rehabilitation and reintegration of 12, 191 surrendered or captured LTTE cadres successfully (Photo by Kushan Pathiraja).

Sri Lanka’s prison system – and the judiciary – that keep flooding overcrowded prisons with an endless stream of inmates is a worthy case study of what one would call systemic incompetence.

Thanks to the accumulated rot, the prison system is now exploding in violent riots with alarming frequency, reminiscent of a Latin American narco-state. The crisis in the prison system is what successive governments in the past and an inept bureaucracy made it to be. The same quarters are now blaming a ‘conspiracy’ behind the riots in overcrowded prison cells that house four times the number of people they were meant to accommodate. And things cannot be  grotesquely comical and out of touch when a supposedly advisory body – the Country Together national operation council, chaired by the president himself – explores the death sentence for drug offences.

The crisis in the prison system is not beyond simple commonsense fixes. But indifference and the frequent tendency for piecemeal solutions have now led the system to its dangerous breaking point.

First consider the crisis in numbers. Sri Lankan prison system, which was built to accommodate only 11,000 inmates, housed 39,516 as of this month.

Nearly three-quarters of the prison population (28,646)  are pre-trial detainees. There are only 10,870 convicted inmates. The numbers become even more despairing as a Sunday Times investigation revealed that historically over 50%-68% of annual convicted admissions into the prison system are petty offenders jailed for unpaid fines.

As a result, the Sri Lankan prison system is filled with drug offenders in free trial detention and fine defaulters. Of the total prison population, 23,000 are currently held on drug-related charges. This means drug offenders account for over half of the prison population, the vast majority of them being caught with a small quantity of drugs in possession for personal use.

The police had recently spent an inordinate amount of time and resources to lock up drug addicts as part of the government’s anti-drug campaign. The prison population, 28,000 in 2024 swelled to 40,000 as a result of the influx of drug addicts.

However, the sense of urgency in locking up the suspects is not seen in the legal process.

The government’s analyst department is a byword for inefficiency. Remand inmates are held in prolonged detention owing to the inefficiency in the system, awaiting test reports, and ineffectual judges who have failed to use the full course of legal provisions to grant bail.

Then, inadequacy of rehabilitation has turned the prison system into a revolving door for drug offenders. Only a fraction of the convicted drug offenders go through some sort of rehabilitation programme in prison. Of nearly 5,000 convicted drug offenders in the prison system, only 1,506 are currently undergoing formal rehabilitation.  The remainder, 3,456 inmates, are housed in normal wards due to the lack of facilities.

The solution to Sri Lanka’s prison crisis is not rocket science.

The justice minister has proposed state-funded bail banks to support inmates held due to unpaid fines. That is the right approach. But a more practical long-term remedy would be unpaid fine-related prison sentences to be converted to mandatory community service. That would halve the number of convicted prisoners held in the prison system.

Second, introduce a house arrest system for pre-trial detainees, a proposal currently under consideration. That alone should result in a substantial reduction in the total prison population that accounts for three- quarter of remand prisoners.

However, considering that the vast majority of pre-trial detainees are drug addicts, a house arrest system could itself become a revolving door. Therefore, instead of protracted court proceedings, the government should devise a simpler mandatory rehabilitation programme for drug addicts.

That is indeed a compassionate alternative to locking up drug addicts in prisons. Officials might find an excuse in the lack of rehabilitation facilities and trained counsellors.

However, the government can borrow from the experience of one of the most successful rehabilitation exercises in the past. In the aftermath of the war, Sri Lanka undertook a mass rehabilitation and reintegration of 12, 191 surrendered or captured LTTE cadres.

That is one of the most successful deradicalisation and rehabilitation programmes in the world. Nearly two decades since then, there is zero recidivism.

The programme was handled by the specially created Bureau of the Commissioner General of Rehabilitation – the government can establish a similar structure for a mass drug rehabilitation initiative.  It does not necessarily need to be headed by the military top brass,  but the facilities, organisational competency, and fast expertise of the defence forces should be utilised within a civilian-led initiative. The rehabilitation of LTTE cadres lasted 12 to 18 months, and included mandatory counselling, post-traumatic stress therapy and vocational training of  40 technical and vocational skills, including IT, carpentry, masonry, tailoring, plumbing and agriculture.

Over 2,500 child soldiers received formal school education and sat for national GCE Ordinary Level and Advanced Level examinations. Upon the completion of the programme, the government provided micro-finance loans, toolkits, sewing machines and agricultural equipment.

Sri Lanka’s prison crisis is an extension of a growing crisis in drug addiction. One cannot be addressed without the other.  The president should revisit the success of the previous rehabilitation programme. That it was undertaken by the Rajapaksas or was foul-mouthed by NGO busybodies should not dissuade the president. He should call on the military expertise of the past, and facilities and manpower at the present. The clock is ticking. The trigger for the next mass protests may be in the overcrowded and ill-managed prison system.
