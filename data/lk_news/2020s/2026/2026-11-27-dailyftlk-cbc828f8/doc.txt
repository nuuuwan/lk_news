Budget 2027: Sri Lanka is running out of time. Trade agreements alone won’t save the economy

Budget 2027 has announced a committee to drive trade agreements across multiple countries

WHILE Sri Lanka debates trade agreements, the rest of Asia is building the future. India is racing towards a $ 5 trillion economy. Vietnam exports more than $ 370 billion annually. Bangladesh's apparel exports now exceed $ 50 billion. ASEAN is becoming the centre of global manufacturing growth. Regional supply chains are being redrawn, investment is shifting across Asia, and countries are competing aggressively for technology, talent and export market share.

Sri Lanka, meanwhile, continues to debate whether it should embrace economic openness at all. That debate belongs to another era. As the country debates Budget 2027, the real question is not whether Sri Lanka needs more Free Trade Agreements (FTAs). It is whether Sri Lanka can become competitive enough to benefit from them before the opportunity passes. The greatest threat to the economy is no longer protectionism, inflation or even debt. It is irrelevant.

The Government points to encouraging economic indicators. Inflation has stabilised. Tourism has rebounded but stalled due to the Middle Eastern crisis. Remittances have improved significantly. Export earnings reached a record $ 17.25 billion in 2025, growing by 5.6%. Sri Lanka is poised to reach $ 20 billion this year. Which are positive signs.

But they are not signs of transformation. Sri Lanka's export earnings are still less than one-twentieth of Vietnam's exports. Apparel still accounts for nearly $ 5 billion of export income. Research and development spending remains below 0.2% of GDP, among the lowest in Asia. Exports remain heavily concentrated in a handful of products and markets. The uncomfortable truth is that Sri Lanka has recovered from a crisis without yet solving the weaknesses that caused it. A country of 22 million people cannot become prosperous by selling largely the same products to largely the same markets for another decade.

For years, Sri Lanka's economic debate has centred on whether FTAs are good or bad. The evidence from Asia suggests this is the wrong question. Vietnam did not become an export powerhouse because it signed trade agreements. It became successful because it simultaneously invested in industrial zones, logistics infrastructure, technical education, foreign investment attraction and export competitiveness.

Bangladesh did not become the world's second-largest apparel exporter because of preferential market access. It built factories, skills and scale. South Korea, Taiwan and Singapore did not become advanced economies because of trade liberalisation alone. They invested relentlessly in productivity, technology, innovation and human capital. Trade agreements created opportunities. Competitiveness converted opportunities into growth. Sri Lanka continues to confuse the two.

Nowhere is Sri Lanka's challenge more evident than in its relationship with India. Bilateral trade exceeded $ 5.3 billion in 2025, however, Sri Lankan exports to India is just $ 1 billion. When I was Chairman of the Sri Lanka Export Development Board around 20 years back, it was at 0.5 billion dollars. Which is the slow pace of export growth. Whilst some celebrate these numbers, they should also raise different questions. A country situated less than 30 kilometres from one of the world's fastest-growing major economies globally exports only $ 1 billion worth of goods to a market of 1.4 billion people for a tiny share of 0.12%. Why? The answer cannot simply be market access. India exports machinery, pharmaceuticals, vehicles, petroleum products and industrial inputs because it possesses scale, productivity and industrial depth.

Sri Lanka exports far less because it has not developed comparable industrial capabilities. The gap is not geographic. The gap is competitive. And it is widening. While India is investing billions of dollars through Make in India, Production Linked Incentive programs, logistics corridors, semiconductor manufacturing and digital infrastructure, Sri Lanka is still arguing about whether trade agreements themselves are beneficial. The rest of Asia settled that debate years ago.

Critics frequently point to Sri Lanka's trade deficit with India as evidence that the Indo-Lanka FTA has failed. That interpretation is politically convenient but economically misleading. Nearly 90% of imports from major trading partners such as India and China occur outside FTA concessions, with importers paying full duties and choosing those products anyway because they remain competitive on price, quality and reliability. That single statistic reveals an uncomfortable reality.

Competitiveness is driving trade, not tariff concessions. Likewise, Sri Lanka's export challenges are not primarily caused by a lack of agreements. They are caused by: Low productivity, High production costs, Weak innovation, Limited economies of scale, Skills shortages, Inadequate industrial sophistication In other words, Sri Lanka has a competitiveness problem disguised as a trade policy problem.

Recent developments in the United States should be a wake-up call. According to IPS estimates, Sri Lanka could have faced effective tariff rates of almost 30%, with apparel tariffs exceeding 36% and potential export losses of more than $ 600 million. Nearly 16,000 jobs could have been affected. Subsequently, the effective rate was reduced by Trump, but the most important lesson is not the tariff itself. It is the exposure.

Any economy whose export performance can be significantly disrupted by a policy decision in a single market has failed to diversify sufficiently. Sri Lanka's dependence on a limited number of products and destinations is becoming a strategic economic risk. Diversification is no longer desirable. It is urgent.

The world economy is increasingly centred in Asia. The RCEP bloc accounts for roughly 30% of global GDP and trade. East Asia's middle class expanded from approximately 834 million consumers in 2016 to more than 1.1 billion by 2022. By 2030, one in every three middle-class consumers globally is expected to live in East Asia. This is where future demand will come from. This is where future investment will flow. This is where future supply chains will be built, just like what we saw in the recent Nvidia debacle. The critical question is whether Sri Lanka will be part of that growth story. Or whether it will watch from the sidelines.

Budget 2027 should not be measured by the number of committees established or agreements negotiated. It should be judged against six questions: Will it increase productivity? Will it raise investment in innovation and research beyond the current 0.2% of GDP? Will it create new export sectors in electronics, medical technology, AI, renewable energy and advanced manufacturing? Will it modernise customs, logistics and trade facilitation? Will it address severe skills shortages across industry? Will it help Sri Lankan firms become globally competitive? If the answer is no, additional FTAs will simply become additions to an already long list of underutilised agreements.

The debate is no longer between protectionism and liberalisation. That is yesterday's argument. The real choice facing Sri Lanka is between competitiveness and irrelevance. Trade agreements can open doors. They cannot create productive factories. They cannot build innovative companies. They cannot develop skilled workers. They cannot substitute for economic reform. Only competitiveness can do that. And that is why Budget 2027 matters.

The window for reform is still open. But it is narrowing quickly as India, Vietnam, Indonesia and other Asian economies accelerate ahead. Sri Lanka's challenge is no longer whether it should join the race. The challenge is that the race has already started. The longer the country delays, the harder it will become to catch up. Budget 2027 must therefore be more than a fiscal statement. It must be a competitive Budget. Otherwise, Sri Lanka risks becoming a spectator in Asia's economic rise rather than a participant in it.
