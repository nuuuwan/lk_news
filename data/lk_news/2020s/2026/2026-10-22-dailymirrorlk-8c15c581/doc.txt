Remembering Colombo’s Golden Era of Video Rentals

The sound of hundreds of plastic sleeves sliding against one another formed the quintessential soundtrack of a Colombo Friday night

A modern movie-watching evening is a solitary, frictionless affair. Sitting on a couch in Colombo, you scroll through a widescreen interface on Netflix or Prime and click play. The movie streams immediately, effortless and convenient. Yet, for anyone who grew up in the city between the late 1980s and the early 2010s, this digital convenience lacks something vital: the distinct thrill of the weekend movie hunt and the social networking experience.

Back then, movies arrived in local cinemas late, and original DVDs came even later or were next to impossible to find. Unless you travelled abroad or had relatives who could bring back a disc, video libraries were your only gateway to global cinema. This was long before high-speed fibre internet and data packages transformed entertainment into instant access, where you can now download House of Dragon and Lanterns at the exact same time as your friends in the U.S. Because of this, many of us still reminisce about the days when the neighbourhood video library served as the cultural anchor for Colombo’s middle class. Outlets like Precious, Brite Video Vision, Video International, and Marathon were not just retail spaces; they were community crossroads where memories were made, one rented disc at a time.

Renting a movie in Colombo was a ritual that engaged all the senses almost like the Triwizard Tournament itself, it seems. It began the moment you stepped through the glass doors, greeted by a freezing blast of AC designed to keep the delicate electronics from overheating. Unlike Western rental chains like Blockbuster, which displayed movies in plastic cases (clamshells), Sri Lankan video libraries crafted a local aesthetic: the polythene sleeve. Wall-to-wall shelves were lined with thousands of clear plastic pockets. Browsing the aisles meant physically flipping through these heavy, rustling rows. The sound of hundreds of plastic sleeves sliding against one another formed the quintessential soundtrack of a Colombo Friday night. Once you found a movie you wanted, you walked up to the front counter. There, behind the wooden desk, the clerks functioned as the ultimate librarians of pop culture, offering spoiler-free recommendations long before the era of Wikipedia and online reviews.

Every neighbourhood in Colombo held its preferred allegiance. If you lived around   Colombo5, Havelock Town, or the chaotic intersections of Kirulapone, your cultural compass pointed directly toward Precious Video Library on Havelock Road. Sprinting up the stairs to get a first-hand glimpse of the latest arrivals was a weekly ritual. Precious thrived during the chaotic transition from late-stage VHS tapes to the VCD and early DVD boom. Holding a laminated Precious membership card was a genuine badge of neighbourhood honour. On weekends, the stretch of road outside the shop would clog with vehicles as families rushed to secure the latest Hollywood releases before another household beat them to it.

Further down the grid, located at No. 31, Lauries Place on Duplication Road, stood Brite Video Vision. Brite proudly marketed itself around the “business of fun.” It was an immense, meticulously organised archive that catered to commuters heading home from the commercial hubs of Fort and Kollupitiya. Brite was legendary for its deep catalogue; if an obscure action movie or a specific season of an American sitcom could not be found anywhere else, the clerks could usually dig it out from a hidden drawer.

Closer to Bambalapitiya and Thimbirigasyaya, Video International established itself as the premium, corporate powerhouse of the rental world. It was the go-to spot for high-quality, authentic cinematic releases and, later, crisp Blu-Ray discs. Other household names included Jini Video, Laser City, and Marathon Video Rental.

They had mostly English films but had a niche with Tamil, Sinhala and Hindi as well.

Local libraries filled a massive cultural void by navigating a grey market of international imports, lending an air of unpredictable adventure to every rental. As technology transitioned from cassettes to laserdiscs and eventually to DVDs, many of us accumulated fond, chaotic memories navigating the search for the right movie.

Everyone from the 2000s era remembers the notorious “subtitle roulette.” You would rent a highly anticipated blockbuster, rush home, and pop the silver disc into the DVD player, only to find the English subtitles hilariously mistranslated, formatted in a giant neon-yellow font, or permanently out of sync with the audio. Spider-Man (2002), The Lord of the Rings, and Pirates of the Caribbean all had this feature. The holy grails, however, were Jurassic Park, Titanic, Speed, Con-Air, or anything starring Arnold Schwarzenegger, Jackie Chan or Bruce Lee. If any of these were available, you grabbed them immediately, and if you returned them late, you would get multiple urgent phone calls from the store on your landline (another relic of the past).

Before the word “binge-watching” entered the lexicon, Colombo audiences did it via bulky, plastic, multi-disc box sets that cost a premium to rent. Brite Video Vision was legendary for having entire shelves dedicated to Friends. Renting Season 4 meant carrying a thick stack of CDs home. Then there was Mind Your Language (1977–1986): even though it was an older British sitcom, it was a massive, cross-generational staple in Sri Lanka. It was routinely rented for family gatherings because of its multicultural humour.

There was also the unique phenomenon of the “R5” or “Telecine” print. Midway through a film, you might hear the faint cough or popcorn munching of a theatre audience halfway across the world, or see the silhouette of a latecomer walking across the bottom of your TV screen. Yet, we could not complain. For a few hundred rupees, Colombo audiences enjoyed access to global culture. Watching these imports was always a race against time; we routinely calculated our viewing schedules around rolling power cuts, praying that the electricity would hold out long enough to finish a film or at least make it through the best fight sequence.

Perhaps the greatest loss in the transition from the physical rental shop to the streaming algorithm is the death of the neighbourhood clerk. Long before Netflix developed AI to predict your preferences, the guys behind the counters knew exactly what you wanted. And if they liked you, you’d get a call “latest Harrison Ford film just came in, come fast before it goes”.

They knew if your father preferred gritty World War II action-thrillers, if your mother was tracking a specific teledrama box set, or if you were finally old enough to rent a horror film. A simple nod from a trusted clerk who slipped a hidden, newly arrived disc from under the counter—accompanied by a quiet, “This one is really good, take it today” carried as much weight as the digital recommendation engines that we have today. They would routinely reserve the hottest new arrivals for their favourite customers, fostering a deep social network.

Anwar at Precious Video and Kumar at Brite Video were always cheerful faces that everyone knew who could give great recommendations. Neighbours ran into neighbours in the comedy aisles, trading feedback. Siblings argued over whether to rent an action movie or a cartoon, bargaining with their parents under the watchful, amused laughs of staff members who had seen the exact same argument play out a dozen times prior at least that very evening.

The decline of Colombo’s video libraries did not happen overnight. As technology progressed into the 2010s, the island was affected by global currents and underwent a rapid technological shift. The advent of high-speed broadband and fibre networks suddenly made streaming films effortless. The one-week rental window, the chore of rewinding cassettes, the disappointment of coming home only to realise a CD was scratched, and the dreaded late fees suddenly felt like ancient inconveniences.

One by one, the lights inside the great libraries flickered out. Premium real estate on Havelock Road and Duplication Road was quickly reclaimed by trendy cafes, clothing boutiques, and corporate offices. Some smaller neighbourhood “CD kades” attempted to survive by pivoting into hybrid shops, selling mobile reloads, stationery, and photocopying services, but the magic of the dedicated archive was long gone.

Today, the physical artefacts of that era are mostly novelty antiques sitting in dusty boxes at home. Yet, the memory of those vibrant spaces remains. Precious, Brite, Marathon, and their contemporaries did something marvellous: they turned the simple act of watching a movie into a shared, physical adventure. They remind us of a time when entertainment required us to venture out, flip through plastic sleeves, and actively participate in the community of cinema.
