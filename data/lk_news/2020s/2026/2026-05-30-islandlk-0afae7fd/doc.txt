Dhamma, Inquiry and the Modern World

In an age shaped by technological advancement, scientific discovery, and unprecedented material progress, humanity continues to grapple with conflict, anxiety, inequality, and moral uncertainty. Despite remarkable achievements in medicine, communication, and industry, millions still live in poverty, violence continues to devastate societies, and environmental destruction threatens the future of the planet. Amid these contradictions, the teachings of Gautama Buddha continue to offer a timeless and deeply relevant path grounded in wisdom, inquiry, and compassion.

The Buddha’s teaching, known as the Dhamma, differs significantly from systems built solely on unquestioned belief or ritual authority. The Dhamma is universal and non-sectarian. It does not demand blind faith, nor does it insist upon obedience through fear or dogma. Instead, the Buddha invited individuals to investigate truth through direct experience. His teaching was ehipassiko — “come and see for yourself.”

The Buddha repeatedly discouraged unquestioning acceptance of teachings, even his own. He encouraged people to examine, practise, and verify truth through personal understanding. This spirit of inquiry remains one of the most distinctive and intellectually compelling features of Buddhism. Rather than merely offering abstract explanations about suffering, the Dhamma provides practical guidance on how suffering arises and how it can be overcome.

At the heart of the Buddha’s teaching lies a profound focus on the present moment. The past cannot be altered, and the future remains uncertain, but one’s actions in the present shape the course of life. Through mindfulness, ethical conduct, and wisdom, individuals can transform their minds and influence their future positively.

The Buddha’s approach therefore places responsibility upon the individual rather than external forces. Liberation is not achieved through divine intervention, but through disciplined effort, self-awareness, and understanding. This practical emphasis has led many scholars and thinkers to observe parallels between aspects of Buddhist thought and scientific inquiry.

Unlike systems that depend entirely on revelation or unquestioned authority, Buddhism encourages observation, reflection, and experience. The Dhamma teaches that all things are impermanent, constantly changing, and interconnected. Nothing exists independently or permanently. Human beings themselves are part of this continuous process of arising and passing away. This concept of impermanence aligns closely with the understanding that reality is dynamic rather than fixed. The Buddha described existence as a state of constant flux, where attachment to permanence creates suffering. Human dissatisfaction arises because people cling to identities, possessions, emotions, and conditions that inevitably change.

Yet Buddhism should not simply be reduced to a scientific philosophy. While some aspects of Buddhist thought may resonate with modern scientific perspectives, the Dhamma ultimately addresses questions of human suffering, morality, consciousness, and liberation in ways that extend beyond scientific measurement.

Throughout history, several prominent intellectuals and scholars have expressed admiration for Buddhist philosophy. The distinguished Pali scholar, Professor T.W. Rhys Davids, once observed that after studying many of the world’s religions, he found none that surpassed the depth and beauty of the Buddha’s teaching. Likewise, the renowned physicist Albert Einstein reportedly remarked that if there were a religion compatible with modern scientific needs, it would be Buddhism.

Such statements are often cited to portray Buddhism as a “scientific religion.” However, reducing Buddhism solely to science can oversimplify both disciplines. Science and Buddhism operate within different frameworks and pursue different aims. Science seeks to understand the external workings of the physical universe through experimentation and observation, while the Dhamma focuses primarily on understanding the mind, suffering, and ethical living.

As Professor Arjuna de Soysa has pointed out, Buddhism is not scientific, nor is science Buddhistic. Attempts to force complete agreement between the two often result in superficial conclusions. Scientific theories evolve and change over time, while the Dhamma addresses existential realities connected to human experience and mental transformation

.Nevertheless, Buddhism’s openness to inquiry distinguishes it from rigid systems of belief. The Buddha never demanded unquestioning loyalty. He invited investigation. This spirit of examination is especially relevant in a modern world where individuals increasingly seek meaning beyond inherited tradition.

Questions surrounding the preservation and interpretation of religious teachings also deserve thoughtful reflection. Since the Buddha lived more than 2,500 years ago, some naturally ask how his teachings were preserved with complete accuracy over centuries of oral transmission before being written down. Such questions should not be dismissed as irreverent or hostile. In fact, the Buddha himself encouraged thoughtful inquiry rather than blind acceptance.

Human interpretation, memory, and cultural influence inevitably shape the transmission of all religious traditions. Yet the enduring strength of the Dhamma lies not merely in textual preservation, but in its practical applicability. Its value is ultimately tested through experience, ethical conduct, and mental cultivation.

The Dhamma also challenges humanity to reconsider the meaning of progress. Modern civilization celebrates economic growth, technological innovation, and material success, yet vast inequalities persist. Millions continue to suffer from hunger, disease, displacement, and violence. Wealth accumulates in one part of the world while poverty devastates another. Resources are consumed at unsustainable levels, and nations invest heavily in weapons even as humanitarian crises expand.

The Buddha recognized long ago that greed, hatred, and ignorance are the root causes of suffering. Technological progress alone cannot eliminate these destructive tendencies. Without moral wisdom, scientific advancement may simply increase humanity’s capacity for harm.

This is why the Dhamma places such importance on compassion, mindfulness, and ethical responsibility. Buddhism encourages individuals to recognize the interconnections of life and cultivate concern for the well being of all living beings. Human beings are urged to move beyond narrow self-interest and develop qualities such as generosity, patience, non-violence, and loving-kindness.

Importantly, the Dhamma does not reject reason or critical thought. At the same time, it recognizes that human understanding has limits. Even science itself often operates through probabilities, models, and evolving theories rather than absolute certainty. Many scientific concepts remain incomplete or debated despite extensive research.

For example, debates continue regarding consciousness, the origins of life, and the precise mechanisms of evolution and speciation. Scientific knowledge constantly evolves as new discoveries emerge. Buddhism similarly acknowledges the complexity and changing nature of existence, though it approaches these questions from the perspective of direct human experience rather than laboratory analysis.

The Buddha’s teachings therefore offer not merely a system of belief, but a path of inner transformation. The aim of the Dhamma is not to win philosophical arguments or provide rigid dogmas, but to help individuals overcome suffering and cultivate wisdom. In today’s world, where societies are increasingly divided by materialism, ideological conflict, and moral confusion, the relevance of the Dhamma remains profound. Its message encourages humanity to think critically, act compassionately, and live mindfully.

The true value of the Buddha’s teaching lies not in whether it can be labeled scientific or religious, but in its capacity to transform human behaviour and reduce suffering. More than 25 centuries after the Buddha’s enlightenment, the Dhamma continues to inspire people across cultures and generations because it speaks directly to the deepest concerns of human existence, how to live wisely, peacefully, and compassionately in an ever-changing world.
