From a whisper in rainforest to a national treasure: The Serendib Scops Owl still guards its secrets

As dusk settles over Sri Lanka’s ancient rainforests, the forest comes alive in ways few people ever witness. Cicadas begin their nightly chorus, frogs call from hidden streams, and somewhere beneath the towering canopy, a tiny owl announces its presence with a haunting call that, for years, puzzled even the country’s most experienced ornithologists.

That mysterious voice belonged to the Serendib Scops Owl (Otus thilohoffmanni)—one of the world’s rarest owls and among Sri Lanka’s greatest natural treasures.

As the world marks International Owl Awareness Day on August 4, the remarkable story of this elusive endemic bird reminds us that even in the 21st century, nature still has the power to surprise, and that some of its greatest mysteries remain hidden within Sri Lanka’s rainforests.

For more than a century, scientists believed Sri Lanka’s endemic bird list was complete. That belief changed dramatically in 1995 when renowned ornithologist Deepal Warakagoda and colleagues, conducting fieldwork in the Makandawa Forest near Kitulgala, heard an unfamiliar owl call unlike anything previously recorded in the country.

For six years the mysterious caller remained invisible. Researchers heard its distinctive voice repeatedly but failed to catch even a glimpse of the bird. Some even speculated the strange sound belonged to an amphibian rather than an owl.

The mystery was finally solved in 2001 when Warakagoda and his team located the bird in the Sinharaja Rainforest. Scientific examination confirmed they had discovered an entirely new species.

It was formally described in 2004, becoming Sri Lanka’s first new bird species to be identified since the Sri Lanka Whistling Thrush was described in 1868.

Named after the ancient name “Serendib” for Sri Lanka, the owl’s scientific name honours the late Thilo W. Hoffmann, whose pioneering conservation work helped protect Sinharaja and other priceless rainforests.

Today, the Serendib Scops Owl is more than a scientific curiosity. Its image appears on Sri Lanka’s Rs. 20 currency note, making it one of the country’s best-known birds, even though very few people know the extraordinary story behind its discovery.

Barely 16 to 17 centimetres long, the owl is perfectly adapted to disappear into its surroundings. Its reddish-brown plumage blends seamlessly with dead leaves and tree bark, while the absence of prominent ear tufts makes it almost impossible to detect during daylight hours. It spends its days quietly roosting close to the forest floor before emerging after sunset to hunt insects among the leaf litter.

Despite being discovered more than two decades ago, scientists still know remarkably little about its breeding biology, nesting behaviour, population size, movements and habitat requirements.

Those unanswered questions are now driving a new generation of researchers.

Among them is Waruni Tissera, Research Officer at the Wildlife and Nature Protection Society of Sri Lanka, who is leading a conservation research project under the EDGE of Existence Fellowship Programme of the Zoological Society of London (ZSL).

Vidanapathirana, Gehan Rajeev, Uditha Hettige and international collaborators from ZSL and Deakin University, Australia, the project seeks to better understand the owl’s distribution, habitat requirements and ecology while promoting responsible nature tourism and community participation in conservation.

Since May 2025, the research team has carried out extensive nocturnal surveys in the Kelani Valley Forest Reserve and the Sinharaja Rainforest.

The work is demanding. Researchers navigate steep, slippery trails in complete darkness, often through leech-infested forests, carefully listening for the owl’s distinctive call while recording habitat characteristics and environmental conditions at every survey site.

According to Waruni, every survey highlights how much remains to be discovered about the species. Although the owl has now been known to science for more than twenty years, many aspects of its life history remain a mystery, making every observation valuable to conservation.

Recognising that field observations alone cannot reveal the species’ complete distribution, the project also incorporates citizen science records and historical observations contributed by birdwatchers, photographers, forest guides and nature enthusiasts.

Waruni believes conservation must extend beyond scientific research. Accordingly, the project works closely with local communities to improve awareness of the ecological importance of owls and to dispel myths that have surrounded these nocturnal birds for generations.

A major focus has been environmental education. More than 200 schoolchildren living around the Makandawa and Sinharaja rainforest landscapes have already participated in awareness programmes introducing them to the Serendib Scops Owl and the importance of conserving Sri Lanka’s remaining rainforests.

On International Owl Awareness Day, the outreach programme will engage a further group of students and young environmental pioneers.

The Serendib Scops Owl remains one of Sri Lanka’s most range-restricted endemic birds, found only in the wet zone’s lowland and submontane rainforests, including Sinharaja, Kitulgala, Runakanda, Kanneliya and Eratna-Gilimale.

Its future depends entirely on the survival of these forests.

Habitat degradation, fragmentation, unregulated tourism and increasing human activity continue to threaten these fragile ecosystems. Protecting the owl therefore means protecting entire rainforest landscapes that harbour countless endemic plants and animals found nowhere else on Earth.

Every call recorded during a night survey, every citizen science observation and every new scientific finding helps fill another gap in our understanding of this remarkable species.

The Serendib Scops Owl is far more than a bird featured on a currency note. It is a living symbol of Sri Lanka’s unique natural heritage, a reminder that scientific discovery is still unfolding within our forests, and compelling evidence that conserving our rainforests remains one of the nation’s most important environmental responsibilities.

Waruni gratefully acknowledges the support extended by the EDGE of Existence Programme of the Zoological Society of London, The Peregrine Fund, the Wildlife and Nature Protection Society of Sri Lanka, the Young Zoologists’ Association of Sri Lanka, her supervisors, dedicated field team and all collaborators whose commitment continues to unlock the secrets of one of Sri Lanka’s most extraordinary birds.
