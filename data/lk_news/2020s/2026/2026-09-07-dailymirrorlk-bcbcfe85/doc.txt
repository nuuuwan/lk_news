New police uniform, but what about public safety?

There is nothing wrong with wanting a modern police force. There is nothing wrong with giving police officers better uniforms, better equipment and better working conditions.

At a time when Sri Lankans are increasingly worried about shootings, organised crime and the ability of the police to keep people safe, President Anura Kumara Dissanayake has announced plans for a new police uniform designed by professional fashion designers.

The President has instructed the Inspector General of Police to work with a professional fashion design company to develop the new uniform, which is expected to be introduced by the middle of next year.

According to the President, the new uniform will be designed to accommodate modern equipment such as body cameras, batons, flashlights and other essential gear. He has also spoken about wider reforms to the Police, including better infrastructure, technology, training, working conditions and recruitment.

All of that sounds reasonable. But one has to ask a very simple question. Is this the need of the hour?

Sri Lanka is not currently facing a crisis of badly dressed police officers. It is facing a crisis of public confidence in law and order.

People are being shot in public places. Some shootings have taken place in broad daylight. Innocent people have been caught in the crossfire. In 2025, Sri Lanka recorded 114 shooting incidents, resulting in 60 deaths and 57 injuries, according to police figures. More than 73 per cent of those incidents were linked to organised crime and drug trafficking rivalries. That is more than one shooting incident every four days.

In January 2026 alone, several shootings left people dead and others injured, including children. A man was shot dead in Jinthupitiya in Colombo while two children, aged three and four, were injured.

This is the reality facing the country. So when the President speaks about modernising the Police, the public would understandably expect the first priority to be intelligence, crime prevention, investigations, technology and manpower. A fashionable uniform will not prevent a shooting.

A better-designed pocket will not dismantle an organised criminal network. To be fair, the President’s announcement was not only about uniforms. He has also announced a planned recruitment of 10,000 police personnel, citing shortages that have resulted in some officers working shifts of between 12 and 18 hours. A risk allowance for police officers is also planned from January.

These are important measures. A police officer who is exhausted after working excessive hours cannot be expected to perform at his or her best. There is also a legitimate argument for redesigning the uniform. If officers are now expected to carry body cameras and other modern equipment, their uniforms should be practical enough to accommodate them. But the Government must be careful about priorities.

Sri Lanka has been through an economic crisis in which every rupee of public money matters. People are struggling with the cost of living. If a new uniform is part of a comprehensive police modernisation programme, then let the Government explain the programme clearly. How much will it cost? How many officers will receive the new uniforms? What will happen to the existing uniforms? How much is being allocated for police intelligence? How much for forensic capabilities? How much for technology? How much for training? How quickly will the 10,000 new recruits actually enter service? These are the questions that matter.

The public needs a Police service capable of tracking organised crime networks, identifying illegal firearms, following financial trails, strengthening investigations and protecting witnesses. It needs officers who are properly trained and adequately equipped.

The good news is that there are signs that the Police are already intensifying their work. In 2025, police seized significant numbers of firearms and arrested dozens of suspects connected to shootings. More than 2,276 illegal weapons were reportedly seized that year. But the scale of the problem means more needs to be done. The first measure of a modern Police service is not how its officers look.

It is whether citizens feel safer and protected when they walk out of their homes.
