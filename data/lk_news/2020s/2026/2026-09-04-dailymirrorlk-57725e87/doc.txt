Rs. 250 mn cancer drugs at risk of being destroyed

Colombo, Sep. 4 (Daily Mirror) - A stock of cancer medicine worth more than Rs. 250 million is at risk of being destroyed due to a dispute over its regulatory approval, while cancer patients are already facing shortages of essential medicines, Doctors' Trade Union Alliance for Medical and Civil Rights Chairman Specialist Dr. Chamal Sanjeewa said.

The medicine, Filgrastim, also known as G-CSF Injection, is currently in Sri Lanka and remains in storage without being distributed to hospitals.

Dr. Sanjeewa questioned why the authorities had failed to find a quick solution to release the medicine, particularly at a time when cancer patients are facing difficulties obtaining essential drugs.

He questioned whether the National Medicines Regulatory Authority (NMRA) could permit the use of the stock after obtaining the approval of a panel of medical experts.

He also questioned why the required stickers could not be brought from China and placed on the medicines under the proper supervision of the relevant authorities.

“Why can't the problem be solved by importing the sticker from China and sticking it on?” Dr. Sanjeewa asked, stressing that the matter should be resolved without further delay if the medicine is found to be safe for patients.

He also questioned whether the regulatory dispute was being used to embarrass the Government.

According to Dr. Sanjeewa, the medicine had been imported based on conditions stipulated by the State Pharmaceuticals Corporation (SPC) and was believed to have been brought into the country through the proper registration process.

However, he said questions later arose over whether the medicine complied with NMRA requirements after it arrived in Sri Lanka.

As a result, the Medical Supplies Division had not accepted the stock for distribution to hospitals, he said.

Dr. Sanjeewa said Health Minister Dr. Nalinda Jayatissa had taken a positive step towards finding a temporary solution to the issue.

However, he called for a full investigation into how the medicine was imported and whether all relevant officials had followed the required procedures.

“If this medicine was imported according to the instructions given by the officials, a proper investigation must be carried out,” he said.

He also questioned whether the medicine could be used in Sri Lanka under strict supervision rather than sending the entire stock back to China.

Dr. Sanjeewa warned that the medicines, valued at more than Rs. 250 million, could eventually be destroyed if they remain unused.

He said this would result in a serious waste of public funds at a time when the country is experiencing shortages of essential cancer medicines.

He said around US$500,000 had been spent to import the medicines, highlighting the financial cost involved.

According to Dr. Sanjeewa, Sri Lanka is currently experiencing shortages of around 40 types of cancer medicines.

He said the shortages were causing serious difficulties for cancer patients and their families, who depend on these medicines for their treatment.

He also alleged that cancer medicines brought into the country illegally were being sold at high prices. He questioned whether sufficient action was being taken to identify and seize such stocks.

Dr. Sanjeewa said it was unfair for delays to occur in releasing medicines imported through the proper registration process over issues such as labelling, while patients were struggling to obtain essential drugs.

He further raised concerns that repeated delays in releasing medicines could create suspicion that other interests were involved, while stressing that such concerns should be properly investigated by the relevant authorities.

He said the issue of medicine shortages and related problems had been raised several times at various institutions under the Health Ministry.

Dr. Sanjeewa also referred to the President's previous remarks on the need to remove officials appointed during previous administrations who were responsible for areas linked to such problems.

He highlighted that the priority should be the patients and ensuring that essential medicines are available when they are needed.

Valuable medicines should not be allowed to expire or be destroyed because of administrative or regulatory disputes, he said.

Dr. Sanjeewa added that he was ready to work with the Health Minister and other officials to find practical solutions to the continuing problems in the country's health sector.
