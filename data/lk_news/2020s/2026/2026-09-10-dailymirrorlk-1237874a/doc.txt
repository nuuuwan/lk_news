Sri Lanka’s uphill battle to combat wildlife crimes

A recent video of a poacher waiting for his kill inside Yala National Park exposed the harrowing realities that happen even within Protected Areas. The video was captured by a safari jeep driver and submitted to the Wildlife Conservation Department. The incident was recorded on August 10 and the Department has launched a probe to apprehend the suspect.

This is one among many incidents that largely remain unreported when it comes to poaching attempts inside Sri Lanka’s national parks. According to conservationists, prevailing extreme heat conditions make wild animals more vulnerable to poaching. Sections 3 to 11 of the Flora and Fauna Protection Ordinance prohibit illegal entry into strict reserves. It is an offense to hunt, kill, take any animal outside of the protected area, or destroy eggs and nests. But despite these regulations, which according to conservationists are very much in abundance, there are various lapses when it comes to enforcing the law.

Sri Lanka has been recognised as one among 36 other global biodiversity hotspots. Biodiversity hotspots by definition are critical habitats for unique species that are at severe risk of disappearing. Therefore, no country can take pride in the fact that they are a biodiversity hotspot. In order to be recognised as a biodiversity hotspot, a region or country has to meet two criteria. These include high endemism and indications of habitat loss.

According to the Convention on Biological Diversity, a significant portion of Sri Lanka’s indigenous vertebrates and flowering plants are exclusive to the island. It states that about a quarter of the 3,000 angiosperms occurring in the country are endemic, along with 43% of indigenous vertebrates (excluding marine forms), with the highest rates recorded among amphibians, freshwater fishes and reptiles. In addition, there are over 800 endemic flowering plants and dozens of fern species heavily concentrated in the wet zone. Sri Lanka is home to around 34 official endemic birds such as the Sri Lankan Blue Magpie and the Serendib Scops Owl. They share the landscape with over 21 endemic mammals and over 50 endemic freshwater fish species.

Research studies continue on various spheres to trace the genetic diversity of flora and fauna, in attempts to revive species on the brink of extinction and to conserve vulnerable populations of various species. However, while painstaking conservation efforts are underway, Sri Lanka’s habitats have become vulnerable to the wildlife trade in two aspects; one as a source country for rare and endemic flora and fauna,  and as a transit hub for international smuggling networks.

Sri Lankans have also made headlines while attempting to smuggle rare and exotic wild animals. In March this year, a 56-year-old man arriving from Bangkok was fined Rs. 250,000 after being caught with 38 exotic pets—including guinea pigs, sugar gliders, and hedgehogs—hidden in his luggage. In July, a Sri Lankan was nabbed at Bangkok airport while trying to smuggle three ball pythons concealed in his underwear.

Another 40-year-old female from Colombo was arrested at BIA for smuggling six snakes, including a yellow anaconda, purchased in Bangkok and routed through India. Recently, a Sri Lankan man was apprehended at an international airport in Banten, Indonesia, after authorities discovered 101 birds packed into 15 small boxes inside his luggage.

The evolving wildlife trade, now with the presence of online sales and social media, has made it a lucrative illicit business for harvesters, poachers, smugglers, suppliers, traders, online sellers and other key figures in the supply chain.

According to the World Wildlife Fund, wildlife crime is a global crisis driven by powerful international criminal networks. Though exact figures are hard to pin down due to the covert nature of the trade, estimates suggest it generates over USD 20 billion annually. These illegal activities not only undermine rule of law while also fuelling corruption; they also pose a threat to nature and the survival of many species.

Sri Lanka, is challenged with systemic and operational hurdles in its efforts to counter and combat wildlife crimes. Without braving these challenges, wildlife conservation as a practice may one day become extinct!
