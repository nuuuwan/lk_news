Organic Farming Is the Future,  but It Requires Patience and Sustainability- Priyambada Jayakumar

Priyambada Jayakumar, a historian, columnist, and the bestselling author of M.S. Swaminathan: The Man Who Fed India, speaks to Daily Mirror on the rights of farmers, the future of farming and food security.

She was raised in India, specifically in Kolkata. Born to a Bengali mother and a Tamil father, she completed her schooling at La Martiniere for Girls, Kolkata. The capital city of Delhi soon beckoned, where she received her BA (Hons) degree in History from St. Stephen’s College, University of Delhi. A Tripos in Social and Political Sciences followed thereafter from the University of Cambridge. Subsequently, she attended Harvard University for a further period of study.

The ‘Man who fed India’ a biography of Prof M.S. Swaminathan, her uncle and the ‘Father of the Green Revolution in India’, the 2024 Bharat Ratna awardee and India’s founding father, tallest agricultural scientist, technocrat and scientific diplomat, is published by HarperCollins in his centennial year (2025-2026). The book has been widely covered by the mainstream national and international media and has consistently featured on national bestselling lists in the non-fiction category.

Q Sri Lanka had a terrible experience in 2022 in attempting to introduce organic farming. What do you actually mean by eco-friendly agriculture?

Now, farming is, whether you live in America, India, or Sri Lanka, the ability to feed yourself means one thing and only one thing. It means you’re free.

The minute you become unable to feed yourself and have to import fertiliser, food or other agricultural inputs, your food security is threatened. Your national sovereignty and national security get threatened.

Now, the biggest problem facing humanity today, in the wake of the conclusion of COP 31, is the fact that our world has already lost time to turn back the clock when it comes to climate change.

Keeping that in mind, anything that involves using nature for our own benefit, and farming is perhaps the most active form of harnessing nature for our sustenance, means that agriculture must be adaptable.

Here I talk about M.S. Swaminathan, who gave India a green revolution in the 1960s, but was aware that the green revolution itself wasn’t sustainable because it was done with the help of importing fertilisers. It was a water and a fertiliser guzzler.

The Green Revolution worked, and the yields per hectare tripled and quadrupled. India produced more grain in four years than it had done in 4,000 years of human history, simply because more fertilisers and water were added.

Over time, over fertilisation, over watering and depletion of the water table mean rising salinity and chemicals in the food system. You have carcinogens that get dumped into the ground. We needed the Green Revolution in the 1960s. Otherwise, Mrs. Gandhi was at the U.S.’s mercy for food security, because we were importing food under the public law, PL 480 food scheme. We were not able to grow our own crops. Like Sri Lanka, India was also a colony.

And under the British crown, agriculture wasn’t a huge priority. We built civil services, railways and the rest of it, but agriculture wasn’t given national importance. India, when it was free in 1947, was born in hunger. We were still a hungry nation. From 1947 to 1967, at the time when India was described as a people who sat with a begging bowl, it was Professor Swaminathan’s conviction that we had to go from the begging bowl to the breadbasket. The only way to do that was the Green Revolution.

The Green Revolution was also an ecological disaster. It served its purpose, but it wasn’t ecologically sustainable. It might have been economically sustainable, though.

So M.S. Swaminathan in 1968 advocated for something called the evergreen revolution, which was production in perpetuity without additional ecological harm. That is when your question arises: how important is organic farming or what is the meaning of ecological farming?

Ancient agricultural wisdom and modern science must work together to promote sustainable farming

Q The ecological aspect is compromised when you use more and more chemicals. Isn’t it?

Yes, the ecological aspect is compromised. In a world where you have too many mouths to feed, don’t forget India, Sri Lanka and even China are countries with small landowners.

We don’t have massive landholdings like in America. To get so much more from a small plot of land means chemicals are added to the ground to top up the yield.

But that comes at an ecological cost. With climate change, that is a huge problem. Climate change makes you realise just how much we’ve exploited nature without giving anything back.

Nature is cyclical. If you take anything out of it, you should actually put it back in. It is the cyclic form of ecology, the blend of ecological awareness and economic sustainability, which underlines organic farming or an organic ecological system.

Now, the problem with that is that anything organic tends to be much slower. That is when the profits get eaten away. When the profits get eaten away, you don’t have the kind of harvest you want.

When you don’t have the kind of harvest you want, you’re unable to feed your country or its people. Of course, population is a huge issue for us in India, but not so much in Sri Lanka.

When I talk about organic [farming}, I just don’t mean non-fertilisers, non-chemical. I also mean ancient wisdom – how our ancestors grew food. They did not have access to chemical fertilisers.

The population, of course, is a different matter altogether. The population intensity is much higher. The population expectation and the population explosion are that much higher.

But it doesn’t take away from the fact that ancient wisdom, agroecology or agro-biodiversity. In the past, we were able to grow food without exploiting or poisoning nature. I think it is time to go back, take a step back and look at our ancient systems of wisdom.

Those ancient systems offer many answers. We saw this in Indonesia in the 1980s, where Swaminathan worked with the government to phase out pesticides and adopt natural methods of pest control. It worked. You need patience for any ancient system. You need patience for an organic system. The reason why organic systems don’t work is also because people don’t have patience. In a highly commercialised economy where food is a commodity traded all across, patience is a slipping virtue. The thrust on organic farming is a genuine need. But it is not going to take off so easily.

It is not going to be adapted so quickly by countries because profit margins are important. But the climate crisis forces us to take a step back.

You have to look at the long-term impact of these measures. The short term is that you get your profits. The long term is that you are sustained. You are able to live. You are able to regenerate systems. So I think it’s a double-edged sword.

You have to be ecologically aware and economically sustainable as well. The two sometimes marry. That marriage is a long one.

It’s a marriage which doesn’t give you instant children. It gives you children in the next 20 years.

Q What do you think of scientific agriculture? Can processed organic manure, using modern agricultural technology, supply the same level of nutrients as chemical fertilisers such as urea? Is this already being practised in India or elsewhere?

Yes, it does. I mean that is called village science- village wisdom. It’s something which has happened in India for years.

But again, farming has become a commercial activity. And when you have a commercial activity takes over, then profit margins come in. The farmer is then not willing to wait.

That is where I think you need to look at the long-term benefits. Government support is also very important. In agriculture, especially in India, the farmer looks towards the government for a lot of support and a lot of respite.

Scientists have good ideas. Traditional ancient systems of wisdom tell us that these are good things to do. But until governments come and give you that kind of support, poor farmers are going to do what they can to provide food on the table for their children and their families.

The greater worry today is the sustainability and the economic viability of farming in general.

At my book launch recently, we were talking about how nobody wants to really farm. Farming in general, as an occupation, is finding absolutely no takers. A farmer in India doesn’t want his children to be farmers, citing no economic viability.

Q At the same time, as farming becomes increasingly commercialised, the number of people engaged in agriculture is declining. However, a new category of agro-entrepreneurs is emerging, moving towards large-scale commercial farming. What are your views?

But they are few and far between. There is an imbalance. You have agro-entrepreneurs and agro-people, but their number is much smaller than the number of people who actually actively give up farming.

Q Actually, how serious is it for a country like India? Because India has to feed its own population?

Q Besides, a lot of other countries in the region also depend on India for food security. Now, for example, Sri Lanka depends on India for big onions and even sugar?

Food security anywhere is a problem. Economic viability of farming today is a big question mark. It’s not just a problem for India but a problem for Sri Lanka.

How do you make farming attractive to the population so that the next generation of farmers is secure? If farming is not made economically viable, who’s going to grow the food? Who’s going to feed the nation? In this context, issues such as land ownership and access to credit become much more significant.

In India, the greater trouble is sharecropping by tenant farmers. These are huge problems.

That’s where farming debt in India is. The great problem we have is farmer suicides. Especially in the western region, in the Baramati region in Maharashtra, at least 8 farmers commit suicide.

Q Why is this irony in the world when farmers are the breadwinners of the world?

At the same time, they are in abject poverty they commit suicide. We look at farmers through the eyes of pity. We look at farmers as people who depend on the state for handouts. We do not celebrate the farmer. We do not respect his wisdom or his work. That itself is a problem.

The minute you start feeling that a farmer is an object of pity, you lose sight of the fact that your essential food security depends on this humble farmer, the breadwinners of the world, to be treated so badly. That is the premise of my book. The National Commission of Farmers, which Professor Swaminathan chaired from 2004 to 2006, only looked at one thing: how to make farming economically viable and farmers socially acceptable.

Making agriculture economically viable is essential to attracting the next generation of farmers

Q What is the solution you advocate at this juncture? Is that scientific agriculture? Some countries like Israel and the Netherlands have been successful in scientific agriculture.

When you talk about Israel and the Netherlands, I think the greater question to answer is size. India is much larger than any of these countries. The larger your country, the more problems you have.

Geographically, we are much more disparate. Every part of India has a different geography, a different topography, a different climate and different agricultural needs. Smaller countries are able to do things much faster because size is in their favour.

Unless we make farming remunerative, we cannot attract and retain people in agriculture. The C2+50% formula, recommended by the National Commission on Farmers, provides for a minimum support price at 50% above the comprehensive cost of production.

Q How can farming be made more secure when Sri Lankan farmers face threats from wild elephants and other wildlife without adequate insurance protection?

Farming has historically been one of the areas that has connected India and Sri Lanka. In India, too, farmers encounter wild animals and face the risk of being injured or otherwise harmed. Nature offers us its bounty, but that very bounty, which ensures our food security and puts food on our tables, reflects two things: nature’s generosity and the farmer’s contribution. We must be grateful for both.

The Tamil equivalent of Thanksgiving is Pongal, which is celebrated here. We have festivals that honour farmers, agricultural produce and nature’s bounty. However, sadly, we do not have a corresponding legal framework to protect farmers’ rights. I think that is where the problem lies.

Unless nations and governments take it upon themselves to safeguard farmers’ rights, these issues will remain unresolved. Look at the National Commission on Farmers, whose recommendations were drafted by Professor M.S. Swaminathan between 2004 and 2006, during the Manmohan Singh government. They were unable to translate those recommendations into law. Subsequently, Prime Minister Narendra Modi also failed to turn them into legislation.

Again, I return to the fact that our forefathers believed farmers and nature’s bounty should be celebrated and protected. However, today’s governments and administrations do not seem to share that belief. I think that is where the problem lies.

Whether it is farmers being attacked by wild elephants and other animals, their crops being destroyed, the lack of government support or the younger generation losing interest in farming, all these issues ultimately come down to the need for government intervention. In ancient times, although systems of governance were different, kingdoms recognised the wisdom of the land and the importance of living in harmony with natural forces.

That’s why there are so many religions which honour the sun.
