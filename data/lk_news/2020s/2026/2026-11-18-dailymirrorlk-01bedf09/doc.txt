The biggest lacuna in the Budget 2027

Sri Lanka’s education policymakers, perhaps owing to the nature of bureaucratic culture, have failed to grasp or are wilfully blind to the long-term degenerative impact of the country’s low tertiary education

Some time back, President Anura Kumara Dissanayake told the party faithful and curious onlookers at his political rallies that the government would build 50 new technical and vocational colleges. That was a solemn and well-meaning undertaking, though I would say given Sri Lanka’s painfully low Gross Enrolment ratio in tertiary education ( 20.7%), the country needs at least 200 new institutions of tertiary education to generate vocational and technical education opportunities for an additional 200,000 intake of youth.

Still, the president’s promise was a good first step, which if implemented correctly add additional 50,000 annual intake into tertiary and vocational education. Alas, the budget 2027 he delivered last week makes no mention of any of them. Instead, all you have is Rs 11 billion in capital investment earmarked to ‘upgrade facilities, update curricula, integrate new technologies, and expand public-private partnerships across the vocational sector’.

Of the said capital investment, Rs. 1.5 billion has been allocated for 2027 into a Rs. 6 billion project to modernise 25 selected Technical Colleges across 25 districts.

In comparison, the government has allocated Rs 451 billion for two expressways: Kurunegala—Galewela and Kahathuduwa—Ingiriya. Bimal Ratnayaka, Transport and Highways and Urban Development Minister, touts it as being funded 100% by public funds sans foreign loans. This is where misplaced priorities in limited resource allocation come into play. While Highways matter, very much so, stunted human capital already has a pernicious impact on the country and the overall economy.

Sri Lanka’s education policymakers, perhaps owing to the nature of bureaucratic culture, have failed to grasp or are wilfully blind to the long-term degenerative impact of the country’s low tertiary education.  Sri Lanka’s Gross Enrolment Ratio (GER) in tertiary education, i.e. the total number of students enrolled in higher education (regardless of age) divided by the total population of the official college-age group (typically 19 to 23 years old),  is a mere 20.7%. The global average is 54.38%.

These numbers reveal the quality of human capital, upward mobility and long-term economic modernisation. More than any other social indicator, GER in tertiary education has the most significant positive correlation with the long-term economic position of a country. While in rich countries,  more people stay in higher education, it is also true that aspiring nations tend to offer more higher education opportunities for their youth. Sri Lanka sadly trails behind even its South Asian peers. India’s Tertiary GER is 34%

I would say this is a sad but predictable outcome of Sri Lanka’s misguided quasi-state monopoly in higher education. Take, for instance, a kid born in 2006-2007. The total number of births in that year was 386,573. ( Department of Census). These kids would come of age well after the end of the war and therefore should have a better shot at success than most of us who preceded their generation.  However, the reality is damning.

Eighteen (18) per cent of children of that birth cohort dropped out of school before GCE O/levels. Another 21 per cent failed O/Levels and dropped out.  Meaning that by the time of Advanced Levels, nearly 40 per cent ( four in every ten children) born in 2006-7 had already dropped out of formal education. And another 4 per cent, who passed O/L, opted out of A/level. That means by the time A/levels, 43 per cent of children of their birth cohort are out of the school system.  What happened to them is anybody’s guess.

For generations, education policymakers in this country have been indifferent to this grotesque squandering of the country’s children— in some ways, that callous indifference is not different from what Velupillai Prabakaran did for the Northern youth.

The starting point of the fix would be to ensure that every child receives a mandatory 13-year education and, where necessary, hold parents accountable by law. The government says that is its intention too, but there is little noticeable progress. Still, even then, the generations before them, who missed out, should be offered the same opportunity to catch up. For that, the government should not only invest, but recognise upgrading and enhancing tertiary education as a major national policy initiative, in the same way some African countries immediately after their independence embarked on a national drive for literacy, before their misplaced statism cannibalised them.

And back to the kids of the 2006-7 birth cohort.  Fifty-seven per cent of them sat for the Advanced Levels, and 37.9 per cent ( 146,405) qualified to go to university, of which a mere 42,000 or around 11 per cent of the total birth cohort, got admitted to a public university. While a roughly similar number enter MOHE-approved non-state degree institutes and foreign-affiliated colleges annually.

Sri Lanka’s technical and vocational education centres (TVEC) also accommodate between 100, 000 students, including O/level leavers, though mostly for short- to medium-term courses. These institutions should be converted into the primary driver for inducting nearly 40 per cent of school dropouts at and before Ordinary levels.  Yet, successive governments have overlooked them, partly because empathy should come from association, whereas even the dumbest offspring of successive leaders had the luxury to migrate to greener pastures or, worse still, land a plum position in an SEO.

A proactive government, one would expect the NPP to be one such, should reorganise the existing Technical and Vocational centres into a coherent and uniform mechanism and upgrade the facilities and staff to induct 200,000 annually, including those who drop out by A/L and past generations who missed out on the opportunity.

With a US$ 1 billion investment, the president could establish a well-established network of technical and Vocational education, with the participation of well-meaning foreign partners and local business bodies. A two-year associate degree can drastically increase productivity and upward mobility of this cohort and Sri Lanka in general.

The second group, those who qualify for universities, deserve a different treatment. Though around 50 per cent of this group has some form of Higher education, another half fail to secure higher education opportunities matching their potential. Even for those who enter local universities, the stubborn truth is that none of them is within the top 1000 global universities.

This is a cruel jest of free university education, where the kids who could easily compete with the best of their peers anywhere in the world are condemned to sub-optimal local universities. To fix this precarious grip of mediocrity, the President could bring the top 3 or 4 local universities under a separate body and develop them in a model similar to China’s C-9 or the Russell Group universities. However, high-flying local academics do not publish, partly owing to a stagnant organisational culture, and many in the social sciences have been through an incestuous cycle of sub-par local degrees. To break this cycle, one must introduce best practices in global universities, which might not be possible without risking cripling trade union actions.

To address the dearth of quality academics in higher education, perhaps the government could allocate $ 50 million annually and send the  500 top- performing students for PhDs in the top 10 global universities in their discipline. On return, they would become better academics than their teachers. That is how countries like Singapore cultivated their talent.

The government can also set up a second tier of technological universities similar to India’s National Institutes of Technology (NIT) and also invite the top 300 global universities to open campuses in Sri Lanka and finance education through a tuition fee loan system.

Sri Lanka is today free of two major crippling economic and social handicaps: the war and the high population growth both cannibalised the living. With the country is now a Upper Middle income group, and the government touting of its savings, some of the government’s savings should be invested where it matter.  Without fixing education at all levels, Sri Lanka’s other potentials would be of little help in its long-term economic prospects.
