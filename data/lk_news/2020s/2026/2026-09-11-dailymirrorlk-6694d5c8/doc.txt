Sri Lanka cannot wait for next crisis to build reserves: Central Bank

Colombo, Sep. 11 (Daily Mirror) - Central Bank Governor Dr. Nandalal Weerasinghe yesterday delivered a strong case for Sri Lanka to build adequate foreign reserve buffers during favourable times, warning that waiting until the next external crisis strikes would be too late.

“Buffers must be built before they are needed. When a country is already facing an external crisis, it is too late to begin building reserves,” Weerasinghe said, drawing on hard lessons from Sri Lanka’s unprecedented economic crisis in 2022.

Delivering the keynote address at the inaugural Reserve Management Conference 2026 in Colombo, the Governor said the 2022 crisis demonstrated in the clearest terms what happens when a country enters a shock with inadequate external buffers.

When reserves fall to critically low levels, imports become constrained, debt servicing becomes difficult, exchange-rate pressures intensify, inflationary pressures can increase and confidence deteriorates.

More importantly, he said, the policy space available to respond to further shocks becomes severely constrained.

“Adequate reserves are not a luxury. They are an essential component of macroeconomic stability,” Weerasinghe stressed.

Sri Lanka has since undertaken a comprehensive process of macroeconomic stabilisation and structural reform, with the external sector strengthening substantially compared with the difficult period of 2022-2023.

However, Weerasinghe cautioned that rebuilding reserves is not a linear process, with buffers accumulated during good times capable of being depleted rapidly when external shocks emerge.

The pertinent question, he said, was therefore not merely how much reserves Sri Lanka has today, but how resilient and accessible those reserves are, how quickly they could be mobilised and whether they would be sufficient to withstand the next shock.

Weerasinghe said reserve accumulation is easiest when confidence is strong, capital is flowing, exports are growing and foreign exchange liquidity is abundant.

Ironically, he noted, these are precisely the periods when there is a temptation to assume favourable conditions will continue indefinitely.

“We should build buffers during good times because we know that good times will not last forever,” he said.

The Governor also stressed that rebuilding reserves cannot be pursued in isolation from the broader macroeconomic framework.

Excessive intervention could distort market signals, excessive monetary expansion could generate inflationary pressures, while excessive dependence on external borrowing could merely create future debt-service obligations.

For Sri Lanka, sustainable reserve accumulation must therefore be underpinned by stronger external-sector fundamentals as well as fiscal and monetary credibility.

“The most sustainable reserve accumulation strategy is therefore not simply to acquire reserves. It is to build an economy that naturally generates and retains foreign exchange,” Weerasinghe said.

The need for stronger buffers has become more pressing given heightened geopolitical uncertainty, trade tensions, volatile commodity prices, shifting interest-rate cycles and rapidly changing global capital flows.

As an energy-importing economy, Sri Lanka remains particularly vulnerable to a spike in global oil prices, which could quickly raise the import bill. At the same time, geopolitical tensions could hurt tourism and other sources of foreign exchange earnings.

Such a scenario could see foreign exchange outflows rise just as inflows weaken, creating precisely the type of pressure reserves are intended to absorb.

Weerasinghe stressed that sustainable reserve rebuilding ultimately requires sound macroeconomic fundamentals, policy credibility and institutional discipline, with no shortcut available to the process.

“We do not build reserves because we expect a crisis. We build reserves because we cannot know when the next crisis will come,” he said.
