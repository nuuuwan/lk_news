Trump’s midterm rout: A lesson in the limits of a superpower

The U.S. elections and subsequent policy decisions will therefore have a bearing on Sri Lanka, particularly in trade, investment, energy security and foreign relations

People across the world were glued to the media to see the outcome of the U.S. midterm elections. The United States, as the global economic powerhouse with unmatched military might, can make an impact across the world with just a stroke of the pen. The Israeli-U.S. war on Iran left countries such as Sri Lanka reeling, with little choice in the matter. It was a bitter experience. President Donald Trump was tested for his actions at the midterm elections. What happened to his party was not merely a defeat but a rout.

Democrats took control of both chambers of Congress, in what amounted to an astounding humiliation for a president whose political brand is built around the projection of power.

Two years ago, retaking the House looked like a genuine fight for Democrats, while the Senate appeared a long shot. However, the election results turned the political landscape upside down, with Democrats securing a majority in the Senate and comfortably taking the House. This was a major setback for a president who had sought to consolidate his political authority after returning to office.

The outcome is a reminder that even the most powerful political leaders are not immune to the verdict of the electorate. Trump, who often projects himself as a leader capable of bending institutions to his will, has been confronted with the reality of the American constitutional system. Congress has an important role in overseeing the administration, controlling public expenditure and scrutinising decisions relating to war and peace.

Be that as it may, the election result does not mean an immediate end to Trump’s presidency or his ability to pursue his political agenda. He remains in office until the end of his term. However, the Democrats will now have greater leverage to challenge his policies, conduct investigations and block legislative initiatives. His administration will also face greater scrutiny over its handling of the war, tariffs and other contentious issues. The final two years of his presidency could therefore be marked by political confrontation.

The economic dimension of the election cannot be overlooked. Trump campaigned on the promise of making America affordable again. Yet, tariffs on imports, coupled with the economic repercussions of the war with Iran, undermined that promise. American consumers ultimately bear part of the cost of tariffs through higher prices. Likewise, disruptions to global energy supplies affect households and businesses far beyond the region where a conflict takes place. The electorate appears to have sent a message that foreign policy adventurism and economic nationalism cannot be pursued without consequences at home.

The implications are not confined to the United States. In an increasingly interconnected world, decisions taken in Washington can reverberate across continents. The conflict involving Iran, Israel and the United States demonstrated how quickly a regional war could turn into a global economic crisis. Disruptions to energy supplies and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz sent shockwaves through international markets. Countries dependent on imported fuel, including Sri Lanka, were particularly vulnerable. They had to contend with rising import costs, pressure on foreign exchange reserves and the prospect of renewed inflation, despite having little influence over the decisions that led to the crisis.

For Sri Lanka, this is not an abstract geopolitical issue. Having undergone an unprecedented economic crisis in 2022, the country remains exposed to external shocks despite progress in economic stabilisation. A sharp increase in global petroleum prices can undermine fiscal calculations, increase transport and production costs and erode the purchasing power of ordinary people. No matter what, smaller economies cannot insulate themselves completely from decisions taken by major powers.

The U.S. elections and subsequent policy decisions will therefore have a bearing on Sri Lanka, particularly in trade, investment, energy security and foreign relations.

There is also the geopolitical dimension. Washington’s relations with China and India and its policies towards the Indian Ocean will remain important considerations for Colombo.

It is easier said than done. Sri Lanka has to learn from such experiences, strengthen its economic resilience and pursue a balanced and independent foreign policy. It cannot allow its national interests to become hostage to the political calculations of powerful countries.
