Does the Public Have Any Say in Sri Lanka's Budget? The Score Is 9 Out of 100

Colombo, Aug. 17 (Daily Mirror) - Sri Lanka is making notable progress in publishing fiscal data, yet ordinary citizens remain almost entirely locked out of shaping how public money is spent. According to the Open Budget Survey 2025, the island scored an encouraging 43 out of 100 for transparency, but a dismal 9 out of 100 for public participation. As preparations for the 2027 budget gather momentum, the government faces a critical test: moving beyond internal ministerial consultations to grant civil society a meaningful voice.

Sri Lanka’s budget got more transparent this year. The public still has almost no say in it.

That’s the takeaway from the Open Budget Survey 2025, the International Budget Partnership’s latest assessment of how governments around the world handle public money. The survey scored 82 countries on three separate measures: how much budget information they publish, how well parliament and auditors check government spending, and how much room ordinary citizens get to shape decisions before they’re made. Sri Lanka’s participation score landed at 9 out of 100.

This isn’t about whether people can read the budget once it’s approved. The participation pillar asks whether anyone outside government gets to shape the budget before it’s finalized: public hearings on spending proposals, formal consultation processes, a way for citizens to comment on what auditors find. It’s the difference between a government that publishes documents and one that actually listens.

Sri Lanka does very little of this. Parliament doesn’t take public testimony on the budget or on audit reports. There’s no standing mechanism for civil society or ordinary citizens to feed into how spending decisions get made. The National Audit Office comes closest to offering an entry point, rated as providing “adequate” oversight, but it isn’t really built as a public participation channel either.

Sri Lanka’s other two scores moved in a more encouraging direction. Transparency rose to 43 out of 100, up from 37 in 2023, continuing a bumpy run that saw the country reach 47 in 2019 before dropping to 30 in 2021.

Oversight came in at 56, mostly on the strength of that same audit office. The Enacted Budget, the final version passed into law, scored 89 out of 100 for how thoroughly it’s published. And for the first time, Sri Lanka put out a Citizens Budget this year: a simplified version of the national budget aimed at the general public.

None of that closes the participation gap. If anything, it makes the gap more obvious. The government has gotten noticeably better at putting information out, without getting any better at inviting a response to it.

A few gaps explain a good chunk of the low score. Sri Lanka doesn’t publish a Pre-Budget Statement, the document meant to lay out the government’s fiscal plans before the budget itself is drafted, so citizens have something to react to early. It hasn’t produced a Mid-Year Review, which would show whether spending is tracking to plan partway through the fiscal year. And the In-Year Reports it does publish tend to come out late, which cuts into how useful they are for anyone trying to follow spending as it happens.

The survey’s authors also note that Sri Lanka has no independent fiscal institution: the kind of body that, in other countries, gives outside analysis of budget decisions without being part of government itself.

The window this criticism applies to is opening right now. The government has set November 12, 2026 for the Budget Speech, the second reading of the 2027 Appropriation Bill, after a first reading on October 7. The draft bill goes to Cabinet on September 14 and is due in the Government Gazette on September 18, with committee-stage debate running from November 21 to December 14, according to the published parliamentary timetable.

President Anura Kumara Dissanayake, who also holds the finance portfolio, has already started a round of pre-budget review meetings with individual ministries, beginning with the Ministry of Justice on August 5, after a broader session in July where he told officials that delays on policy decisions would not be tolerated in the lead-up to the budget. Government statements have described this as part of “a more consultative approach to public expenditure planning.”

So far, the consultation reported has been between the President and ministry officials, not the public. There’s no indication yet of a published Pre-Budget Statement open for comment, public hearings on spending priorities, or a way for citizens or civil society groups to weigh in before the draft goes to Cabinet in September. Whether that changes before November 12 is, in effect, the next real test of the participation gap the Open Budget Survey documented.

On transparency specifically, Sri Lanka still sits below the regional average of 49, trailing Nepal (52), Pakistan (45), and India (44), though it stays ahead of Bangladesh (37). Participation figures for regional peers weren’t broken out the same way in the material reviewed for this piece, but a score of 9 out of 100 is low by almost any comparison.

The International Budget Partnership’s recommendations for Sri Lanka are fairly specific: publish the Pre-Budget Statement and In-Year Reports online and on schedule, start producing Mid-Year Reviews, and let parliament hear public testimony on both budget proposals and audit findings. None of that requires new legislation or a change to the country’s fiscal framework. It requires the government to open a door that, so far, it has mostly kept shut.
