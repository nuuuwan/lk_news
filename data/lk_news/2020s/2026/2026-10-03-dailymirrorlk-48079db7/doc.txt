Rethinking Adequate Housing  for All

Where communities can safely remain, upgrading infrastructure and living conditions may be more appropriate than relocation.

World Habitat Day 2026 calls for a new understanding of housing — one that goes beyond four walls and a roof to create safe, affordable, inclusive, climate-resilient and well-connected communities

At first glance, the answer seems straightforward. A home is a place with walls, a roof, a door and a space where a family can live. But in today’s rapidly changing world, that definition is no longer sufficient.

A house may provide shelter, but is it really an adequate home if the family cannot afford to maintain it? Can we call a house adequate if it is located in a flood-prone area, far from schools and hospitals, without reliable public transport or safe roads? These questions are becoming increasingly important as countries struggle with rising housing costs, rapid urbanisation, population change, environmental degradation and the growing impacts of climate change.

It is against this background that World Habitat Day 2026, observed on the first Monday of October, focuses on the theme “Adequate Housing for All.” The theme reminds us that housing is not merely a physical structure. It is closely connected to human dignity, health, safety, livelihoods, accessibility, environmental quality and the overall quality of life. For Sri Lanka, this message is particularly relevant.

Sri Lanka has made considerable progress in expanding housing and basic infrastructure. According to the 2024 Census of Population and Housing, the country has more than six million occupied housing units. A large majority are classified as single houses, reflecting the country’s predominantly individual-household housing pattern.

Yet, the existence of a house does not automatically mean that adequate housing has been achieved. The housing challenge is becoming more complex. For some households, the problem is the cost of land and construction. For others, it is the quality of the dwelling, overcrowding, uncertain tenure or inadequate infrastructure.

The 2024 Census also recorded more than 350,000 one-room housing units nationally. In the urban sector, one-room housing units account for a larger proportion of the housing stock than the national average.Behind these statistics are real families, real aspirations and real challenges.

Therefore, the question Sri Lanka needs to ask is not simply: “How many houses do we have?” The more important question is: “How adequately can people live in those houses?”

One of the most important aspects of adequate housing is location. Imagine a family receiving an affordable house on the outskirts of a city. The house itself may be structurally sound and relatively inexpensive. But if the nearest school is several kilometres away, employment opportunities are limited, public transport is unreliable and healthcare facilities are difficult to reach, the apparent affordability of the house may hide another burden.

Adequate housing should provide reasonable access to employment, education, healthcare, transportation, markets, recreation and other essential services. For this reason, housing policy and urban planning must work together.

Instead of asking only “Where is land available for housing?”, planners need to ask: “Where can people live safely and affordably while remaining connected to the opportunities and services they need?”

For many families, affordability is one of the greatest barriers to adequate housing. The cost of a home is not limited to the price of the building. It includes land, construction materials, labour, utilities, maintenance, transportation and other recurring expenses.

Another major challenge is climate change. A house that was considered safe in the past may face very different risks in the future. Flooding, landslides, coastal hazards, extreme rainfall, heat and other environmental stresses are increasingly influencing where communities can safely live. Sri Lanka’s experience with extreme weather events has demonstrated the vulnerability of settlements and infrastructure. When a disaster strikes, low-income households are often particularly vulnerable because they may have fewer financial resources to repair or rebuild their homes. Therefore, climate resilience must become a central component of housing policy.

Adequate housing must also reflect demographic and social change. A home that is comfortable for a young family may become difficult to use as its residents grow older. Housing design should therefore increasingly incorporate the principles of universal design. Step-free entrances, safer bathrooms, handrails, wider doorways, adequate lighting, accessible pathways and appropriate ventilation are relatively simple features that can make homes safer and more comfortable. Housing adequacy is also closely related to security of tenure. A family may have a house but still feel insecure if it does not have sufficient protection from displacement or uncertainty about its right to remain on the land. Informal settlements often emerge because formal housing systems cannot meet the needs of all households. Such settlements should not automatically be viewed only as problems to be removed.

Perhaps the biggest change required is a shift in thinking. We need to move from the idea of a “housing project” to the idea of a “liveable community.”

Modern technology provides Sri Lanka with powerful tools to address these challenges. Geographic Information Systems (GIS) can help planners identify suitable locations for housing by integrating multiple layers of information. The more important question becomes: Is this location suitable for people to live in safely, affordably and sustainably?

Technology alone, however, cannot solve the housing challenge. Planning must also be participatory. People who live in a community possess knowledge that may not appear in official maps or datasets. This local knowledge should be incorporated into housing and settlement planning. Moving towards adequate housing for all requires a coordinated and long-term approach. First, integrate housing with spatial planning.Housing development should be closely connected with land-use planning, transportation, environmental management and economic development.

Second, strengthen risk-sensitive housing planning.Flood, landslide, coastal and other hazard information should be incorporated into decisions about where new settlements are developed.

Third, expand affordable housing choices.Policy should recognise that home ownership is not the only housing solution. Affordable rental housing, incremental housing, upgrading and community-based approaches can also play important roles.

Fourth, improve existing settlements.Where communities can safely remain, upgrading infrastructure and living conditions may be more appropriate than relocation.

Fifth, promote climate-resilient housing. Housing standards should consider changing rainfall patterns, flooding, heat, water management, energy efficiency and other climate-related risks.

Sixth, make neighbourhoods accessible. Housing developments should be connected to public transport, pedestrian networks, schools, healthcare and employment.

Seventh, design for different generations. Children, older people, persons with disabilities and other vulnerable groups should be considered in both housing and neighbourhood design.

Eighth, make better use of spatial data. GIS, remote sensing and other digital technologies can support evidence-based decisions about housing location, risk and accessibility.

Ninth, strengthen community participation. Residents should have a meaningful voice in decisions that affect their homes and neighbourhoods.

Finally, measure what really matters. The success of a housing programme should not be judged only by the number of houses constructed. We should also ask whether people are safer, healthier, better connected and able to afford their homes.

World Habitat Day 2026 provides an opportunity to reconsider what we mean when we say “housing for all.” It should not mean simply putting a roof over every person’s head. It should mean creating the conditions in which every person can live with dignity.

A child should be able to grow up in a safe and healthy environment. An older person should be able to move safely around the home and neighbourhood. A person with a disability should be able to access public services and transportation. A working family should not have to choose between paying for housing and paying for food, education or healthcare. A community should not have to fear losing its home every time heavy rainfall arrives. And no family should be forced to live in a location where basic services and opportunities are beyond reasonable reach simply because that is the only housing they can afford.

The challenge is to create safe homes, inclusive neighbourhoods and resilient communities. As we mark World Habitat Day 2026, perhaps the most important question is not:

It is: “What kind of places do we want people to call home?”

(Dr. Chathurika Jayasinghe is Senior Lecturer, Department of Geography, University of Ruhuna).
