Without internal  unity, Sri Lanka cannot navigate external turbulence-  former Singapore Foreign Minister

Sri Lanka should be cognisant of India’s strategic interests- not to take direction from India, but to anticipate concerns

I think looking ahead, Sri Lanka should move closer to ASEAN

Former Singapore Foreign Minister George Yeo, who was in Sri Lanka, shared his views on current global developments and the way forward for small and medium powers such as Sri Lanka in the whirlpool of events. He was here at the invitation of the leading think tank ‘Geopolitical Cartographer’ founded by former President Ranil Wickremesinghe. It is a body dedicated to studying geopolitics and the growing role of the Indian Ocean in global political, security and economic spheres.  From September 1988 to May 2011, he served 23 years in the Singapore Government and was Minister for Information and the Arts, Minister for Health, Minister for Trade & Industry and Minister for Foreign Affairs.  Mr Yeo studied Engineering at Cambridge University on a President’s Scholarship, graduating with a Double First in 1976, and became a Signals Officer in the Singapore Armed Forces. After graduating from the Singapore Command and Staff College in 1979, he was posted to the Republic of Singapore Air Force. He graduated with an MBA (Baker Scholar) from the Harvard Business School in 1985. He was appointed Chief-of-Staff of the Air Staff (1985-1986) and Director of Joint Operations and Planning in the Defence Ministry (1985-1988), attaining the rank of Brigadier-General.  Mr Yeo was a member of the Pontifical Commission for Reference on the Economic-Administrative Structure of the Holy See in 2013-2014 and the Vatican Council for the Economy from February 2014 to July 2020. He said that Sri Lanka needs unity in domestic politics for the better advancement of its foreign policy. According to him, Sri Lanka should have a dynamic neutrality in its foreign policy. Excerpts

QIn the current world order, we see superpower rivalry. At the same time, we see the United States (U.S.) and China warming up to each other. What does it indicate for the future of the world?

Well, we are moving towards a multipolar configuration, where there are different centres of power, and the relationships are dynamic.

Right now we have Chinese President Xi Jinping in Washington, and China-US relations seem to be moving to a pattern of strategic stability, which is good for all of us. We don’t know how long it will last. It’s based on each side having strong cards to use against the other.

Therefore, it is creating a kind of a hard balance, which may be good for the world. Because of that, I think every other part of the world will re-triangulate, given the fact that the US-China relationship is stabilising. India will begin to normalise its relations with China, and that explains why Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi had this very long handshake with Xi Jinping in Delhi just 10 days ago.

I think the Europeans will begin to act more firmly in their own interests, and find some ways to move closer to China, and eventually reach an equilibrium in Russia.

QWithout being a casualty of this rivalry, how can small, middle powers like Sri Lanka navigate these intricacies in this world?

Well, we should be very clear about the way the world is going, and try to make sense of it as best as we can. There are a lot of uncertainties, of course.

We have very little say in what they do. We have very little influence on the outcomes. But what we can do is to decide for ourselves, in our position, that we have agency. Do I want to be closer to one or to the other? Do I strike a balance? That is within our own control. What do we do?

QSo, for a country like Sri Lanka, does this foreign policy really amount to neutrality? Or does neutrality, in the practical sense, mean that Sri Lanka should develop strategic partnerships with different powers, such as India and China, based on different areas of mutual interest, while maintaining its strategic autonomy?

I think for Sri Lanka, it’s better to have a dynamic neutrality. So India, of course, will always be very important to you.

But you must have diversification, which means that you need relationships with the U.S., China and others so that you have more flexibility to deal with any one major power.

If you are locked into a static position, I think that’s very uncomfortable, because the rest of the world is changing.

Q Now, actually, we see India and China warming up to each other, with President Xi Jinping coming to New Delhi to attend the BRICS summits and all. So for a country like Sri Lanka, what are the takeaways from this warming up?

Well, Sri Lanka should always have a good relationship with India, but at the same time, retain its independence. Given the fact that the Chinese economy will continue to grow, it’s a huge opportunity for Sri Lanka to build its infrastructure, to attract investments, to promote tourism, and to have a more dynamic economy. So you must have a good relationship with China. You don’t want to choose.

In order not to choose, you must be cognisant of India’s strategic interests- not to take direction from India, but to anticipate what the concerns are, so that you avoid hard rocks along your passage.

QYou once mentioned that the strength of ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations) is its neutrality. What do you mean by that?

We don’t have an alliance with one against the other. But of course, if any major power pushes too hard, it will find ASEAN shifting its weight to counterbalance the excessive pressure. Very dynamic.

And our preference is always to retain neutrality, to retain balance.

Q ASEAN is a missed opportunity for Sri Lanka because there were opportunities in the past for Sri Lanka also to be part of ASEAN as a member. What do you think?

Well, I won’t make too much of that because the world was very different at that time. I think looking ahead, Sri Lanka should move closer to ASEAN.

Well, there are economic opportunities. Yes. It’s good to huddle with like-minded people, especially when in the room there are big guys.

QAll the Theravada Buddhist countries are part of ASEAN. Is that one reason?

There’s a Theravada connection. There’s a Muslim connection. There’s a Christian connection. And Sri Lanka should not be overly Buddhist in its presentation, but should be multi-religious, multi-ethnic. I think Sri Lanka is.

Q How do you see the importance of the Indian Ocean growing from security, political and economic perspectives?

Well, the growth of India economically, it must be good for Sri Lanka. I mean, if India were a basket case, how would it help Sri Lanka? If India is a dynamic economy, there will be limitless opportunities for Sri Lanka. The question is the security dimension.

India, as it becomes more powerful economically, will exert a strong, powerful influence on you, which will cause you to lose some of your own autonomy. Therefore, diversification is very important. This is exactly the calculus in the minds of ASEAN countries when they face a growing China.

Q Now, looking at Sri Lanka’s current positioning in the region and the wider international system, how would you assess the country’s strategic position today? Sri Lanka, like Singapore and Dubai, has historically had considerable advantages arising from its location. Yet, despite these advantages, it has not fully realised its potential for economic transformation and development. In your view, what are the key factors that prevented Sri Lanka from translating its strategic location into sustained development, and does the current geopolitical environment offer Sri Lanka another opportunity to do so?

I think for Sri Lanka, the most important concerns are domestic, not external. I think: get your domestic politics right, get your economy moving again, put your finances in good order. Without that internal unity, you can’t navigate external turbulence.

People may say that Sri Lanka should have a more active foreign policy. But if internally you’re divided, a more active foreign policy doesn’t help you.

You must get your act together domestically first. Now, there must be enough consensus in your elite structure that whatever political party you’re from, whatever religion you espouse, you see a common destiny for all Sri Lankans. There are therefore limits to democratic competition. In the end, we’re in the same boat. Don’t rock that boat. We’ve got to navigate this boat down dangerous rapids.

QHow do you define this concept of non-alignment in the current context?

We have no natural inclination to align one against the other. If you align yourself against India, you’re asking trouble for yourself. If you align yourself with India rigidly, you’re self-optimising for yourself.

QWill the BRICS be a forum that unites the entire Global South to present its case internationally? No, Singapore is not a member of BRICS.

I think it should, but it is not. I can guess why. Because the Americans are not very excited about BRICS.

We need Americans, so we prefer to take a more detached position. Maybe Sri Lanka has an equivalent consideration. I don’t know.

Maybe you are too internally preoccupied to want to expend nervous energy on external alliances. But calculate your own self-interest!
