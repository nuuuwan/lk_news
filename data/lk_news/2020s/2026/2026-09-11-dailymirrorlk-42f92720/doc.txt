Strongest El Niño in 75 years?

Colombo, Sep. 12 (Daily Mirror) - Sri Lanka is facing the potential impact of an unusually strong El Niño, with the Department of Meteorology warning that the phenomenon could significantly disrupt normal weather patterns, bringing heavy rainfall later this year followed by a risk of water shortages early next year.

Director General of Meteorology Ajith Wijemannage said global El Niño conditions have strengthened considerably over the past month, with sea surface temperatures in the eastern equatorial Pacific rising sharply during August.

“Last month, we estimated a 69% probability that this El Niño would surpass all events recorded since 1950. The latest forecasts now indicate a much higher likelihood of it becoming the strongest in the past 75 years.

“Rainfall in Sri Lanka is expected to remain relatively low over the next two weeks, but conditions will gradually intensify from late September, with above-average rainfall expected across the island in October and November. A positive Indian Ocean Dipole is also expected to prolong the wet conditions through December.”

The probability of the event developing into a strong El Niño between October and December has risen to 98%, he said.

However, the wetter conditions could be followed by a significant decline in precipitation during January, February and March, raising the risk of water shortages in several parts of the country.

Wijemannage said that El Niño should not be viewed simply as a prolonged dry spell, but as a major disruption to Sri Lanka’s normal seasonal weather patterns.

“When people hear about El Niño, they usually associate it purely with a drought. But for Sri Lanka, it is not simply a drought; it is a major shift in our seasonal monsoon patterns. There is an initial dry spell, followed by heavy rainfall during the second inter-monsoon in October and November, and then another dry period after January,” he said.

He also warned that rising global temperatures would keep both daytime and nighttime temperatures elevated across the island.

“Even when it rains, the temperature will not decrease significantly. You will still feel elevated heat,” he said.

In response, a Cabinet-level committee comprising several key state institutions has begun meeting regularly to prepare water-management strategies and strengthen measures to mitigate potential weather-related risks.
