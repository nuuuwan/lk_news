Anti-Corruption  drive needs a bottoms-up approach as well

Even in advanced industrial societies, worker dissatisfaction can lead to violent protests (Photo courtesy: AFP)

Last week, we discussed the how and why of the government’s anti-corruption drive. This has been a headline-grabbing priority for the past months, carried out with a sense of urgency and moral rectitude.

But there is one fundamental flaw in this exercise. It’s a top-down approach, ignoring the bottoms-up side, which is the other half of the coin. No matter how many top level politicians and bureaucrats are charged and convicted, long-term success will be hard to achieve without addressing the lower stratas of society as well.

The prevailing official view maybe that, if one sets an example at the top, the lower echelons would follow that example. But this is arguable at best. It was pointed out last week in this column that large-scale corruption became the norm in the country after the post-1977 economic liberalisation, when corruption – commissions, bribes and money-laundering – lost the stigma it had in previous decades. One may even say it became socially acceptable. For those who wanted to be upwardly mobile, it was the easiest way forward.

As it was pointed out earlier,  this corruption was visible at all levels – from ministerial level to clerks, whereas in the pre-1977 era it was practised more at the lower levels. This was due to poverty. In the liberal economy, go-getter era, It’s became the prerogative of the top levels. They made more money – incredible amounts of it, whereas the lower levels in was modest. For example, if a technical officer in some construction project bought a man, that could be riches from the point of view of those dreaming of buying something bigger than a bike or three wheeler. But that isn’t wealth compared to having an overseas bank account.

In the pre-1977 years,  corruption was mostly at the lower levels, not at the top. I remember a story by a journalist who went to meet a 1970-1977 government minister in his office. The minister was taken unawares as the visitor entered, and tried to hide the new wrist watch he was wearing; he admitted with an embarrassed laugh it was a gift from somebody!

Today, such incidents look laughable. But corruption was rampant at lower levels – the Co-Operative system was notorious for that. Or departmental clerks and other functionaries took small bribes. I remember overhearing a businessman boasting that he managed to escape being booked for a traffic offense by handing over his license (a booklet in the 1970s) to a constable with a fifty rupee note folded inside.

But, while condemning people for accepting bribes, one should also ask why they demanded bribes in the first place. At the lower levels, this was due to poverty. For someone earning Rs. 1000-2000 a month in the 1970s (many earned less), an extra one hundred rupees or even fifty was a godsend. Today, basic salaries in both government and private sectors for minor or middle level functionaries may look big by comparison – Rs.  30,000 to 60,000, sometimes a bit more. But they become insignificant given current inflation levels and cost of living. For someone with a family, an extra 5000, 2000 or even a thousand is what Rs. 100 was to a wage earner three to four decades ago.

Poverty is not given here as an excuse for corruption. But it should be understand as the reason. Most of those caught and convicted by the Bribery Commission in the past were ‘small fry.’

Since 2024, big fish are getting caught. This is fine, but the government – or any government which comes to power – should understand a society cannot be corruption free until it is clean at both ends – this requires a bottoms up approach as well as a top down one. Right now, we see the top down approach going ahead at full power.

The solution is not to start jailing people for ‘minor’ bribes. The solution is to ensure worker contentment so that people do not have to resort to bribes. After all, it is humiliating to both parties – giver as well as taker.

Raising the standard of living may sound utopian at the best of times, but it should be overwhelmingly the primary goal. What has happened over the past decades is that the standard of living of a certain class has been raised, often by improper means.

The best way to improve everyone’s standard of living and reduce rich-poor gaps is through better productivity. This is a problem all modern societies have grappled with, big or small, industrial or agricultural, developed or ‘less developed.’

In Japan, Asia’s powerful till the rise of China, workers were rarely known to strike. But that did not mean universal worker contentment in Japan. Union activity was kept at a low ebb by managements, and workers in Japanese factories often resorted to wearing headbands sprouting protest slogans as they worked. Japan’s unique social cohesion may have produced this compromise. But Japanese salaries were often at satisfactory levels compared to other Asian countries.

In the former Soviet Union, the workers did not get lucrative salaries, but were compensated with holidays and other forms of compensation given by the state, including state-provided housing. But that arrangement did not lead to universal worker satisfaction, either. The result was that quality control in Soviet factories was notoriously uneven. While top priority areas – the space programme, aeronautics and armaments industry – was taken care of, consumer goods suffered, which eventually led to the downfall of the Soviet Union.

In Britain, one could argue that too much union control of British industry led to worker-management issues which played a role in the downfall of some of Britain’s key industries, for example the automobile industry (though this is not the only reason). The resulting chaos eventually led to Thatcherism which led to greater rich-poor gaps and did not lead to any industrial revival.

France, too, has seen a lot of strikes and unrest in recent years due to worker dissatisfaction. In the United States, a country with enormous rich-poor gaps, worker dissatisfaction has led to sabotage by workers in automobile factories. But in the above-mentioned industrial societies, workers have received satisfactory wages which, at least in times of manageable inflation, did not force them to seek extra incomes elsewhere. In other words, while what they experienced was relative poverty, it wasn’t actual poverty, which is what many people are experiencing in this country.

As we have seen, worker dissatisfaction exists in more affluent societies as well. But corruption will assume other forms rather than soliciting bribes. The industrial sabotage in some American factories mentioned above is one example. In our case, sabotage has always taken two forms – political violence which cost the state billions of rupees, or low productivity, misuse of state of company assets etc. or outright theft.

The solution isn’t spending more and more on surveillance systems but to find ways to lower worker or employee dissatisfaction, whether this concerns blue or white collar jobs. Raising salaries is the most effective way of doing this. Starting salaries are still shockingly low in many sectors, and the private sector is a major contributor to this exploitation of labour. Managements will argue they cannot pay more until their revenue is increased through greater productivity, and this is a tough goal in times of financial crises and high inflation. But the fact remains that it’s the lower end which suffers more when cuts are made, while high level perks often remain untouched.
