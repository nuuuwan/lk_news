The AI debate we are not having

China went from almost no high speed rail to the largest network on earth in twenty years.

There is a hard part to this, though, and I am not going to skip it. Some people here will lose their jobs

Strip away the noise,  and what has actually happened over the past three years is fairly simple. Computers have become able to do a particular kind of work that previously required a trained human. Not physical work. Not the arithmetic that calculators have handled for fifty years. The work in between. Reading a stack of documents and telling you what matters in them. Looking at a year of transactions and noticing the pattern. Drafting the contract, checking the ledger, building the forecast, writing the report.

That work has always been expensive, because it required someone educated, paid monthly, and difficult to replace. It is now becoming cheap. That is the entire event. Everything else is an argument about what it means. And the argument, so far, has been about loss. Ask almost anyone in San Francisco or Brussels what artificial intelligence means,  and you will hear about the paralegal, the junior analyst, the graduate hire who was going to learn the trade by doing the tedious parts of it.

The worry is legitimate. Those people will lose work they depend on. But notice what the question assumes before it is even asked. It assumes somebody already had the job. Replacement requires an incumbent. And that is where this stops describing us. Twenty minutes from where I am writing this, there is a factory. Twenty two people, real customers, an order book into next quarter. There is no CFO, and there has never been one. Not because the founder is unsophisticated, because she can tell you the landed cost of every line she makes from memory and she is usually right. There is no CFO because a competent one costs more per year than the company clears in profit.

Which means the finance work does not get done badly. It does not get done. Cash flow is a feeling in the stomach at 11pm. Pricing is instinct. The big order gets accepted on optimism, because working it out properly needs a capability she cannot buy at any price she can survive. If that were one unlucky company, it would not be worth writing about. But move that factory to Karachi, or Nairobi, or Lima, and the currency changes, the tax code changes, and the arithmetic does not. Small and medium enterprises are more than ninety percent of firms on earth, seventy percent of employment and half of global GDP. What looks like an exception from a distance is the default condition of commerce.

That absence has a cost, and it is not abstract. Companies like hers rarely die of weak demand. They die of a cash gap nobody saw coming from three weeks out, or a receivable that aged quietly, or a good order that turned out to be underwater once the currency moved. Each of those is the same failure, which is a failure to see far enough ahead to act. The same blindness follows them to the bank. A business whose numbers exist only as a feeling cannot be lent to, so the International Finance Corporation now puts the unmet credit need of small firms in developing economies at 5.7 trillion dollars a year.

That is not a shortage of capital. It is a shortage of information. Lenders who cannot price risk ask for collateral instead, which excludes precisely the firms with the most growth in them and the least land. Hold all of that in mind, and then ask her the question they are asking in San Francisco. What happens when a machine can do an analyst’s work? She is not going to wonder what happens to hers. She is going to tell you she will finally have one. You cannot be replaced at a job nobody was doing. If this sounds like special pleading, look at what people actually believe.

Ipsos data compiled in Stanford’s AI Index found 83 percent of Chinese respondents saying AI delivers more benefits than drawbacks, against 39 percent of Americans. Roughly three quarters of Chinese respondents expect it to create jobs. In the West the expectation runs the other way, and has for years. That gap is usually explained as propaganda or naivety, which is condescending and, I think, wrong. Optimism about AI tracks almost perfectly with where a country sits on this same question. Asia and Latin America are consistently the most positive. Europe and North America are the most nervous. These are not differences in temperament. They are differences in what you have to lose.

A society that already built the institutions, filled the roles and staffed the professions experiences a cheap substitute as a threat to something it owns. A society still building them experiences it as a shortcut. China is the clearest case because it has done this before, and recently enough to remember. It went from almost no high speed rail to the largest network on earth in twenty years. It skipped credit cards entirely and went from cash to phones. When you have personally watched a missing thing arrive within your working life, a new capability does not read as a threat to the old arrangement. There was no old arrangement. That is closer to our position than San Francisco’s is.

There is a hard part to this, though, and I am not going to skip it. Some people here will lose their jobs. The outsourcing industry across South Asia employs millions of people doing structured, rules based, document heavy work. That is precisely the work these systems are best at. The same is true of the bookkeeper who keeps the ledgers for eleven small firms, and the junior at the accounting practice, and the clerk entering invoices at a distributor. These are real jobs supporting real households, in a country with almost none of the safety net that Europe and America are relying on. Those people are owed time, retraining and a serious plan. They are not owed a lecture about how the economy comes out ahead.

But the two effects have different shapes, and the difference explains why one of them gets discussed. The loss is concentrated in specific roles, mostly urban and mostly formal. The gain is spread thinly across millions of firms that never had the capability at all. Concentrated losses are loud. Diffuse gains are invisible. So the debate will be about displacement even where the net effect is strongly positive, which is why anyone making my argument has to say the hard part rather than let the good part carry it. Nor is the good version automatic, and I would rather say so now than be quoted on it later.

Intelligence can simply become an import, rented from two countries and priced in dollars, so that productivity rises here and the payment leaves. The best funded firms adopt first and pull further ahead, and the gap that closes between countries opens up inside them. And capability without institutions is only a better documented failure, because you can see your cash position perfectly and still lose the company to a dispute that takes six years to resolve. None of it substitutes for the port, the power cut, the exchange rate or the courts. What it does is remove one specific constraint on people who are already good at what they do, which was never the constraint anyone chose. It was arithmetic. So the replacement question is real, and it deserves a serious answer where it applies. It is simply not the first question. For most of the world, for most of the businesses and most of the people who will decide what this century actually looks like, the first question is what happens when a capability that was always out of reach becomes ordinary.
