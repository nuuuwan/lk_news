We cannot afford to import our health security

Sri Lanka has learnt many painful lessons from depending too heavily on the outside world. Fuel shortages taught us about energy security. Food shortages reminded us about agricultural security. The economic crisis showed us the dangers of depending on foreign exchange to pay for almost everything we need.

But there is another area where Sri Lanka remains heavily dependent on imports - medicines.

Around 85 percent of Sri Lanka’s pharmaceutical requirements are still met through imports. The local pharmaceutical market was estimated at around US$750 million in 2025, meaning hundreds of millions of dollars are spent every year bringing medicines into the country.

India is naturally one of our most important suppliers. Its pharmaceutical industry is enormous, competitive and capable of producing medicines at prices Sri Lanka often cannot match locally.

There is nothing wrong with buying medicines from India. But the question we should be asking is whether 85 percent dependence is sensible for a country of 22 million people.

The answer should be no. The issue becomes particularly serious when there are failures in the international supply chain. A medicine may be inexpensive and readily available, but if quality assurance fails somewhere between manufacturing, procurement, shipping and distribution, the consequences can be serious.

In 2023, the National Medicines Regulatory Authority admitted that several imported medicines had failed quality tests after being brought into the country under registration waivers. Among them was prednisolone acetate ophthalmic suspension. The product was withdrawn after samples failed sterility testing, while hospitals reported cases of eye inflammation following its use.

The incident was serious enough for Sri Lanka to seek explanations from Indian authorities. This should not become an excuse to paint every Indian pharmaceutical company with the same brush. But it should make Sri Lanka ask a much bigger question.

Why are we not producing more of our own essential medicines?

The country already has the beginnings of a pharmaceutical manufacturing industry. The State Pharmaceuticals Manufacturing Corporation has experience producing medicines, while private sector manufacturers have also entered the market. What is missing is scale, investment, research and a long term national strategy.

The government should treat pharmaceutical manufacturing as a strategic industry rather than simply another manufacturing sector.

Sri Lanka spends significant foreign exchange importing medicines. Part of that money could be redirected towards modern manufacturing facilities, pharmaceutical research and training scientists, pharmacists, engineers and technicians. Sri Lanka does not have to manufacture every medicine in the world. We should identify medicines that are used in large quantities and can realistically be produced locally. The goal should not simply be import substitution.

The goal should eventually be exports. Sri Lanka sits in the middle of a region with hundreds of millions of potential consumers. South Asia has a massive demand for affordable generic medicines. There are already encouraging signs. In 2026, Zydus Lifesciences and Sunshine Healthcare announced a joint venture involving an investment of more than US$20 million to establish a pharmaceutical manufacturing facility in Sri Lanka. This is precisely the type of investment Sri Lanka should encourage.

But investment alone will not solve the problem. Regulation must improve at the same time. The NMRA has an important responsibility to ensure every medicine entering Sri Lanka meets the same high standard, whether it comes from India, China, Bangladesh, Europe or elsewhere. The World Health Organisation has also warned that substandard and falsified medicines remain a major public health threat. Sri Lanka has been strengthening efforts to identify and combat such products.

We therefore need both stronger regulation and stronger local production. Medicine is not just another import. It is a matter of national security. A country that cannot guarantee a reliable supply of essential medicines during a foreign exchange crisis, international emergency or disruption to global supply chains remains vulnerable.

Sri Lanka should move from being a country that largely buys medicines from the region to a country that makes medicines for the region.

Our people deserve medicines that are affordable, accessible and, above all, safe.

Health security should not begin at the port when a shipment of medicine arrives.
