Colombo - A city that should not drown

Colombo, Nov.9 (Daily Mirror) - Colombo’s roads turned into rivers on Friday evening, residential neighbourhoods were submerged, sewage systems overflowed and thousands of commuters were left stranded after 129mm of rain fell within just two hours. What should have been a routine evening in the commercial capital turned into a nightmare for residents, exposing once again the glaring weaknesses in the city’s drainage infrastructure and the failure of those entrusted with maintaining it.

While heavy rainfall is certainly beyond the control of municipal authorities, the failure to prepare for increasingly unpredictable weather is not. Flooding is no longer an occasional inconvenience that cities can afford to address only after disaster strikes. It is a growing urban challenge, particularly in countries such as Sri Lanka, which are increasingly vulnerable to climate change.

What is particularly disappointing is the response from Colombo Mayor Vraie Cally Balthazaar, who explained that the city’s drainage system was designed to accommodate approximately 30mm of rainfall, whereas Colombo received 129mm within a few hours. While the explanation may be technically correct, is this really an acceptable excuse from someone entrusted with governing the country’s commercial capital?

If Colombo’s drainage infrastructure can only withstand 30mm to 50mm of rainfall, then surely the question should be why successive municipal administrations have failed to improve this capacity. More importantly, what has the present administration done during its months in office to address these longstanding shortcomings?

Climate change is not a new phenomenon. Scientists have warned for years that changing weather patterns will bring more intense rainfall, unpredictable monsoons and extreme weather events. Sri Lanka, like several other South Asian countries, is particularly vulnerable to these developments. To continue managing a capital city using infrastructure designed for yesterday’s weather is simply irresponsible.

The problem is not merely the volume of rainfall. Blocked drains, neglected canals, unauthorised constructions, garbage accumulation and the destruction of natural waterways all contribute to urban flooding. When sewage systems overflow into residential neighbourhoods, the consequences extend beyond inconvenience. They create serious public health concerns, particularly for children, elderly residents and vulnerable communities.

A properly functioning municipal council should have a systematic programme to inspect, clean and maintain every drainage channel, particularly before the monsoon seasons. Was this done adequately? Were the drainage networks inspected, and were blocked canals identified and cleared before the rains arrived? These are questions the Mayor must answer rather than simply pointing to the extraordinary volume of rainfall.

Other South Asian cities offer important lessons, even though none is immune to flooding. Mumbai has repeatedly experienced devastating urban floods, including the 2024 monsoon floods. Yet the city has continued investing in stormwater drainage improvements and flood management infrastructure. Chennai, which experienced catastrophic flooding in 2015, has pursued an integrated urban flood management programme with support from the Asian Development Bank. Its approach includes improving drainage infrastructure, strengthening preparedness and ensuring long-term maintenance.

Dhaka, meanwhile, provides a warning of what happens when urban expansion comes at the expense of natural waterways. The loss of wetlands and canals has significantly weakened the city’s ability to absorb and drain excess rainwater. Colombo must learn from these experiences rather than repeat the same mistakes.

The solutions are not beyond our reach. First, the CMC must undertake a comprehensive audit of the entire drainage network, identifying blocked, damaged and undersized drains. A publicly available maintenance schedule should be introduced, with regular inspections and accountability for officials responsible for ensuring the work is completed.

Second, the city’s canals, wetlands and natural waterways must be protected from illegal encroachments and indiscriminate dumping. Construction approvals must be scrutinised, while existing obstructions should be addressed through lawful procedures that also protect affected communities.

Third, Colombo needs a long-term climate-resilient drainage master plan, developed with engineers, environmental experts and urban planners. This must include larger stormwater drains where necessary, additional pumping facilities, retention ponds and rainwater harvesting systems. Parks and green spaces should also be incorporated into urban planning to allow rainwater to seep naturally into the ground instead of immediately overwhelming drainage channels.

Technology must play a greater role too. Real-time rainfall monitoring, flood-risk mapping and early warning systems could help authorities identify vulnerable neighbourhoods and respond before roads become impassable.

Finally, the CMC must work with the Urban Development Authority, the Sri Lanka Land Development Corporation and other relevant institutions. Flood management cannot be treated as an isolated municipal responsibility.

The establishment of a special task force following Friday’s floods is welcome, but committees and meetings cannot substitute for actual work on the ground.

Colombo is the country’s commercial heart. It attracts investors, tourists, businesses and thousands of daily commuters. A capital city that cannot manage heavy rainfall risks damaging not only its residents’ confidence but also its reputation as a place to do business.

The Mayor must understand that leadership is not about explaining why a city floods after the damage is done. It is about anticipating problems, investing in solutions and ensuring that public institutions perform the responsibilities entrusted to them.

Climate change may be beyond our control, but poor planning, neglected infrastructure and administrative complacency are not. Colombo cannot afford another rainy evening that leaves its residents swimming through their own streets. The city deserves better than excuses.
