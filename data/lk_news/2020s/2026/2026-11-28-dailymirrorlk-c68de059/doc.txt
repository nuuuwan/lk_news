Green groups flag US$ 15.3bn in ADB’s incineration-linked financing, Sri Lanka among recipients

Incineration features in 41 of 49 ADB-funded “false solution” projects worth US$ 15.3bn since 2015

Citizens’ Atlas maps seven Sri Lankan projects, including one ADB-backed project

​Waste incineration features in 41 of the 49 Asian Development Bank (ADB) projects flagged as false climate and circularity solutions over the past decade, a portfolio associated with more than US$ 15.3 billion in financing, according to the Global Alliance for Incinerator Alternatives Asia Pacific (GAIA AP).

Drawn from GAIA AP’s Citizens’ Atlas on False Climate and Circularity Solutions, the findings show that close to 84 percent of the flagged projects the bank supported between 2015 and 2025 carry an incineration component. GAIA AP released them at a symbolic “retirement party” for the bank, organised by the NGO Forum on ADB and partner civil society groups in Quezon City, Philippines on Wednesday, ahead of the ADB’s 60th Annual Meeting in Nagoya, Japan next year. The event launched a wider campaign under the banner “ADB at 60: Retire the Harms, Provide Reparations, Cancel the Debt”, with the NGO Forum on ADB presenting a People’s Retirement Plan for ADB at 60. The plan sets out ten demands, ranging from an end to fossil fuel financing, false solutions and debt-creating climate finance to meaningful community participation and reparations for affected communities.

Loans make up the bulk of the bank’s incineration-linked exposure, with US$ 11.2 billion extended across 24 projects accounting for 73.2 percent of the total. That works out to an average of roughly US$ 467 million per loan-backed project. The balance of about US$ 4.1 billion is spread across other instruments, including equity and technical assistance.

Sri Lanka, a founding member of the bank in 1966, accounts for more than US$ 150 million of the ADB financing captured in the mapping, roughly 1 percent of the regional total, as per a GAIA AP factsheet on the bank. That places the island seventh among recipient countries, marginally behind the Maldives (US$ 154.66 million) and Thailand (US$ 154 million). India dominates the list with over US$ 8.05 billion, more than half the overall figure, followed by Indonesia at US$ 4.2 billion. However, the atlas shows that the ADB is only one of several financiers behind such projects in Sri Lanka. Of the seven Sri Lankan cases mapped on the platform, just one is backed by the ADB, while the remaining six, or 86 percent, are financed by other multilateral and bilateral donors.

Waste-to-energy incineration makes up more than half of the Sri Lankan project classifications, with the rest split evenly between other forms of incineration and refuse-derived fuel. Only two of the seven projects are listed as active, and most are mapped in and around Colombo. At the ADB’s Asia Clean Energy Forum earlier this year, Aitken Spence PLC showcased its 10MW Kerawalapitiya plant as a role model for land-constrained nations, describing it as Sri Lanka’s first and only waste-to-energy power plant. Developed and financed by the company through its subsidiary Western Power Company, the plant cost US$ 80 million, or about US$ 8 million per megawatt, and can process up to 700 tonnes of municipal solid waste a day.

GAIA AP warned that the bank’s financing of capital-intensive waste technologies risks locking cities into decades of waste generation, costly infrastructure and debt, since incinerators and refuse-derived fuel facilities depend on a constant supply of waste. The group argued that such plants undermine waste prevention and Zero Waste approaches, while threatening the livelihoods of waste pickers by diverting recyclables from existing recovery systems. The mapping also exposes gaps in the bank’s approach to a just transition. Only five of the 49 projects, or around one in ten, mention reabsorbing informal waste workers or addressing child and bonded labour risks, and only six refer to or have implemented Gender Action Plans. Meanwhile, 21 projects carry high or moderate involuntary resettlement risks, yet only three have resettlement action plans, leaving six in every seven such projects without one.

“We call on the ADB to finally retire its portfolio from waste incineration and other false solutions to pollution and climate change,” GAIA Asia Pacific Climate and Anti-Incineration Campaigner Brex Arevalo said.

“There is clear harm when projects tagged as high-risk for human rights violations get railroaded approval. There is clear harm when, after 60 years, the Philippines and the Global South continue to sink even deeper into debt, poverty, and pollution,” he added.

Communities across the region are contesting ADB-backed projects over pollution, participation and environmental safeguards, the group noted. It cited the 12-plant GWTE waste-to-energy project and a southern biomass project in Thailand, Cebu’s proposed waste management public-private partnership in the Philippines, and the Greater Malé waste-to-energy project in the Maldives, which has prompted a complaint to the bank’s Accountability Mechanism.

GAIA AP urged the ADB to redirect financing towards Zero Waste systems built on waste prevention, reuse, repair, recycling and composting, alongside people-centred approaches that recognise the essential role of waste workers. (NF)
