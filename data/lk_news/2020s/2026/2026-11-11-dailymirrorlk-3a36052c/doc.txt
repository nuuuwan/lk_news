High-Value Tourism - High Value for Whom?

Recently at the World Travel & Tourism Council Global Summit, aptly known as “the Davos of travel and tourism,” 1,000 tourism leaders from around the world came together and one thing that kept resonating over these days in Valletta Malta was the idea of “value over volume” when it comes to tourism. This cry is also the latest concern in Sri Lanka’s tourism industry, that earnings are failing to keep pace with visitor numbers. “Volume up, value down” is the lament, followed by calls to move “beyond arrivals” and attract high-spending tourists. This deserves attention, although more visitors do not necessarily produce a stronger tourism economy, particularly when businesses are discounting heavily and destinations are struggling with congestion.

Having spent some of my working life managing hotels, I understand the importance of obtaining a decent rate. Without adequate returns, a hotel cannot maintain its property, retain good staff or improve the experience it offers. My concern is with the rather easy assumption that attracting a higher-spending visitor will automatically produce a better outcome for Sri Lanka. A better room rate is welcome, but how much of that additional income reaches the people and businesses around the hotel, and how much remains in the country?

Unless we understand this distinction, we may simply replace the arrival target with an earnings target, and continue celebrating the headline without examining what lies behind it.

We tend to use “high spending”, “high end” and “high value” as though they mean the same thing. An expensive hotel may offer excellent accommodation and service, and its guests may spend generously. But from the country’s point of view, the question extends beyond the amount on the bill. It includes how much remains in the economy, who benefits from it and what demands the visit places on the destination.

A visitor staying at an expensive resort may be highly valuable to that business, and could spend much of the visit within the hotel enjoying its facilities. The wider contribution depends on how the resort operates: its employment practices, purchasing arrangements, taxes, imported inputs and connections with other enterprises. On the other hand, a visitor staying in modest accommodation may spend less each day, but remain longer and purchase directly from restaurants, guides, transport providers and small businesses. Neither visitor can be judged properly from the accommodation invoice alone.

This is not an argument for one category of tourism over another. A large, professionally managed hotel can support substantial employment and a dependable network of domestic suppliers. A small establishment can offer poor working conditions, evade regulation and contribute little beyond the owner’s household. Size and price alone tell us too little about the wider benefit.

The established sustainable tourism literature recognises this distinction. The UNEP–UNWTO framework includes commercial viability, local prosperity, employment quality and social equity among its objectives. Business success matters, but it cannot stand as evidence that the other objectives have also been met. ( https://www.un.org/sustainabledevelopment/)

Before concluding that Sri Lanka is attracting the wrong type of tourist, it would be good to first be sure that  we are comparing the figures on the same basis. How much of the reported decline comes from changes in spending and length of stay, and how much comes from changes in the way earnings are estimated?

Sri Lanka calculates total tourism earnings by gathering spending data through interviews with a sample of departing tourists at airports. This method relies on human memory, with tourists estimating what they spent days or weeks earlier. Recall errors can affect the reporting of cash expenditure, while the composition of the sample matters when assessing differences in spending between premium and budget travellers. Spending estimates are combined with arrival numbers and average length of stay, and periodically updated through the work of the Sri Lanka Tourism Development Authority and the Central Bank of Sri Lanka.

Reliance on sample-based surveys therefore raises questions about how fully different spending patterns are captured. These surveys need to be strengthened with systematically tracked tourist spending through credit-card or formal banking data. Mobile network big data (MNBD) has been introduced in some countries to help understand visitor movements. It can show where tourists go, but it cannot, on its own, tell us how much they spend.

Actual spending, length of stay, discounting and market composition need to be examined on a consistent basis. If visitors are staying fewer nights, the response may differ from that required when hotels are cutting rates. If tourists have money to spend, but find few worthwhile experiences to purchase, the problem lies partly in the product. Attributing every weakness to the wrong type of tourist allows the industry to avoid examining its own performance.

The concern that higher earnings may accrue mainly to established hotel groups and DMCs is a reality that needs careful attention. But it should not become an assumption that these businesses contribute little to the wider economy.

A hotel’s contribution extends well beyond the guests staying in it. A regular order for vegetables, fish or laundry services can provide a dependable income for a local business. A DMC can help a small experience provider reach overseas customers, manage bookings and deal with commercial risks that would otherwise be difficult to handle. These connections matter, but they have to be built and maintained. A higher room rate, by itself, tells us little about whether they are becoming stronger.

Higher earnings do not spread automatically. A higher room rate does not always result in better pay, nor does an excursion sold at a premium necessarily provide a fair return to its guide or community host. Purchasing may connect a property with surrounding producers, or bypass them almost entirely.

Two other issues need to be distinguished. The first is money leaving Sri Lanka through imports and overseas payments, which is international leakage. Offshore bookings and informal payment arrangements raise further questions about how much expenditure enters the domestic economy and how accurately it is recorded. But money outside the formal banking system is not necessarily money lost to Sri Lanka. These flows need to be examined before conclusions are drawn about their scale or economic effect.

The second issue is money remaining here but accruing disproportionately to a few owners, which is domestic concentration. A business can be entirely Sri Lankan-owned and still distribute its gains narrowly. There is nothing inherently wrong with profitable tourism enterprises. The policy question is whether publicly supported tourism growth creates opportunities beyond the main investors, and whether those opportunities offer worthwhile livelihoods.

I sometimes wonder whether we confuse a more expensive tourism product with a more elaborate building. Sri Lanka does not need to compete everywhere through larger rooms, more marble and increasingly elaborate fittings. Comfort and good service matter, but so does a property’s character, its setting and the experience offered around it. A well-designed place that feels Sri Lankan can have considerable appeal without becoming ostentatious.

In wildlife tourism, for example, the quality of the naturalist can make an enormous difference. Seeing an elephant is one experience; understanding its behaviour and the habitat around it is another. The value comes partly from the person interpreting what is being seen. The same applies to an archaeological site or a village experience. These products need careful preparation and professional delivery, rather than simply being given a new name and added to a brochure.

The same discipline should apply to claims of low-impact luxury. A wealthy visitor is not necessarily a responsible visitor. Willingness to pay does not guarantee respect for wildlife, restrained resource use or consideration for residents. Visitor behaviour and destination management must be addressed directly, whatever the price category.

Premium markets require confidence in the product, reliable service and effective distribution. They cannot be attracted simply by changing the language of a campaign.

Before looking for an entirely different visitor, there is work to be done with the tourism economy already here. Worthwhile experiences must be easy to find and book, guides need the training and remuneration to deliver them properly, and small suppliers must be able to meet the standards hotels require. Encouraging a villager to supply a hotel achieves little if the business cannot finance production or wait for its invoices to be settled.

Improving these conditions may generate benefits sooner than waiting for an entirely different visitor mix, which is a slow process that has to be done by careful re-positioning and marketing.  Local purchasing requires attention to quality, volume and delivery. It may involve helping producers combine their supplies, improve food safety and gain access to working capital. Hotels cannot be expected to compromise on reliability or standards, but there is scope to help local businesses meet those requirements.

Measurement must change as well. Visitor expenditure and business returns should be examined alongside the value retained in Sri Lanka, the quality of employment and the pressure placed on destinations. These measures need to remain visible separately, because a single impressive total can conceal gains in one area and losses in another.

This is also a question I am trying to address in the Managed Yield, Controlled Volume, Diversified Tourism (MYCVD) model approach I have been developing for Sri Lanka Tourism. The intention is to improve earnings through a better balance of visitors and experiences, while keeping volumes within manageable limits. (https://lnkd.in/p/g8ZgWrVe)  But increasing expenditure per visitor will not be enough if the additional income benefits only a narrow section of the industry. Managed yield must also consider what remains in Sri Lanka and how it reaches employees, suppliers and communities. Otherwise the model would leave unanswered the very question raised in this article.

The commercial viability of a tourism enterprise cannot be taken lightly. Equally, a national tourism policy must look beyond the hotel’s own returns. The villager supplying its kitchen, the guide interpreting a site and the employee supporting a family, are all part of the same economy. Their gains need to be examined alongside the costs borne by the destination.

Until that becomes part of how we assess tourism, I would be cautious about calling a visitor “high value” simply because the invoice is high.
