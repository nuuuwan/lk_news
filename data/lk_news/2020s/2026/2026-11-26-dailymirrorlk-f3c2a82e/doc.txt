What happens when  agitators become  administrators?

Today, the placards have been traded for ministerial portfolios, and the tune has changed entirely

There is an undeniable, almost poetic irony unfolding across Sri Lanka’s public sector as trade unions mobilise over the national budget. As teachers, principals, doctors, university staff, and state administrators threaten to bring essential public services to a standstill, the government finds itself staring directly into a mirror of its own making.

For decades, the political forces now occupying the seats of power built their brand on the streets, mastering the art of relentless agitation. Now, seated behind desks with the burden of statecraft upon their shoulders, they are confronting a truth they once routinely dismissed: reality cannot be negotiated away at a picket line.

When in opposition, the playbook was straightforward and politically lucrative. No union demand was ever deemed too steep, no fiscal constraint was accepted as a valid excuse, and every work stoppage—regardless of its crippling impact on schools, hospitals, or the broader economy—was championed as a righteous struggle against an uncaring establishment.

Economic orthodoxy was routinely derided as a heartless talking point; basic arithmetic was treated as an adversary of the working class. During years of economic turmoil and high inflation, promises were made with reckless abandon. Voters and state employees were led to believe that decades-old salary anomalies could be wiped out with the stroke of a pen that allowances could effortlessly match the rising cost of living, and that fiscal discipline was merely a pretext used by corrupt elites to deny workers their due.

Today, the placards have been traded for ministerial portfolios, and the tune has changed entirely.

Ministers and lawmakers who once led the charge from the frontlines of protest marches are now lecturing trade unions on the dangers of destabilising the state sector. They warn against isolated concessions, emphasise the delicate balance of state finances, and plead for patience while committees and commissions deliberate. Having gained access to the actual ledgers of the Treasury, the administration has abruptly discovered the cold, unforgiving mechanics of governing a recovering economy.

This collision between past rhetoric and present reality exposes a profound crisis of political maturity—one that implicates both the ruling administration and the trade union movement itself.

First, the government is paying the inevitable price for populist overpromising. In politics, weaponising unrealistic expectations is an effective way to win elections, but it is a disastrous foundation for governance. By assuring public servants that relief was merely a matter of political will rather than economic capacity, the party boxed itself into an impossible corner. Now, even prudent, responsible fiscal management is viewed by its own core voter base not as statesmanship, but as betrayal. While an honest acknowledgment in Parliament that public sector salaries have still not caught up with the cost of living is welcome, candour after taking office does little to pacify unions that were explicitly told such hurdles did not exist.

Second, the trade unions themselves must confront the economic facts of life rather than clinging to the illusions fed to them during election season. Threatening island-wide strikes and holding patients, students, and taxpaying citizens hostage to extract budget concessions from  already strained state coffers is neither sustainable nor fair. The broader public, the vast majority of whom work in the private and informal sectors without pension guarantees or state-backed job security, are the ones who ultimately fund public sector salaries through direct and indirect taxes. Demanding sweeping, unfunded allowances or specialised tax exemptions while the country remains bound by strict debt-restructuring targets and International Monetary Fund revenue benchmarks ignores the fragile nature of Sri Lanka’s economic stabilisation.

If the state caves in to every sectoral demand simply to buy industrial peace, the resulting fiscal blowout will quickly erase whatever nominal salary gains workers secure on paper.

Governance is not an extension of an election campaign, and a national budget cannot be treated as a political wishlist. For Sri Lanka to break free from its recurring cycles of economic crisis and social unrest, both sides must grow into their responsibilities. The government must own the consequences of the expectations it stoked and public sector unions must recognise that economic reality is non-negotiable.
