Sri Lanka: More than just a tourist destination - A home to many foreigners

Colombo, Nov. 25 (Daily Mirror) - Of the approximately 20,000 foreign nationals living, working and investing in Sri Lanka, a significant number are presently on the wrong visa type or are overstaying their visas. This is often through no fault of their own, but rather because of poor advice and guidance from substandard visa agents.

There is no doubt that Sri Lanka is one of the most sought-after destinations in the world to visit and live in. Its geographic location, its beaches being declared among the world's best in 2026, the Pekoe Trail being named one of TIME's World's Greatest Places, and its many other accolades all add to its appeal. Combined with the opportunities it offers for employment and investment, these qualities place Sri Lanka on the bucket list of many a global citizen.

Yet it tugs at the heartstrings to hear of a foreign woman who was told unequivocally by a visa agent that she could work as a yoga instructor at a wellness spa without a valid private sector employment visa, only to be deported for not holding the correct visa.

Then there is the elderly English gentleman who was advised that he could run his guest house and employ foreign nationals without SLTDA approval. And so the list goes on.

But first, let us focus on why they are here, in this beautiful country.

When the American filmmaker Samuel Goldwyn stated that "great truths eventually become clichés", it would be fair to say that he was not thinking of Sri Lanka. However, the sentiment is apt for this teardrop isle. The terms "Pearl of the Indian Ocean", "paradise isle" and "the resplendent isle" - the list goes on - are based on fundamental truths. The country's landscape is as spectacular as it is varied: beaches, mountains, paddy fields, and picture-postcard towns and villages compressed into 25,000 square miles. It is as if it were shaped by a hand inspired by beauty and diversity, as well as character and delicious chaos. Add to that the overwhelming declaration by visitors that "the Sri Lankan people are so friendly" and, as actress Olivia Colman famously said, "Some of the best food I've had was in Sri Lanka", and you have a country with which many foreigners fall in love. Who can blame them?

Now, in your mind's eye, picture "Frank". Frank is German. As you read this, he is sitting at his laptop in a cafe on Hiriketiya Bay, answering emails and meeting deadlines. As he peers over the top of his screen, shielding his eyes from the glare of the sun, he can see swimmers, surfers, and those simply trying to remain on their boogie boards dotting the water.

He is not one of the two million tourists who visited Sri Lanka this year. He may well have been at one point, but he is no longer a tourist. Like thousands of other foreign nationals who have settled in Sri Lanka, he lives here.

Every foreign resident has a story about moving here: the anxiety of making such a life-changing decision, the anticipation of a new life and new experiences, and, in many cases, the angst of navigating visa applications. Too often, that process is placed in the hands of agents more invested in obtaining a visa at any cost than in securing the correct visa through the proper channels.

The reality is that a legitimate stay in Sri Lanka, and the peace of mind that comes with it, depend on following the correct procedures. These procedures are designed to protect Sri Lanka's interests as well as those of its foreign residents. Any agent who fails to explain, for example, that employment visas require recommendations from the relevant line ministry, that tourism-related businesses employing foreign nationals should be registered with the SLTDA, which bank accounts are required for an investor visa, or the difference between an IIA account and a VPFCA account, undermines this framework and compromises the stability of the foreign national's stay in the country.

Well-managed immigration and adherence to its requirements create an ideal symbiosis: foreign nationals are able to live, work and invest legitimately in Sri Lanka, while Sri Lanka's laws and national interests remain protected. This principle is not confined to immigration; it applies to every sphere of interaction and activity between foreign nationals and Sri Lanka.

For those hoping to make Sri Lanka a long-term home, falling in love with the country is only the beginning. Living here legitimately depends on accurate information, the correct documentation, and a sound understanding of the country's immigration framework. These may be less glamorous than the surf in Arugam Bay or the tea estates of Hatton, but they are the reality.

So, as Frank closes his laptop, looks out at the bay, and wonders whether to take a swim or have a cold beer, let us hope that he is here on the correct visa, enjoying the peace of mind that comes with it and living his best life on the Pearl of the Indian Ocean.

Kavitha is a Barrister-at-Law (Lincoln’s Inn), a senior legal academic and the Head of Operations at Ceylon Migration Pvt. Ltd.
