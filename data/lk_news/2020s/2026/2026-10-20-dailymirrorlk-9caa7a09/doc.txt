Banks’ offshore lending jumps 52% as Middle East strain surfaces in Stage 2 loans

Lending to overseas entities up 51.9% YoY, taking 37.1% of banks’ FCY loans

Stage 2 loans up 10.4% QoQ in 2Q, led by overseas, trade and tourism exposures

Sri Lanka’s banks expanded lending to the overseas borrowers by more than half in the year to June, channelling over a third of their foreign currency loans offshore, even as signs of stress linked to the Middle East conflict began to surface in their loan books, as per the Central Bank of Sri Lanka (CBSL) data.

Lending to overseas entities, which covers the loans booked through both banks’ overseas branches and their domestic branches, grew 51.9 percent year-on-year (YoY) by end-June 2026, accelerating from 32.3 percent a year earlier. The stock of such loans stood at an estimated Rs.1.15 trillion or around US $ 3.4 billion, roughly double its level two years ago.

By end-June, 37.1 percent of the banks’ outstanding foreign currency (FCY) loans were granted to overseas entities. The banks’ total FCY credit, by comparison, grew by only 4.4 percent in dollar terms and 17.2 percent in rupee terms.

Stripping out the currency effect of around 12 percent implied by those two figures, offshore lending grew by roughly 35 percent in dollar terms, around eight times the pace of the overall FCY loan book.

However, the Stage 2 loans, which signal a significant increase in credit risk short of impairment, rose 10.4 percent in the second quarter alone, with the increase concentrated in lending to overseas entities, trade and tourism.

The CBSL attributed the rise to “the adverse economic spillovers arising from the conflict in the Middle East and the increase in cross-border vulnerabilities”, noting that these loans could migrate into Stage 3, if the borrowers’ repayment capacity weakens.

Aided by the rapid credit growth, the sector’s Stage 3 loans ratio fell to 9.1 percent, from 11.9 percent a year earlier, although the Stage 3 loans edged up in both the first and second quarters, following Cyclone Ditwah, higher fuel prices and the Middle East war.

Meanwhile, the banks’ FCY investments fell 20.7 percent YoY, primarily as several banks reduced their holdings of foreign sovereign securities, a shift the CBSL noted “may partly reflect a reallocation of FCY funds towards lending, including significant cross-border lending”.

Growth in total FCY assets consequently slowed to 1.3 percent, from 10.5 percent a year earlier.

With the FCY liabilities growing 4.0 percent, driven mainly by the FCY deposits that were partly supported by the workers’ remittances, the sector’s net FCY assets contracted 22.6 percent, reversing the 43.3 percent growth recorded a year earlier.

The CBSL cautioned that the “aggressive expansion” of cross-border lending may expose the sector to cross-border credit risk and FCY liquidity risk as well as country-specific and foreign exchange risks.

On balance sheet, the sector swung from a net FCY asset position of Rs.51.5 billion to a net liability position of Rs.106.8 billion, a turnaround of Rs.158.3 billion. This was more than offset by the off-balance sheet long positions, leaving the overall net FCY exposure 31.0 percent higher at Rs.504.3 billion or 21.3 percent of regulatory capital, compared to 18.9 percent a year earlier.

The data predate the CBSL’s decision last month to carve the top-rated foreign sovereign debt out of the large exposure limits, allowing the banks from October 1 to hold up to half of their Tier 1 capital in the local-currency debt of any single top-rated foreign sovereign, as reported by Mirror Business.

The move gives the banks room to rebuild the foreign sovereign buffers; several of them ran down in the first half, although whether they will do so at the expense of higher-yielding offshore loans remains to be seen.

The lenders elsewhere in the region have grown more cautious since the conflict escalated. India’s largest lender, State Bank of India, paused new business in the Gulf Cooperation Council region in May, after the Asian and Chinese banks extended a record of more than US $ 15 billion in Gulf loans in 2025, roughly triple the previous year, as per the Bloomberg-compiled data.

The CBSL’s review did not disclose where the offshore loans were extended or how the book is split between overseas and domestic branches, although it called for continued monitoring of “the pace, composition and quality of credit”.
