Strengthen criminal justice system before reintroducing death penalty: Criminal Defence Attorney

Colombo, August 15 (Daily Mirror) - A senior lawyer specialising in criminal cases and appeals has stressed that Sri Lanka should first strengthen its criminal justice system before considering the reintroduction of the death penalty, warning that an irreversible punishment carries grave risks if investigative and judicial safeguards are inadequate.

Senior Counsel Darshana Kuruppu, in a Facebook post, said the debate over reintroducing capital punishment had once again come to the forefront amid public demands for severe punishment for serious crimes.

He acknowledged that victims and their families deserved justice and that those responsible for serious offences must be held accountable. However, he said the death penalty was fundamentally different from other forms of punishment because wrongful imprisonment could, at least in principle, be corrected, whereas an execution could never be reversed.

Kuruppu said his own professional experience had demonstrated the importance of reliable scientific evidence in criminal trials.

He said he had called and presented DNA evidence in two murder trials. In one of those cases, an extremely poor accused person who could not afford private legal representation had initially been convicted by the High Court and sentenced to death.

The Court of Appeal subsequently ordered a retrial. At the retrial, appearing for the accused, he presented DNA evidence, which played an important role in establishing the circumstances of the case. The High Court ultimately acquitted the accused and released him.

“This experience taught me a very important lesson,” he said, stressing that if proper scientific evidence was not identified, collected and presented during a criminal investigation, an innocent person could potentially be convicted of a serious crime.

He questioned how Sri Lanka could justify implementing an irreversible punishment while weaknesses remained in criminal investigations, evidence collection and preservation, legal representation and the judicial process.

He said the objective of a criminal investigation should not merely be to find evidence against a suspect, but to discover the truth.

Evidence supporting a suspect’s innocence should also be investigated and disclosed, he said, warning against investigations that begin with an assumption of guilt and then seek only evidence to support that assumption.

He proposed the introduction of body-worn cameras for police officers, particularly those involved in arrests, searches, raids and the seizure of evidence.

Recording important stages of investigations through audio and video could provide an objective account of what occurred and protect both police officers and suspects while assisting courts in determining the facts, he said.

However, he stressed that strict procedures would be required to prevent recordings from being altered, deleted or lost, while original recordings should be securely preserved and made available to courts when required.

The senior counsel also called for allegations concerning fabricated evidence and fabricated cases to be taken seriously, particularly in cases involving drug arrests and seizures.

He said the circumstances surrounding arrests, the discovery and seizure of drugs, the handling of exhibits and their production before court should be governed by transparent and verifiable procedures.

“If there is doubt about the integrity of any part of that process, the credibility of the entire prosecution case may ultimately be affected,” he said, adding that the risk became even more serious when the punishment under consideration was death.

Kuruppu also cautioned that human errors could occur within the judicial process itself.

Referring to his experience appearing before the Supreme Court in numerous cases, he said serious legal concerns had arisen in two murder cases regarding Court of Appeal judgments. Questions had been raised over similarities between those judgments and written submissions made by State Counsel.

He said the Supreme Court subsequently set aside those Court of Appeal judgments and sent the cases back for reconsideration or retrial.

He further highlighted the issue of economic inequality in access to justice.

He said an accused person with financial resources might be able to retain an experienced lawyer and obtain expert assistance, while an extremely poor accused might lack the resources required to obtain scientific evidence, call expert witnesses or properly challenge weaknesses in an investigation.

“If a person’s life is at stake, economic circumstances should never determine the quality of the legal defence available to that person,” he said.

He therefore called on the State to ensure effective legal representation for every accused person, regardless of their financial circumstances, before the death penalty is implemented.

He also advocated greater reliance on DNA, fingerprints, digital evidence, CCTV footage and other forms of scientific and technological evidence rather than depending solely on eyewitness testimony, statements or confessions.

He said Sri Lanka should invest further in forensic science and strengthen the capacity of investigators and forensic institutions to properly collect, analyse and present scientific evidence.

Among the reforms he proposed before any decision to implement the death penalty were the introduction of body-worn cameras for police officers involved in arrests, searches and raids; strengthening the use of DNA and other scientific evidence; establishing strict and independently verifiable chain-of-custody procedures; and introducing transparent procedures governing arrests, searches and seizures.

He also called for effective legal representation for indigent accused persons, independent mechanisms to investigate serious complaints concerning criminal investigations, stronger mechanisms to review potentially wrongful convictions and the highest level of appellate and judicial safeguards in death penalty cases.

Kuruppu stressed that his position did not amount to opposition to severe punishment for serious crimes.

“Victims deserve justice. Their families deserve justice. Criminal offenders must be held accountable under the law,” he said, while emphasising that this must be achieved through a fair investigation and reliable judicial process.

He noted that Sri Lanka had maintained a long-standing moratorium on executions, although the death penalty remained part of the country’s legal framework.

The central question, he said, should therefore not simply be whether executions should resume, but whether the criminal justice system was sufficiently reliable to prevent an innocent person from being convicted because of investigative failures, fabricated evidence, inadequate legal representation or judicial error.

“A judicial conviction can be overturned. A prison sentence can be corrected. A person can be released and compensated, but a human life cannot be restored once it has been taken,” Kuruppu said.
