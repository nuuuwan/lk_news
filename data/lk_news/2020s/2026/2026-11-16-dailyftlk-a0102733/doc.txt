Investing in Sri Lanka’s resilience: The case for patient capital

Whenever economies emerge from turbulent periods, recovery narratives are almost always framed through macroeconomic scorecards such as foreign exchange reserves, currency stabilisations, and sovereign credit trajectories. When Fitch Ratings upgraded Sri Lanka’s sovereign rating to 'B-' with a Stable Outlook was one encouraging sign. Tourist arrivals  reaching 1.64 million visitors in late 2026 nearly offered another indication of recovery, although challenges remained.

Yet for international investors, recovery presents a deeper question of purpose. Some investors enter emerging markets when valuations are low, seeking quick returns, and preparing to leave at the first hint of volatility.

That approach fundamentally misunderstands how resilient economies are built. Sustainable recovery requires more than capital seeking a quick return. It requires patient capital, a willingness to invest across economic cycles, develop people’s skills, and stay committed through difficult periods.

Enduring commercial value is created through resilience, not retreat. When a host nation faces external shocks, the conventional corporate playbook advises retrenchment by halting capital expenditure, cutting payrolls, and mothballing operational assets until complete certainty returns.

The approach must be to continue investing through those difficult periods. For example, our hospitality arm Asia Leisure, chose to maintain and upgrade boutique properties such as Le Grand Galle, The Habitat Kosgoda, and Tamarind Hill through the most challenging seasons of recent years. Rather than scaling back, we focused on continuous property enhancements, service refinement, and operational readiness.

This was not speculative optimism, but a recognition that tourism infrastructure cannot simply be switched off and restarted when conditions improve. When properties are preserved and revitalised during downturns, they are better placed to welcome visitors when demand returns.

Continuing to invest also demonstrates confidence in Sri Lanka’s future and a commitment to an enduring partnership.

Nowhere is the compounding power of patient capital clearer than in hospitality. As Prime Minister Dr. Harini Amarasuriya recently emphasised, achieving Sri Lanka's 2030 tourism ambitions requires more than physical infrastructure development. It demands a skilled workforce, quality employment generation, and the active empowerment of youth and women.

Far from a purely recreational sector, tourism operates as an essential economic catalyst, directly driving national employment and ensuring that the benefits of growth reach regional communities.

There is an outdated assumption that commercial returns and community welfare exist in conflict, as if generating shareholder value requires minimising local expenditure. In truth, sustainable commercial success and local economic prosperity reinforce each other. A hotel’s long-term success is closely tied to the wellbeing of the community around it. Its long-term viability depends on the vitality, skill, and goodwill of that community.

In coastal hubs like Galle and Kosgoda, we have  always placed importance on developing local talent. By recruiting locally, providing continuous vocational training, and developing career pathways for young men and women, commercial investment helps families build more secure livelihoods. The benefits extend to local businesses through sourcing produce from local farmers, partnering with artisanal fishermen, contracting regional transport operators, and engaging neighbourhood craftsmen. When an investor deliberately roots their supply chain in the domestic community, commercial growth creates opportunities well beyond the hotel itself.

Viewing Sri Lanka through the lens of Southeast Asia’s economic journey reveals striking parallels. Over three decades, ASEAN economies navigated repeated financial crises, geopolitical shifts, and external shocks. What propelled the region into one of the world's most vibrant economic blocs was not the absence of shocks, but the resilience of private enterprise and the steady inflow of patient capital that looked past short-term headlines.

Sri Lanka possesses important strengths, including an educated and adaptable workforce, an extraordinary cultural and ecological heritage, and a strategic geographic position in the Indian Ocean.  For long-term investors, periods of volatility do not erase that potential.

By committing early and demonstrating staying power, international investors do more than generate financial returns. Their continued investment can give others greater confidence in Sri Lanka’s long-term potential and encourage them to explore opportunities of their own.

National resilience is ultimately built over decades, not measured in quarterly earnings.

Governments establish the policy frameworks and monetary conditions for recovery, but private capital must breathe life into the real economy. When businesses reject the easy exit and choose instead to reinvest in people, preserve physical infrastructure, and uplift regional communities, they cease to be foreign entities observing from the margins. They become genuine stakeholders in the country's collective future.

Sri Lanka has repeatedly demonstrated an extraordinary capacity to endure and rebuild. For those of us who have stayed the course, our conviction is clear, the future of this nation belongs to those who have the patience to build alongside it.

(The author is Executive Chairman of the QI Group of Companies)
