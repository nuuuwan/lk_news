’’Sri Lanka emerges as key contender for Indian ocean blue economy hub’’

Colombo, Sep. 28 (Daily Mirror) - For centuries, the waters around Sri Lanka were the silent engine of global commerce. Arab, Chinese, Indian, and later European traders rode the monsoon winds to our shores, turning ancient ports like Galle, Trincomalee, and Jaffna into bustling nodes of exchange. That historic maritime path never lost its strategic value it merely changed its cargo.

Imports: Roman coins, Persian and Chinese ceramics, glassware, and luxury textiles.

Mantai (Mahatittha): Located on the northwestern coast near Mannar, it was the island's premier trading emporium from the 5th century BCE to the 13th century CE, connecting ships from Rome, Arabia, Persia, and China.

Godavaya (Godapavata): Situated on the southern coast near Hambantota, this bustling port served the ancient Ruhuna kingdom and featured an organized customs system. It is also home to an ancient shipwreck dating back to the 2nd century BCE.

Gokanna (Trincomalee): Located on the northeast coast, it provided one of the world's largest and safest natural deep-water harbors.

Jambukola: Located in the northern peninsula, it served as a key port for arrivals and departures to and from India, such as when the sacred Bodhi tree sapling was brought to Sri Lanka.

Kirinda: Positioned near the Kirindi Oya, it facilitated trade and connection for the southern inland hub of Tissamaharama.

Today, the modern ports of Colombo and Hambantota stand as the direct inheritors of that legacy. They function as vital logistical hubs for refuelling and container shipping along the same historic sea routes, and their importance is only growing. With over 60% of the world's container traffic continuing to pass through the shipping lanes just off Sri Lanka's coast, the island is no longer a peripheral stop on the map. It is a pivotal junction in the architecture of global trade.

The numbers are staggering. The east-west shipping corridor that skirts Sri Lanka carries the bulk of manufactured goods moving between East Asia, the Middle East, Europe, and the Americas. Roughly 200 to 300 ships traverse these waters daily, making it one of the busiest maritime corridors on Earth. For vessels travelling from Singapore to Suez, or Shanghai to Rotterdam, Sri Lanka sits squarely on the mainline.

This geography has always been destiny. What is new is the scale and sophistication of the infrastructure built to serve it.

The Port of Colombo remains the crown jewel of Sri Lankan logistics. As South Asia's busiest transshipment hub, it handles millions of twenty-foot equivalent units (TEUs) each year, serving as a critical interchange for cargo destined for India, Bangladesh, Pakistan, and beyond. Its deep-water terminals, modern gantry cranes, and free-zone facilities allow it to compete with regional giants such as Singapore and Dubai.

But Colombo's success is not just about volume. It is about reliability. In an era when supply chains are fragile and delays cost millions, Colombo's ability to move containers efficiently from one vessel to another is a strategic asset—not only for Sri Lanka but for the entire Indian Ocean rim.

If Colombo is the established anchor, Hambantota is the ambitious challenger. Located on the southern coast, just minutes from the main east-west shipping lane, Hambantota offers something Colombo cannot: proximity to the highway of global trade. Ships passing Sri Lanka need not deviate significantly to refuel, restock, or offload cargo.

Its deep-water natural harbour can accommodate the largest vessels afloat, including ultra-large container ships and supertankers. With an industrial zone, an international airport nearby, and plans for expanded bunkering and logistics services, Hambantota represents Sri Lanka's bid to move up the value chain from a transshipment point to a fully integrated maritime and industrial hub.

No discussion of Sri Lanka's ports is complete without acknowledging the geopolitical currents that swirl around them. The Indian Ocean has become a theatre of great-power competition, with India, China, and the United States all watching Sri Lanka's maritime infrastructure closely.

Hambantota and Colombo ports main challenge is to balance sovereignty, economic pragmatism, and strategic autonomy to ensure that its ports serve Sri Lankan interests first, while remaining open to global commerce.

Experts say policy reforms and infrastructure investment are key to transforming Sri Lanka into a maritime powerhouse. With a strategic location and vast maritime resources, the island nation aims to lead the region in sustainable ocean development.

Former Singaporean Minister George Yeo delivered a keynote address on "The Geopolitics and Future of the Indian Ocean" on September 23, 2026, talk hosted by The Geopolitical Cartographer and led by former President Ranil Wickremesinghe in Colombo. His remarks underscored several critical themes:

Yeo stressed that the future center of gravity for global trade, capital flows, and strategic dynamics lies within the Indian Ocean and the broader Indo-Pacific framework.

India-China Relations: He emphasized that regional peace and stability depend heavily on constructive ties between India and China. Improved relations between the two Asian giants benefit neighboring regions like Southeast Asia, which view friction between them with great anxiety.

Multipolar Transition: The lecture touched upon the global transition toward a multipolar world order, where nations like India are growing organically and playing an increasingly vital balancing role in international commerce and maritime security.

Sri Lanka's Strategic Role: Yeo's remarks reinforced Sri Lanka's position as a pivotal junction in the architecture of global trade a nation whose geography has always been destiny, and whose modern ports are the inheritors of a rich maritime legacy.

Many experts say, the real prize is not merely handling other countries' containers. It is building a maritime ecosystem that generates jobs, technology transfer, and industrial growth at home.

Bunkering and ship repair – servicing the thousands of vessels that pass daily.· Logistics and warehousing – adding value to cargo before it moves on.· Marine engineering and renewable energy tapping into offshore wind and ocean energy.· Fisheries and aquaculture – modernising a traditional sector with sustainable practices.

These are not just economic opportunities; they are strategic imperatives. A port that only transships boxes is vulnerable to competition. A port that anchors an entire maritime cluster is indispensable.

Sri Lanka's geographic advantage is permanent. Its infrastructure and policy environment are not. To fully capitalise on the more than 60% of global container traffic passing its shores, the country must:

1. Invest in digitalisation and automation at Colombo and Hambantota to match regional benchmarks.2. Streamline customs and trade facilitation to reduce turnaround times.3. Develop human capital in maritime logistics, engineering, and law.4. Pursue balanced diplomacy that protects national interests while attracting diverse international partners.5. Integrate port development with regional growth – ensuring that prosperity reaches beyond the harbour walls.

From the ancient silks and spices that once filled the holds of Arab dhows to the millions of containers stacked on today's mega-ships, Sri Lanka's maritime story is one of continuity and reinvention. Colombo and Hambantota are not just ports; they are declarations that this island nation intends to be a maker of global trade, not merely a passer-by.

The ships are already passing. The question is whether Sri Lanka will seize the moment or let it sail by.
