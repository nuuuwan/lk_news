22A in place of the new Constitution promised

Presidents Chandrika Kumaratunge,  Mahinda Rajapakse and Maithripala Sirisena (left to right) all promised new constitutions but failed to deliver

As the leaders of the Opposition pointed out, the 22A was not in the manifesto or any other agenda of the National People’s Power (NPP) before the last Presidential or Parliamentary election

The controversy surrounding the 22nd Amendment to the Constitution has eclipsed almost every political issue in the country, even though ordinary people seem to be totally dissociated with it. They do not seem to be interested in it as if it does not affect their lives.

However, it is the main issue and the only issue for those obsessed with politics,  and they are making a huge fuss over it. They are so obsessed with it that the government is sneaking in another controversial piece of legislation, ‘Protection of the State from Terrorism Act (PSTA), to repeal and replace the long-standing and widely criticised Prevention of Terrorism Act (PTA) of 1979, without being noticed by the Opposition parties.

Interestingly, the Cabinet granted approval to the justice minister to gazette and present to the Parliament the draft titled ‘Protection of the State from Terrorism Bill’ on the same day (August 18) the government presented the 22nd Amendment in the Parliament.

As the leaders of the Opposition pointed out, the 22A was not in the manifesto or any other agenda of the National People’s Power (NPP) before the last Presidential or Parliamentary election. It was a totally new Constitution which would replace the current second republican Constitution, instead piecemeal Constitutional amendments that was promised by the NPP during those elections.

However, the government now argues that it has been compelled by  circumstances to bring in piecemeal amendments. Responding to Opposition Leader Sajith Premadasa, Justice Minister Harshana Nanayakkara said on Monday that the government has to do many things that were not promised by the NPP before the last elections, citing the salary increase for the public sector, as an example.

He contended that adopting a new Constitution is a long process,  but some issues that come under the Constitution crop up unexpectedly, demanding immediate or speedy resolution, which is true. The urgency in extending the retirement age of judges, according to  leaders of the government, is prompted by the sufferings of the people due to inordinate delay in disposal of court cases. This point has now been ratcheted up by the reports about the huge backlog of unresolved court cases which  resulted in  prison congestion,  apparently causing recent prison riots.

We are restrained to discuss the contents as well as the merits and demerits of the 22A, as it is now a subject matter of several petitions before the Supreme Court. However, the NPP must have foreseen, prior to the elections, the necessity to bring in amendments to the Constitution before it is replaced with a new one, as several past governments encountered this situation or created such situations.

Almost all governments since 1970 had promised to repeal the existing Constitutions to adopt new ones. And almost all those governments since 1977 either introduced or attempted to introduce piecemeal amendments before their promises were kept or broken. The first leader to do so was President J.R. Jayewardene who replaced the first Republican Constitution of 1972 with the second Republican Constitution of 1978.

Despite him commanding five sixths of power in Parliament for the first and the only time in Sri Lanka’s history, apart from having massive public support, Jayewardene did not wait for that Constitutional overhaul to introduce the Executive Presidential mode of government. Instead, he amended the existing Constitution (the first Republican Constitution of 1972) to that effect in October 1977 – within three months of his government assuming office.

It showed his shaky confidence in the stability of his own government regarding a new Constitution, despite the unprecedented majority it then commanded.

The prime pledge by Chandrika Kumaratunga -- who broke the 17-year-long rule by the United National Party (UNP) in 1994, first by winning the Parliament election and then by winning the Presidential election -- was to scrap “J.R. Jayewardene’s “Bahubootha” Constitution, as she put it. However, she never did so, but presented a relatively very progressive new draft Constitution in Parliament in 2000. The UNP members set fire to the copies of that draft within the chamber of the Parliament.

She also attempted to bring in a “federal” mode of government, as a Constitutional amendment before that, in 1995 and 1996. Then during the “Parivasa” (probational) government with the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP), the latter compelled her to adopt the 17th Amendment, another progressive step forward.

Mahinda Rajapaksa who also promised a new Constitution never attempted to keep it, despite commanding a two thirds power in Parliament. He, instead used that power to strengthen the executive Presidency, which he promised to scrap, through the 18th Amendment.

This was also true for the Yahapalana government and the Gotabaya Rajapaksa administration. The Maithri-Ranil government, which also promised a new Constitution, instituted a Constitutional Assembly for that end only in January 2016, after adopting the 19th Amendment to the Constitution in April 2015.

In September 2020, President Gotabaya Rajapaksa appointed a nine-member expert committee chaired by President’s Counsel Romesh de Silva to draft a new constitution. Yet, ironically, a month later in October, 2020, his government passed the 20th Amendment to the Constitution, reversing most of the democratic and check-and-balance reforms introduced by the 19th Amendment, and restoring wide unilateral authority to the presidency.

Therefore, in a way, none of the current Opposition parties have the moral right to question the NPP government for bringing in piecemeal amendments in place of a new Constitution. However, people who voted the NPP has that right, not because the party did not foresee this necessity before the election, but there are no signs on the ground of a process to formulate a new Constitution, in spite of various claims by various government leaders.

Health and Media Minister Dr. Nalinda Jayatissa told media in December, 2024 that a new Constitution would be presented in three years. In the same month Justice Minister Harshana Nanayakkara stated that initial steps of Constitution making would be taken in January, 2025. Prime Minister Harini Amarasuriya told Parliament in April, 2025 that the process of drafting a new Constitution would be initiated after the provincial council elections.

Then again, she stated in Parliament on July 25 last year that initial steps for a new Constitution are being taken. Later in December, responding to the Opposition Leader, she said that at present reports of committees previously appointed to draft a new Constitution and other constitutional reform proposals are being considered. What is the real progress of the constitution making process? Or, is there really any process?
