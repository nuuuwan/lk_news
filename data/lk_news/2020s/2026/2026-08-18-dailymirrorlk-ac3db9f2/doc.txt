‘Between the Lamp and the Pot:’ A referendum remembered, and a warning for today

Despite these cordial appearances, President J. R. Jayawardene  deprived former Prime Minister Sirimavo Bandaranaike  of her civic rights

In 1982, democracy was threatened by a referendum. Forty-four years later, the danger may lie at the opposite extreme

There is much talk about referendums today, and it takes me back 44 years to Sri Lanka’s first, and so far only, national referendum, held in December 1982. President J. R. Jayewardene (JRJ) called it to extend the life of Parliament by another six years. The political circumstances were very different from today: the government wanted the referendum, while the opposition strongly opposed it.

But the statistics and constitutional arguments tell only part of the story. My own experience at a polling booth in Attidiya gave me a disturbing firsthand glimpse of how elections were conducted then. Over the past thirty years, the powers and independence of the Elections Commissioner have been significantly strengthened, providing greater authority to ensure the integrity and fairness of the electoral process.

To understand why the referendum was held, one must go back to the 1977 general election, when the UNP won an unprecedented 140 of 168 parliamentary seats. Using this huge majority, the government introduced the 1978 Constitution, established an executive presidency, introduced proportional representation and extended the terms of Parliament and the presidency to six years.

In October 1982, JR won Sri Lanka’s first direct presidential election with 52% of the vote, while SLFP candidate Hector Kobbekaduwa received 35%. Former Prime Minister Sirimavo Bandaranaike had been deprived of her civic rights and was therefore unable to contest. It was the first time in 30 years that an incumbent party had won a national election in Sri Lanka.

Although Jayewardene possessed extensive executive powers, he still needed Parliament’s approval for budgets and major economic decisions. The UNP therefore had a strong reason to avoid a general election. By 1982, it had increased its parliamentary strength to 142 seats, well above the two-thirds majority required for constitutional amendments. Jayewardene warned that the SLFP could win at least 60 seats in a new election, a possibility he described as disastrous for the country.

The solution was to extend Parliament’s term without holding a general election. The government introduced the Fourth Amendment to extend the life of the First Parliament until  August 4 1989. The Supreme Court approved the proposal by a 4–3 majority, subject to the required parliamentary majority and referendum approval. Parliament passed the bill on  November 5 1982 by 142 votes to four.

On 14 November, JR called the referendum for December 22. Voters were asked whether they approved extending Parliament’s term until 4 August 1989. A “Yes” vote was represented by a lamp and a “No” vote by a pot. Under the Referendum Act of 1981, if less than two-thirds of registered voters participated, at least one-third of all registered voters also had to vote “Yes” for the proposal to pass.

The campaign was bitterly contested. Following the October presidential election, Jayewardene had imposed a state of emergency, claiming that sections of the SLFP were plotting against him. The emergency remained in force during the referendum.

The opposition, led by Sirimavo Bandaranaike, campaigned vigorously against the extension. Despite having lost her civic rights, she was permitted to lead the campaign and was supported by Tamil and communist parties. She urged voters to choose the “Pot”, saying that it represented their right to continue electing Parliament and governments. Jayewardene campaigned equally vigorously for the “Lamp”. He argued that extending Parliament was necessary in the national interest and warned that an election could strengthen what he called “Naxalites”. He also presented the referendum as a vote of confidence in his government’s economic policies.

The referendum recorded a 71% turnout among 8,145,015 registered voters. More than 54% voted in favour, and 120 of the 168 electorates recorded majorities supporting the extension. Most voters in Tamil-majority areas, however, voted against it. But what happened at one polling booth in Attidiya tells a very different story from the official figures.

A day or two before the referendum, I was travelling home from my office in Colpetty with a friend who was a close supporter of the late Lalith Athulathmudali, then Minister of Ports and Shipping. He often gave me a lift home. On this occasion, he suggested that we stop at an organisers’ meeting Athulathmudali was holding in Ratmalana.

My friend introduced me to the minister as a staunch UNP supporter, a flattering but completely untrue description. Athulathmudali was giving his supporters last-minute instructions about manning polling booths. Someone complained that there were not enough people to cover several booths in the Attidiya area. The minister turned to my friend and asked whether he could help.

At around 8.00 a.m., the turnout was very low, and there was little for us to do. We were sitting around when a few men wearing green caps arrived and asked the presiding officer about the percentage of votes cast. They soon left. Shortly afterwards, a gang leader arrived with a group of men and ordered the opposition polling agents to leave the booth. They then demanded the remaining ballot papers from the presiding officer. The papers were distributed among the four of us, with instructions to mark them in favour of the “Lamp”.

The gang leader collected the marked papers and pushed them into the ballot box, leaving only a few unmarked papers with the presiding officer. I have often thought about that morning. It was not merely a political argument over whether Parliament should be extended. I saw, at close quarters, how intimidation and the abuse of electoral procedures could turn a democratic exercise into something very different.

The election report published by the then relatively powerless Elections Commissioner Chandrananda de Silva, documented serious electoral irregularities. It recorded widespread voter intimidation, ballot-box stuffing, unauthorised replacement of ballot boxes, and threats against polling officials. My experience cannot, of course, represent every polling station in the country. But it gave me a personal understanding of why the conduct of the referendum became such a controversial issue.

For me, the enduring lesson is more personal. A referendum may be presented as the purest expression of the people’s will, but that will can only be meaningful when voters are free to cast their ballots without fear, intimidation or manipulation. The events I witnessed in Attidiya 44 years ago remain a reminder that the integrity of an election is measured not only by the final result, but by what happens inside the polling booth.

In 1982, democracy was threatened by a referendum. Forty-four years later, the danger may lie at the opposite extreme. The Government is reportedly planning to extend the term of sitting judges by two years through its supermajority, thereby avoiding a referendum at a time when the people’s direct mandate may be constitutionally required. Different circumstances, different methods, but the lesson remains the same. Democracy is weakened whenever the people’s right to decide is set aside for political convenience.
