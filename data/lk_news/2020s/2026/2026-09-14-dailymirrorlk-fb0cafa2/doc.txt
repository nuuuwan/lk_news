Beyond the Mugshots: The Real Scandal of SriLankan Airlines

The arrest itself risks becoming the political headline while deeper institutional failure disappears from view (Pic courtesy of BBC News)

The arrest of SLPP National Organiser and Parliamentarian Namal Rajapaksa over alleged corruption linked to SriLankan Airlines’ Airbus procurement has revived one of the most troubling chapters in the national carrier’s history.  Predictably, some welcome the arrest as proof the Government takes corruption seriously, while others see a politically motivated probe. Both reactions miss the main point.

The Airbus affair is ultimately about establishing who was responsible for exposing billions of dollars of public money, whether illegal payments occurred, and why the system failed to stop it. If found guilty, individuals involved, including Namal Rajapaksa, must face the maximum punishment under the law. Sri Lanka deserves those answers, absolute justice, and a system in which tomorrow’s corruption is much harder to commit.

The Airbus affair should not be reduced to whether one politician is guilty or innocent. The central question is: how did a loss-making state airline become involved in aircraft transactions worth billions of dollars, and why have the institutions responsible for protecting public money repeatedly failed to establish accountability? That is the real scandal.

The controversy dates back to 2013, when SriLankan Airlines entered into arrangements involving Airbus A330-300 and A350-900 aircraft in a fleet-renewal programme valued over US$2.5 billion. For a struggling airline, commitments on that scale exposed vast public funds. The cancellation of some arrangements reportedly resulted in penalties exceeding US$100 million. Sri Lanka paid a heavy price for decisions made by custodians of a national asset.

What Does the 2020 UK Judgment Establish? Much of the political debate invokes the 2020 UK judgment concerning Airbus bribery, which established serious corruption involving Airbus and payments connected to aircraft procurement. According to the judgment, Airbus employees offered payments of up to US$16.84 million to a company linked to the wife of a person involved in SriLankan Airlines’ procurement. Ultimately, US$2 million was paid.

Those facts are serious, but another detail cannot be ignored: the 2020 UK judgment does not name Namal Rajapaksa. That does not prove his innocence, nor does it prevent investigators from obtaining evidence linking him to the matter. But the UK judgment itself cannot be presented as proof that Rajapaksa personally received the alleged bribe. This distinction separates a judicial finding from a political assertion. If the present case rests on evidence obtained after the UK proceedings, that evidence should be tested in court.

Former Board Director Professor Sonal Fernando has offered another perspective on aircraft procurement. He argued that commissions paid to buyers can be incorporated into aircraft pricing and claimed a transaction could become void if a buyer refused a seller’s commission. He noted that aircraft can be purchased directly from manufacturers, through authorised representatives, or via brokers—arguing that intermediaries can sometimes be commercially advantageous. Describing the international aircraft market as controlled by a “mafia,” Prof. Fernando criticised SriLankan Airlines’ decision to pursue the A350 procurement as foolish.

His comments highlight the complexity of aircraft procurement, but complexity cannot excuse opacity. There is an essential distinction between a legitimate commercial commission and an illegal payment intended to influence a decision-maker. The existence of a commission does not establish corruption. The crucial questions are -- who received it, why it was paid, what service was provided, who authorised it, and whether it was intended to influence the decision.

The present allegations against Rajapaksa appear to arise from evidence obtained through a subsequent investigation. Following an inquiry that included an affidavit from businessman Nimal Perera, the Colombo Chief Magistrate’s Court was told that Perera alleged he handed Rs. 100 million to Rajapaksa in connection with funds received through the Airbus deal. That remains an allegation. Perera’s credibility and evidentiary value must ultimately be determined through the judicial process.

Deputy Solicitor General Janaka Bandara told court that Rajapaksa’s arrest followed a joint investigation by CIABOC and the CID, with help from authorities in Australia, Singapore, France, and the United States. According to court submissions, €1.45 million had allegedly been paid by Airbus in connection with the transaction. Investigators examined funds originating from a Singapore bank account under “Biz Solution,” owned by Priyanka Niyomali Wijenayake, wife of former CEO Kapila Chandrasena. Funds from that account were transferred to a Singapore account belonging to a company chaired by Nimal Perera.

These transactions may form an evidentiary chain, but prosecutors must establish a direct, provable connection between the money and the alleged payment to Rajapaksa.

The arrest itself risks becoming the political headline while deeper institutional failure disappears from view. SriLankan Airlines did not become financially troubled because of one politician, nor did the Airbus controversy emerge in isolation. It developed within a system where successive governments exercised influence over state institutions, appointments were politicised, and major decisions lacked transparency.

Following a change of government, SriLankan Airlines sought to withdraw from some aircraft arrangements, incurring penalties exceeding US$100 million. A high-powered inquiry followed, yet despite recommendations that former executives face prosecution, those investigations did not result in convictions. If corruption occurred, why did it take so long to pursue? If evidence existed, why did it fail to yield convictions? These questions cannot be answered simply by arresting another politician.

The NPP Government faces a challenge larger than securing convictions against political opponents. It must prove that its anti-corruption campaign is about changing the system rather than changing the names of the people who control it.The Government should be judged on whether it strengthens procurement institutions, protects state enterprises from political interference, improves oversight, enforces conflict-of-interest rules, and ensures independent prosecution. The number of arrests is a poor measure of anti-corruption success. Convictions based on credible evidence, recovery of stolen public money, and durable institutional reforms are far better measures. Sri Lanka has seen enough political theatre; governments have long promised to punish predecessors’ corruption while preserving the institutional weaknesses that enabled it.

There is a legitimate public demand for accountability, but accountability without due process can become political vengeance. An arrest is not a conviction, an affidavit is not proof, and a foreign judgment that does not name an individual cannot be transformed into a finding against them. Conversely, the absence of a name from an earlier judgment should not shield anyone from legitimate new evidence. The correct path is neither automatic condemnation nor automatic exoneration; it is evidence. If investigators possess credible evidence linking Namal Rajapaksa to an illegal payment, they must present it in court.

The 2022 collapse was driven by weak institutions, policy failures, excessive borrowing, mismanagement, and corruption. Restoring public trust requires showing that rules apply equally to politicians, officials, businessmen, and state institutions alike. The Government must resist measuring success by high-profile arrests.

Its real achievement would be building institutions strong enough that future governments cannot repeat past abuses. This focus on institutional reform is by no means an attempt to shield or clear corrupt politicians; accountability must be absolute.
