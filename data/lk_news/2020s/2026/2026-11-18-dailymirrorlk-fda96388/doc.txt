South Asian students studying abroad  face tough challenges

Over 1.8 million students from India, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Nepal, and Sri Lanka have been pursuing education abroad since 2022

It is also important for Western educational institutions to first understand the cultural context of South Asian students, instead of rushing to punish them if they did not meet local standards

International Students’ Day falls on November 17. Though it marks Czechoslovakian students’ resistance to Nazi brutality in November 1939, the occasion is used to take stock of the contemporary condition of students in various parts of the world. Here is a profile of South Asian students studying abroad and the challenges they face.

In the last few years, South Asia has emerged as a powerhouse of international student mobility. Over 1.8 million students from India, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Nepal, and Sri Lanka have been pursuing education abroad since 2022. In 2025, South Asian students constituted 28 % of the university student population in Canada, 22% in the UK, 15% in Australia, 7% in Germany.

In overseas student enrolment, India continues to lead. But Bangladesh, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka have also shown accelerated outbound growth. Among South Asian students, there is a rising interest in Science, Technology, Engineering and Maths (STEM), healthcare, business analytics and public policy programmes.

Post-degree work rights and migration pathways remain key motivations to go abroad. However, post-degree employment has been coming under scrutiny and restrictions, especially in the US.

According to www.pienews.com, over 750,000 Indian students were in universities abroad in 2022, rising to 930,000 in 2023 and peaking at 1.33 million in 2024. Despite Ottawa’s clampdown on international students with 74% of Indian study permit applications rejected in August 2025, Canada still hosts the largest number of Indian students, with 427,085 on the rolls.

In the US, despite a 44% drop in study visas for Indian students in August 2025, compared to 2024, India remained the largest source of foreign students with 255,000 enrolled (31% of all international students).

The Indian Ministry of External Affairs (MEA) showed that in 2025, the UK had 173,190 Indian students; Australia 138,579; Germany 59,000;  Russia 27,000; Kyrgyzstan 16,500; and Georgia 16,000.

According to another study, females accounted for 40% of Indian students abroad. Interestingly, these females came from smaller cities like Coimbatore, Indore and Bhubaneswar.

Policy changes in the host countries are impacting Indian students’ decisions.  Canada has cut new international study permit allocations by 49 to 55% as part of a broader crackdown on temporary resident numbers. Nine out of 10 students now fear for their visas.

Postgraduate enrolments are falling across UK universities, even though institutions there are facing a potential loss of £925-per-international student denied admission.

Meanwhile, the annual cost of studying in the US has risen over the five years, to British pounds 8,200 to 8,300. With the result, there has been a rise of new destinations such as Germany, Russia, Kyrgyzstan and Georgia which are lower-cost destinations, especially for STEM and medical education.

From Bangladesh, more than 120,000 students went abroad between 2022 and 2025. Although, the US, the UK, Australia, Canada, Germany, Japan and some other European countries are the usual destinations for Bangladeshi students, in recent years many have opted for other countries.

According to the UNESCO data, the United Arab Emirates is the top destination for Bangladeshis with 11,157 students followed by the USA with 8,665 students and Malaysia with 6,180. There has also been a surge in applications to Canada, with many students using it as a migration pathway.

There is a strong demand for foreign education in Pakistan though  Pakistanis also face visa challenges. But undeterred by this, 150,000 Pakistani students were abroad in 2022-25. Typically, they headed to UK, USA, Australia and Germany. Rejections from UK and US in 2024–25 triggered increased interest in Turkey and Malaysia.

Pakistani applicants face strict vetting on financial proof, authentic student intent, and soaring rejection rates in key markets. For instance, recent reports highlight that UK student visa rejection rates for Pakistan climbed sharply (reaching over 40% in recent tracking), prompting students to look towards more accessible study hubs.

More than 95,000 Nepalese were abroad for education in the 2022-25 period, mainly in Canada, but also in Australia, UK and Japan. But Australia’s 2025 rules for Genuine Temporary Entrants (GTE) led to decreased interest, pushing students toward Canada and Japan.

Subjects of interest to Nepalese were Nursing, Hospitality, Environmental Science, and IT. Nepalese students were heavily reliant on Nepalese education consultants and were therefore vulnerable to misinformation.

Between 2022 and 2025, 45,000 Sri Lankan students went abroad for education, mostly to UK, Australia, India, Malaysia, and Germany. There has been a growing interest in STEM and postgraduate coursework programmes in Medicine, Finance, Education and Psychology. There is also a growing interest in getting scholarships through Commonwealth programmes, particularly in the UK and Australia.

A study entitled “Sri Lankan Students’ Perceptions and the Factors that Affect Migrating for Their Higher Studies”  found that students cited the high quality of education abroad (56.4%), economic instability in Sri Lanka (53.6%), and limited enrolment and resources in government universities (20%) as reasons for going overseas.

Students preferred studying abroad also due to dissatisfaction with the local higher education system. University graduates tend to migrate for education and not return home. This can cause both short-term and long-term brain drain and outflow of foreign exchange.

Among the challenges faced by South Asian students are visa rejections (mainly due to weak reporting, financial gaps and issues in the interview); rising tuition and housing costs; language barriers in both English- and non-English-speaking destinations and dependence on unregulated agents.

There has been a 36% year-over-year decline in new US F-1 visa (student Visa) issuances as of March 2026. India, the largest source of foreign students, recorded a 60% drop in the 2025 summer issuances. On January 12, 2026, the State Department said that it had revoked more than 100,000 visas, including 8,000 student visas “to keep America safe.” President Trump had also ordered to stop vias to citizens of 39 countries.

For decades, the US allowed foreign students to remain in the United States as long as they maintained their studies. But under the new system, those who need more time must file for an extension through the US Citizenship and Immigration Service (USCIS), pay a $420 fee (as of May 2026), and complete biometrics.

However, on Sept 14, 2026, a federal judge in Massachusetts issued a nationwide preliminary injunction temporarily blocking that order.

By going ahead with these restrictions, the US is said to be foregoing a lot of income - $43 billion annually.

Aside from all these adverse systemic challenges, there are social and cultural barriers that South Asian students, especially from India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, encounter. Food, language disability and cultural inhibitions stand in the way social interactions and integration with locals. This is both in the classroom and outside.

Younger students feel more homesick than older ones. Some researchers suggest that universities should organise orientation sessions for students from the Global South, and social workers should visit such students to advice them on behaviour expectations in the host society.

The Western education system is based on self-study and free expression of ideas even in opposition to handed-down knowledge. But this is alien to the majority of South Asian students who are used to the top-down approach.

South Asian students from non-elite institutions back home, tend to be accused of plagiarism. They become more professional over time, but initial rejections by the system can be very hurtful. Therefore, academic expectations should be made clear to them at the very outset, and they should be encouraged to think on their own, express themselves in their own words, and cite sources if information and analyses are borrowed.

It is also important for Western educational institutions to first understand the cultural context of South Asian students, instead of rushing to punish them if they did not meet local standards.
