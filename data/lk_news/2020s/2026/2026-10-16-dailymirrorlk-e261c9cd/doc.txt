Katchatheevu is a small, uninhabited island of about 285 acres (1.15 square kilometers) in the Palk Strait, between Rameswaram on India’s coast and Delft (Neduntheevu) in Sri Lanka. It lies within Sri Lanka’s sovereign jurisdiction and is administered as part of the Northern Province. It has no permanent residents, yet every year thousands of Catholic pilgrims from both countries cross to the island for the annual feast of St. Anthony’s Church, a multi-day festival held usually in March, which has long drawn fishing communities from both sides of the strait.

On 6th October 2026, India’s Supreme Court heard petitions challenging the 1974 and 1976 agreements with Sri Lanka. The petitioners wanted Katchatheevu returned to India. The bench, Justices Sanjay Kumar and Sanjeev Sachdeva, granted no relief at this stage. It urged the Union and Tamil Nadu governments to caution Tamil Nadu’s fishermen against entering Sri Lankan waters, and adjourned the case to February 2027. The order is interim, procedural and humanitarian. The court has neither reopened nor struck down the agreements, and the constitutional questions remain before it.

The Indian Attorney General told the court that the Centre does not wish to reopen the issue with Sri Lanka, citing India’s excellent relationship with its neighbour. The Court’s own concern was the repeated arrest of Indian fishermen. This may look like a routine direction, but it matters more than it appears. The petitioners asked the judiciary to treat an understanding between two governments as a wrong, and the court did not accept that invitation. The government, under pressure to bring the island back, declined to reopen the settlement. Neither institution endorsed the call to revisit it.

For Colombo, this moment calls for calm. The signal is welcome, but the dispute was never only about territory. It is about livelihoods and a strained marine environment. Fishermen on both sides of the strait earn a precarious living from a sea that can no longer sustain the way they fish. Arrests, grievances and political friction will continue until the economics change.

Sri Lanka therefore has two tasks. It must hold the boundary firmly, because the principle that states keep their agreements matters most to smaller nations. It must also approach India patiently on fisheries, because a settled border cannot sit alongside unsettled livelihoods for long.

The legal position is not hard to follow. In June 1974, Prime Ministers Indira Gandhi and Sirimavo Bandaranaike agreed on the boundary in the historic waters between the two countries, placing Katchatheevu on the Sri Lankan side. In March 1976, they agreed on the maritime boundary in the Gulf of Mannar and the Bay of Bengal, and the two foreign secretaries exchanged letters. Under the 1976 arrangements, each country’s fishermen are barred from fishing in the other’s historic waters, territorial sea and exclusive economic zone without express permission.

Two points deserve emphasis. First, the settlement was a negotiated boundary, not a gift. Article 5 of the 1974 agreement allows Indian fishermen and pilgrims to visit Katchatheevu without travel documents or visas. Tamil Nadu’s parties have long argued, relying also on Article 6 on traditional rights, that the agreement means more. Yet India’s government told its Supreme Court in 2013 that this access does not include fishing rights around the island, and the 1976 arrangements addressed the fishing question in plain terms.

Second, India has never formally rejected the settlement. In August 2014, Attorney General Mukul Rohatgi told the Supreme Court that the island went to Sri Lanka by agreement in 1974, and that retrieving it would require going to war. In 2013, the Centre had argued that no Indian territory was ceded, because British India and Ceylon had disputed the island and no boundary had ever been agreed. The arguments differ, but both lead to the same place: the matter is closed. Political statements in India, particularly at election time, have at times suggested otherwise, but the Centre’s formal position before its highest court has remained consistent. Sri Lanka should value this consistency. Agreements between states are worth something only if they are not reopened whenever a domestic constituency finds them inconvenient.

Katchatheevu is a barren speck with no fresh water. Its main feature is the small church of St. Anthony. No one would call it an economic prize; its weight is symbolic. In Tamil Nadu, the island stands for a feeling of being overlooked. The state’s fishermen work some of the most heavily fished waters in the region, and many links the 1974 and 1976 settlements to the loss of grounds their families once fished. Whether or not that is historically accurate, the feeling is real and deep. Such a symbol is politically durable. It costs little to invoke, and evidence about fish stocks cannot easily disprove it. It also allows the grievance to be directed outward, across the water, rather than towards the harder problems of falling catches, too many trawlers and depleted inshore waters.

Sri Lanka can acknowledge the sentiment behind this issue while holding its position firmly, since that position rests on law and on a settled agreement. The concerns of Tamil Nadu’s fishermen may be sincere, but they cannot be answered at the expense of Sri Lanka’s sovereignty or of the northern fishermen whose waters and livelihoods are at stake. A courteous and understanding approach, offered from a position of strength and not of concession, will serve Sri Lanka well in law and in regional goodwill.

The real dispute lies not on the islet but beneath the water. The Palk Bay is a shallow, partly enclosed sea, with Tamil Nadu on one side and the Northern Province of Sri Lanka on the other. It has long been rich in prawn and other bottom-dwelling species.

The difficulty is how the catch is now taken. Bottom trawling drags a weighted net across the seabed. It is efficient but destructive. It scrapes away the habitat that young fish and crustaceans need, catches large quantities of unwanted species, and damages the gear of small-scale fishermen. The grounds on the Indian side of the line have been heavily exploited, and crossing is tempting and makes economic sense for each boat owner, even though it is illegal. Justice Sanjay Kumar himself observed at the hearing that Indian fishermen had heavily exploited the waters near their own coast, while Sri Lanka appeared to have protected its fisheries with greater care.

Northern Sri Lankan fishing communities, many still recovering from the war, bear the cost. Fishing in the north was restricted during the conflict, and a generation of fishermen lost their livelihoods and capital. They returned to a sea that had been fished intensively in their absence. In Jaffna, Mannar and Mullaitivu, the sight of trawlers damaging nets and taking the catch on which they depend is regarded as a continuing injustice, a matter of both income and dignity. The arrests are the visible symptom. Boats are impounded and cases go to court. Each incident is reported in Tamil Nadu as the detention of poor fishermen by a foreign navy. The cycle repeats because the underlying economics have not changed.

The court’s call for awareness is sensible but not sufficient. Many fishermen who cross the line know exactly where it is; the GPS on their boats tells them. They cross because the reward outweighs the risk, and because penalties often fall on crews rather than on the owners who profit from the catch. The problem is one of incentive, not ignorance.

It would be unrealistic to expect the court’s direction alone to quieten Tamil Nadu’s politics. The issue arises at election time, after an arrest, and whenever parties across the spectrum wish to show their support for fishing communities.

Sri Lanka should see this as part of India’s domestic politics, not a change in Indian policy, and should respond calmly, restating its position consistently. Argument alone will not end the issue. A lasting solution must improve the circumstances of the fishermen themselves, and so remove the material that feeds the politics.

India’s relationship with Sri Lanka is strategic. It covers security cooperation, energy and connectivity projects, trade, and a shared interest in a stable Palk Strait. Reopening the 1974 settlement would weaken the principle that maritime boundaries are settled by agreement, and could invite challenges to India’s own settlements with other neighbours.

Returning the island would not give Tamil Nadu’s fishermen what they want either. The islet is of no use to them as a fishing base. They seek access to richer grounds, and no legal claim to the island would secure that. Both governments, attentive to these realities, may find lasting common ground in cooperative fisheries management, sustainable livelihood policy and the responsible regulation of trawler fleets.

Sri Lanka can take encouragement from this, while recognising that every position is shaped by wider considerations. India’s stance reflects a careful assessment of its long-term interests, and such interests naturally evolve in response to domestic developments. Colombo’s task is to continue nurturing the shared interests that sustain the friendship between the two countries, and to advance a constructive, workable approach to the fishery that both sides can welcome.

The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) offers a legal framework for both tasks, with two cautions.

Article 123 covers cooperation between states bordering an enclosed or semi-enclosed sea. It asks them to coordinate the management, conservation and use of living resources, the protection of the marine environment, and scientific research. Whether the Palk Bay meets the Convention’s definition is arguable, and the Article asks states only to “endeavour”, not to achieve a result. It is best read as a model for joint management, not as a source of enforceable duties.

Article 73 governs enforcement in the exclusive economic zone (EEZ), the waters beyond the territorial sea where a coastal state controls fishing and other resources. It permits boarding, inspection and arrest. But it requires prompt release of vessels and crews upon the posting of a reasonable bond, bars imprisonment for fisheries offences unless the states concerned agree otherwise, and requires prompt notice to the flag state, the country where the vessel is registered. It does not apply in the territorial sea, where Sri Lanka’s powers are wider. Sri Lanka’s position is therefore strongest where arrests clearly take place in its territorial waters, and weakest where the location is disputed or lies in the EEZ. Either way, proportionate and humane enforcement is sound policy and serves Sri Lanka’s own interests.

Build on the diplomatic gain, quietly. India’s statement before its own highest court, that it does not wish to reopen the agreement, is a significant signal made on the record. Sri Lanka should refer to it in future talks without any show of triumph. Public celebration would embarrass a partner that has just acted responsibly under domestic pressure.

Treat enforcement as a means, not an end. Sri Lanka has the right to protect its waters, and arresting illegal fishermen is lawful. But reliance on enforcement alone does not work and can backfire. Long detentions and seized boats feed the grievance in Tamil Nadu and give critics in India their best material. Humane treatment, prompt consular access, timely release where justified, and a distinction between crews and owners serve both countries. The purpose should be to end the practice, not to cause suffering to those who have turned to it out of necessity.

This is the heart of the matter. Sri Lanka prohibited bottom trawling in its own waters through legislative amendments in 2017, which gives it a principled basis for asking its neighbour to do the same in the shared bay. But a demand with nothing on offer will fail. The practical path is a negotiated, time-bound transition that reduces trawler numbers in phases, backed by Indian programmes to convert boats for deep-sea fishing, train crews, and move into aquaculture, seaweed farming and processing. This has been discussed for years. It now needs a decision, a timetable and a budget.

Communities in the Northern Province bear the costs of the present arrangement. They should be stakeholders at the table, not merely subjects of discussion. The government should consult their organisations when shaping Sri Lanka’s position, compensate them for gear damaged by foreign vessels, and speed up investment in landing sites, cold storage and market access. A prosperous northern fishery is the best defence of Sri Lanka’s waters.

For half a century, Katchatheevu has carried concerns that reach well beyond the islet itself, on how territory is transferred, the welfare of fishing communities, and the relationship between a state government and the national capital. Possession of a small island will not resolve them. Thoughtful engagement with the people who hold them, and arrangements that give them a real stake in a peaceful sea, offer a far better prospect.

The boundary at Katchatheevu rests on firmer ground than it has in some years, and Sri Lanka values the restraint shown by India’s judiciary and government. It would be fitting to honour that goodwill through courteous diplomacy, a measured tone and a continued readiness for dialogue. The task before two friendly neighbours is to ensure that the line across these waters becomes not a point of division but a shared space, where both countries care for their common sea with dignity, restraint and mutual respect.
