This article is based on an oration organised by the Sri Lanka Association for the Advancement of Science (SLAAS) in honour of the late Prof. S. R. Kottegoda, who laid the foundation for research ethics in Sri Lanka and made significant contributions to medical education, research and practice. Prof. Kottegoda established SLAAS’s ethics committee, the oldest in Sri Lanka. He was committed to ensuring that scientific research was carried out with integrity, accountability and respect for human values.

Reviewing the history of research ethics is important for laying the foundation for this article. The earliest recorded cases involving research ethics relate directly to medicine and date from 1500 to 1700. The Nazi human experiments conducted on prisoners during the 1930s and 1940s are probably the first comprehensively investigated and well-documented major ethical violations in research. They led to the establishment of the Nuremberg Code (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Code), which required voluntary consent, risk minimisation, and scientific necessity. The Tuskegee Syphilis Study and the discovery of congenital disabilities in children due to an inadequately tested drug raised growing concerns about research ethics. A major international development in research ethics was the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki (https://www.wma.net/), which set out ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects. Another development was the Belmont Report (https://www.hhs.gov/ohrp/regulations-and-policy/belmont-report/index.html), which established the principles of respect for persons, beneficence, and justice. As a result, institutions conducting medical research established research ethics committees and boards to oversee research involving human subjects and ensure ethical standards.

Today, global research is a multi-trillion-dollar enterprise funded by governments and others, including individual donors, industry and business. We conduct research in a complex environment where competition for funding, conflicts of interest, research data, the training of highly qualified people, research dissemination, national and international rankings, and other factors affect research quality, integrity and outcomes. The importance of research ethics extends far beyond medicine and research involving human subjects. Therefore, this article addresses a topic at the very core of the research enterprise: research ethics and the training of research degree students.

At no point in history has this responsibility been more significant than it is now, as artificial intelligence reshapes research methods, conduct, and dissemination. Data can be collected, tampered with, analysed, and disseminated at unprecedented speed. Research collaborations increasingly transcend geographical, legal, and cultural boundaries, while political ideologies and differences have affected research through boycotts and restrictions on collaboration. Public trust in expertise is continually tested.

Training research degree students (e.g., MPhil/PhD) is an important responsibility of the modern research enterprise. Students participate in grant proposal preparation, conduct research, create and manage data, and contribute to research dissemination. They are the future guardians of the research enterprise, and their training must include research ethics, integrity and responsible conduct. In this context, several issues at the core of the modern research enterprise require attention.

A key message here is that research ethics is no longer simply about compliance; it is essential to maintaining trust in scholarship. Assuming researchers are trustworthy, we are granted extraordinary privileges such as:

Access to people to conduct research, including the use of human and animal subjects for research purposes

Academic freedom (freedom of inquiry, right to disseminate results, freedom to challenge conventional thought, freedom to express opinion, and freedom from institutional censorship)

In return, society expects researchers to act responsibly. Important to recognise that research ethics emerged largely in response to historical ethical failures.

Training institutions must ensure that research degree students have more than disciplinary and methodological expertise. Students must be trained in moral judgement. The best approach for supervisors is not just to preach ethics but to practise it at every stage of research. Students readily follow their supervisors’ practices and judgements. Research advisors must educate students about these responsibilities, and students should constantly reflect on them when they engage in research. Therefore, supervisors must always emphasise that ethics protects participants, researchers, institutions and public confidence.

Around the world, examples show that ethical failures rarely arise from ignorance alone. They often emerge from poor research cultures. Unfortunately, these cultures grow because of the competitiveness of the research enterprise.

As institutions develop integrity-based research cultures, five international best practices have emerged.

a) Ethics across the entire research lifecycle – Requires ethics to be embedded throughout proposal development, data collection, experimentation, analysis, publication and dissemination. Ethics does not end once the ethics committee approves the proposal. In fact, it really starts after the committee’s approval.

b) Mandatory integrity training - Many institutions today require ethics certification, integrity modules and refresher courses before students commence independent research.

c) Supervisor training - An important aspect of research student training is the relationship between supervisors and students. Supervisory behaviour strongly influences student practices. Students often adopt the ethical standards their supervisors demonstrate.

d) Open science and data stewardship - With the increasing focus on transparent research and open access to research data, scholarly journals and granting agencies now require researchers to provide access to the data and methods used in their research. Today, credible research journals require authors to include a statement on data access and sufficient details about methods and tools.

e) Community and indigenous engagement - Research increasingly involves community and Indigenous engagement and emphasises the importance of respecting traditions, values and cultures when conducting research. Under these conditions, modern ethical frameworks require recognising participants as partners rather than subjects. This represents a significant shift in ethical thinking.

Readers interested in best practices may review the following sites and documents: UK Research Integrity Office, Canadian Tri-Council Responsible Conduct of Research Framework, Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research, Singapore Statement on Research Integrity and European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity.

Canada’s Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans (TCPS 2) is among the world’s most comprehensive research ethics frameworks (https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/policy-politique_tcps2-eptc2_2022.html). It is jointly administered by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research, the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council, and the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council.

Its importance lies in its emphasis on principles rather than rules. Three core principles underpin TCPS 2. They are: Respect for Persons, Concern for Welfare and Justice. Respect for Persons recognises human dignity, autonomy, and informed consent; Concern for Welfare must consider physical, psychological, economic, and social wellbeing; and Justice must ensure fair participant selection, inclusion, and equity. These three principles form the ethical foundation for research involving humans. TCPS 2 provides flexibility, allows ethical judgment rather than rigid compliance, encourages inclusivity, and applies across disciplines. TCPS also emphasises risk-based reviews, requiring more intensive scrutiny for higher-risk research.

An important element of the TCPS is its dedicated approach to research involving Indigenous communities. The framework emphasises respect, reciprocity, partnership and community engagement. A key element of TCPS 2 is moving from protecting participants to respecting them.

The modern research enterprise requires us to recognise that research ethics and integrity extend far beyond protection for human participants. The Canadian Tri-Agency Framework: Responsible Conduct of Research (https://rcr.ethics.gc.ca/eng/home.html) establishes responsibilities for researchers, institutions and funding agencies. The objective of RCRF is to strengthen research integrity throughout the research ecosystem. Key expectations include honesty, presenting findings truthfully, rigour, using appropriate methods, transparency, maintaining complete records, accountability, accepting responsibility for decisions, stewardship, and responsible management of funding. The framework also identifies misconduct such as fabrication, falsification, plagiarism, inappropriate authorship, misrepresentation and mismanagement of research funds.

For research degrees, there are high entry requirements, prestigious scholarships, rigorous assessments of research progress and publication requirements. Yet, most research degree students earn their degrees without understanding research ethics and integrity. Universities increasingly recognise the importance of research ethics and integrity, regardless of the research topic. Research degree programs must follow mandatory ethics and integrity training for all research degree students. International best practices require every doctoral and master’s student to complete structured training on ethics and the Responsible Conduct of Research. Supervisors have a moral responsibility to ensure that students understand publication ethics (acknowledgement of literature, plagiarism, proper authorship), data management (data records, data falsification, data ownership, etc.), AI governance (writing, analysis, funding agency and publication guidelines), authorship (no bogus authors, authorship reflects contributions), and conflict of interest (personal conflicts, favouritism, funding, etc.).

Supervisor development is an important element of institutional responsibility. Supervision of research degree students should be limited to those who have completed formal research ethics preparation. Many universities and granting agencies have prepared literature and developed tools to support research ethics training for supervisors.

Universities should foster an ethical research culture by promoting and rewarding integrity, mentorship and responsible scholarship, rather than focusing solely on funding amounts and the number of papers published during performance reviews. For professorial promotions, emphasis should be placed on publication quality, the rigour of journals’ peer-review processes, the accomplishments of former research students, and peer recognitions, rather than turning promotion evaluation into a bean-counting exercise.

Reflective practices require students to be encouraged to discuss regularly the ethical dilemmas that arise during their candidature. Developing ethics through reflection, rather than regulation alone, is best practice. As researchers, we must always be self-critical, examining the quality and value of our work and constantly aspiring to meet high standards. The research degree students we train today are the future custodians of scholarship. Therefore, they must be trained not only to conduct research and publish papers but also to be ethical scholars worthy of public trust.

Therefore, universities have a responsibility to cultivate researchers who can not only discover new knowledge but also be trusted to create it.

AI will profoundly affect research and research integrity. AI presents both opportunities and challenges for research. It will provide new research opportunities and enhance scientific discovery. We must carefully address its ethical use in research and develop recommendations and guidelines to ensure it does not open the door to research misconduct and unethical practices. Funding agencies and universities have already begun addressing this important issue, and guidelines and recommendations are either in place or under development. In a recent article, David B. Resnik and Mohammad Hosseini, Northwestern University, USA, AI and Ethics (2025) 5:1499–1521, present nine recommendations covering ethical use of AI in research.

Researchers are privileged individuals, and the public, governments, and business and industry support, celebrate and respect them. Over the past decades, funding for scientific research has increased, more advanced research infrastructure has been built, and a large global pool of talent has become available. So, while research resources are growing, there is also a growing trend of declining trust and ethical lapses.

Sri Lankan universities, research institutes, and funding agencies must establish frameworks on research ethics and integrity, develop guidelines on the responsible conduct of research, offer mandatory research ethics and integrity training for grantees, research supervisors, and graduate students, and develop mechanisms to investigate research misconduct.
