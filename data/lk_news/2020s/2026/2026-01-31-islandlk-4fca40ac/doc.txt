After We Leave Home

Ever since I moved to Canada, my friends, especially the ones closest to me have often asked what it’s like here and what my impressions were. For some reason, they almost always assume I left because I grew tired of Sri Lanka, which couldn’t be further from the truth. For a long time, I never had a sensible response for them. Perhaps I wasn’t mature enough then to fully understand the predicament I was in back then. But now, after a few years have passed, I suspect it was a culmination of curiosity that brought me here. A desire to see what the outside world had to offer, the kind we grew up watching on television, listening to in music, or seeing unfold in movies along with a longing for broader cultural exposure.

It may sound naive, but it was innocent, too. But more than anything, I craved freedom. Like many people my age, I had spent years living under what I sarcastically called the “wrath of a school regime.” However, I also realized that very period, once felt suffocating has now slowly become a memory I return to fondly, every now and then.

Having my childhood spent entirely in Sri Lanka and some time here in North America, I think it’s safe to say that schools here feel noticeably plainer than ours. If I had to compare them to a delicacy, I’d say North American schools resembled something like a regular pancake, consistent, mild in flavor, and guaranteed to get the job done. Schools in Sri Lanka, on the other hand, were like those traditional pancakes, crisp on the outside, soft in texture, and filled with a sweetness laced with a hint of spice. They also come in many shapes and forms, sometimes inconsistent, but exceptional when the recipe was right.

Hence, I often wondered how different our country might look if every child had access to the same facilities as those in cities like Colombo, instead of facing such stark disparities in resources. I also admired how foreign institutions didn’t place the same level of pressure on students and how their curricula emphasized practical skills such as public speaking and also visual learning, rather than hours spent memorizing textbooks. They also didn’t seem to endure the fierce competition we faced, from Grade five scholarship exams all the way to A/Ls, something I still envy to this day.

“You talk too much about school. You’re not a kid anymore,” my parents would joke, often placing extra emphasis on the word kid, more than I would’ve liked. Unfortunately for me, these comments usually surfaced when they were hearing my treasured stories for the millionth time. “Fine,” I’d yell back. “I’m never telling those stories again,” I’d declare, locking myself in my room as if to make a point, knowing fully well that both my parents and I were aware that I’d bring them up again in less than a week.

I remember a one moment when a friend of mine in Canada was astonished after seeing a photo of me in my school uniform. She had begged me to share it after I saw hers. Having never been required to wear a uniform herself, she was utterly bedazzled.

“That’s you?” she shouted, what sounded like all caps inside our college union hall.

“What’s that hair?” she asked, trying her best to mask her laughter.

“That’s how they wanted us to do it, okay?” I replied defensively, justifying what, in her opinion, was one of the most unfortunate hairstyles of the twenty-first century.

“Our school,” I said, knowing fully well that someone who could voluntarily wear pajamas to school had no real concept of how extreme, sometimes absurd our school system could be.

Despite having access to a higher standard of living, personally, I don’t think overseas life suits those who crave daily human interaction the way most Sri Lankans unknowingly do. In many countries, casually chatting with your neighbours simply because you have time or because you haven’t seen them in a while would feel almost intrusive as many people there seem to find their comfort and emotional support in solitude most likely through a pet. That’s why I always believed those of my grandmother’s generation could never spend more than a couple of months here.

A few years ago, I used to see an elderly couple, both around 80, on my walk home from college every other day. They would sit under a tree on Adirondack chairs in their garden, facing the street, which felt like a rare sight. Over time, their friendly smiles turned into small talk.

“It’s too boring inside the house, and it’s summer,” the older woman would say, almost as if she needed to justify their presence outdoors.

“I’d do the same,” I’d reply, perhaps because they reminded me so much of my own grandmother. She would sit on her small chair beneath the ambarella tree in our garden, which she claimed was older than she was, gazing over the yard for hours. Sometimes I’d join her, curious about what held her attention, but I could never last more than 15 minutes before growing restless. But now I realize that may have been her quiet form of therapy, her daily dose of serenity, being in touch with nature and reminiscing about years she believed were behind her.

As time passed, I noticed that many of my school friends, like me, are scattered across different countries soon after graduating, a trend that’s only grown stronger in recent years. When we talk now, the one common thread is how much we miss the simple act of planning to hang out, something that has since become occasion worthy. We often laugh about childhood stories, like the afternoon one of our daredevil friends fell into an abandoned pond at school while searching for the “goldfish” she claimed to have seen the day before.

“You guys stay back and watch me,” she reassured us, knowing none of us would dare cross the fence surrounding the pond. After failing to spot the fish, she decided to get closer. “Don’t make any noise,” she warned us, seconds before losing her balance and plunging in.

“Help me!” she screamed, as a loud splash confirmed our worst fear.

“Tadpoles. That’s all they’ve got here, nothing else,” she muttered afterward, drenched, shaken, and trying to hide her embarrassment. Luckily, we found a spare uniform in the first-aid room, and the incident went unnoticed by teachers. Years later ironically, that same friend went on to became one of the best swimmers in school and is now preparing to get married later this year, reminding how fleeting time truly is.

As I grow older, there’s one quote that rings truer with each passing day, “time waits for no one.” For many, moving abroad is a dream, a strategic decision, or both. What often goes unspoken, however, is the quiet cost, the family events, milestones, and everyday moments missed back home. Even when people pretend to be unfazed, the absence can weigh heavily, even on the strongest among us.

And once we leave our native land, everything connected to it begins to feel slightly enchanted. Even in photographs, the sun looks brighter, the sky bluer, and smiles wider. Perhaps it’s nostalgia that tightens our grip on memory, making us long, even for a short visit, hoping that the same charm still lingers in the places we now carry with us.
