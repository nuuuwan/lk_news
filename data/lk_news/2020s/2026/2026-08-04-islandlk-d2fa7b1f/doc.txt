Tarnished Pearl: How short-sighted governance is squandering Sri Lanka’s greatest treasures

Sri Lanka has long been celebrated as the Pearl of the Indian Ocean; a nation blessed with an extraordinary concentration of natural beauty, ancient history, fertile lands, and strategic maritime real estate. The dazzling brilliance of a pearl has been the hallmark of this island from time immemorial. Yet for all that, to look closely at how these immense gifts are currently managed is to witness a tragedy of the most loathsome systemic neglect. Through a mix of bureaucratic inertia, lack of vision, and short-sighted commercialisation, those entrusted with the nation’s stewardship are allowing the radiant sheen of this pearl to dull and go down to an unrecognisable level.

Instead of crafting a high-value, culturally rich national brand that honours our heritage and unleashes our unlimited economic potential, authorities have settled for reactive, short-sighted low-yield strategies. We are sitting on a land with world-defining historical milestones, breath-taking coastal routes, and unrivalled natural resources, yet we have foolishly elected to sit back and treat them as virtual afterthoughts. It is time to examine what Sri Lanka possesses, how terribly underutilised it is, and how a refined, visionary approach could transform the island into a global benchmark of excellence and prosperity.

Nowhere is our institutional thoughtlessness more glaring than in how we handle one of our undisputedly unique archaeological and historical legacies. Any nation with a fraction of Sri Lanka’s history regarding an ancient medical wonder would broadcast its achievements to every corner of the globe. Consider Mihintale, with documented historical and archaeological evidence confirming that it was home to the world’s first dedicated hospital complex, dating back to the 9th century, and built upon the healing traditions that spanned centuries prior.

Centuries ago, in the sacred, emerald precinct of Mihintale, the visionary Monarch, King Sena II, established what history records as the very first organised hospital complex in the entire world. It is a testament to an ancient kingdom built upon the bedrock of compassion, wisdom, and medical foresight. It is that very same spirit of relentless benevolence that would summon millions of tourists to come to see this wonder, if only that undeniable association was widely known and broadcast globally. The ruins clearly point to a blueprint of Peripheral Consulting Rooms, Inner Courtyards, Central Buildings for In-Ward Residential Management of ill patients, Hydrotherapy Baths, Medicinal Troughs, Granite Baths, Medicinal Herbal Preparation Units, and many other facilities that are required to treat human ill health. Though millennia have passed since those granite medicinal troughs were carved, they remain as poignant reminders of an indigenous medical system that was one of the wonders of the world.

This exceptional ancient hospital, the very first of its kind in the world, was not a randomly thought-out rudimentary shelter for the sick. It was a superbly planned and sophisticatedly designed blueprint for even modern healthcare architecture. It represents a landmark in human civilisation and the moment healthcare transitioned into a structured, compassionate, and scientific institution.

All it needs now is to preserve the ancient remains with great care and to build a sheltered, miniature, and absolutely accurate replica of the hospital adjacent to the ruins, to portray and explain how it would have worked all those centuries ago. Well-educated and superbly informed resident staff could explain to tourists the intricacies of how that magnificent institution would have worked in the good old days, providing a health service that was a singular characteristic of a caring nation and how that prototype works as a shining model to guide even the designs of modern healthcare institutions of repute..

If such a site existed in Europe or East Asia or, for that matter, in any other country, it would be a world-renowned pilgrimage ground for medical historians, researchers, and global tourists. The nation to which it belongs would take every conceivable measure to make full usage of such an inheritance. They would indeed practically go to town. It would be made to anchor international medical symposiums and feature interactive heritage centres. They would have minted money using just the reputation and the façade. Instead, at Mihintale, this monumental testament to ancient Sinhala medical science receives little more than a weathered signboard and passive neglect. The morons that we have around do not even vaguely seem to take notice of the seminal significance of such an invaluable edifice. It is a microcosm of a broader failure: we possess world-class history, yet present it with third-rate imagination and a total disregard for the potential of such a treasure.

Sri Lanka is an island encircled by temperate, scenic, and historical waters. Yet, our national transport network remains heavily reliant on congested roads and ageing rail tracks. The sea, our most abundant natural highway, is almost entirely ignored for domestic passenger travel.

Imagine a premium, high-speed, and extremely safe ferry service circumnavigating the island, connecting key coastal nodes such as Colombo, Galle, Hambantota, Arugam Bay, Batticaloa, Trincomalee, Jaffna, Mannar, and Puttlam. Such a Sri Lankan Coastal Ferry Circuit would do far more than ease traffic; it could redefine experiential travel:

· All-Inclusive Cultural Hospitality: Tickets could include authentic indigenous meals and all forms of local beverages. A main meal could include rice, a variety of local vegetable curries like ‘Kiri Kos Maaluwa’ and ‘Wattakka curry’, ‘fresh vegetable sambals’, prawn and crab preparations, ‘Leafy Vegetable Mallun’, delicate fish preparations including fried fish, freshly brewed Ceylon tea, herbal infusions like ‘Kothalahimbutu’ and ‘Belimal’, indigenous sweetmeats, local tropical fruits and fruit-drinks, and even regional culinary specialities, freshly prepared onboard.

Loads of high-end tourists would not come to our country and crave medium-rare beef steaks which they can have in plenty in their own countries but would love to taste our indigenous fare. The culinary expertise of our people could be used effectively to provide the exact flavours that would titillate the sensitive and discerning palates of visitors from many different cultural regions of the planet.

· A New Perspective on the Pearl: Travellers could view the island’s coastal geography, historic lighthouses, and marine life from the ocean, turning transit time into a core highlight of the journey. Sightseeing breaks could be incorporated into the schedules to allow the tourists to visit certain areas of undeniable interest. Even night stays in hotels could be incorporated into programmes that would span even a few days.

Built and operated to rigorous international maritime standards, these ferries would offer a serene, reliable, and comfortable alternative to chaotic road transport.

In recent years, regions like Ella have been allowed to transform into overcrowded hubs geared toward low-budget, scantily dressed and noisy backpacker tourism. While all travellers are welcome, allowing ecologically sensitive mountain areas to be overrun by cheap bars and uncontrolled commercialisation dilutes the country’s appeal and yields minimal economic returns, relative to the environmental toll.

Sri Lanka’s cool mist-draped central highlands, ancient rainforests, and mountain ranges deserve a strategy centred on refinement, luxury, and ecological preservation. Exposure to the humble rural life of our people would be an added attraction for tourists from affluent countries. Exclusive heritage bungalow stays, curated tea-tasting masterclasses, and “pick-to-cup” artisanal experiences charging premium rates would be another attractive thing for them. By elevating our agricultural heritage, turning tea estates and ancient paddy systems into immersive, educational, and high-end retreats, we would be able to attract discerning global travellers who respect the local culture and contribute substantially to the local economy.

Trincomalee: The Goldmine of a Natural Harbour Left in the Lurch

To possess one of the finest and largest natural harbours in the world and leave it underutilised is an economic crime. Trincomalee’s deep-water bay offers strategic and commercial advantages that few ports on Earth can match.

Instead of letting Trincomalee sit largely idle or locked in geopolitical stalemates, it should be developed into a multifaceted economic motor:

· A Global Cruise and Yacht Hub: Trincomalee’s sheer depth and natural shelter make it an ideal homeport for luxury international cruise liners and private superyachts exploring the Indian Ocean.

Situated directly along major East-West shipping lanes, it can serve as a premier centre for ship repairs, eco-friendly vessel refuelling, and maritime logistics.

The surrounding waters are rich with marine life, including blue whales and sperm whales, alongside sunken naval history. Balanced development could pair maritime commerce with world-class, regulated marine research and eco-tourism.

A visitor’s impression of a country is forged largely by how they move through it. Internal travel in Sri Lanka should be elevated from a chaotic and miserable necessity to an art form.

· Heritage Railway Revival: Restoring classic train routes with luxury glass-roofed observation carriages, silver-service dining, and immaculate punctuality.

Connecting our inland lakes and coastal bays via a network of quiet, efficient floatplanes to drastically reduce travel times for high-value visitors.

Raising the standards of local tour guiding through rigorous national certification in history, ecology, and hospitality, ensuring every narrative told to a visitor reflects the true depth of our civilisation.

Sri Lanka does not lack natural resources. In fact, they are there in plenty. What the country lacks is the vision, discipline, and political stewardship required to manage them. We do not need to invent new attractions out of thin air. Our assets are already here: in the granite foundations of Mihintale, the deep sheltered waters of Trincomalee, the emerald green of our tea slopes, and the blue ocean surrounding our shores.

To reclaim our standing, we must abandon the mentality of cheap, totally aimless, uncoordinated exploitation. Those in authority must either step up to deliver properly or step aside, so that a new standard of excellence can take root. By honouring our past, protecting our natural environment, and executing modern ideas with uncompromising quality, Sri Lanka can finally shine with the brilliant, unblemished sheen it was always meant to possess and display. What we desperately need is an administration that is willing and able to take the entire situation by the scruff of its neck and do what needs to be done to take this land into unbridled prosperity. It cannot be done from within the air-conditioned palatial offices in Colombo, but by the untiring efforts of dedicated people who are more than willing to venture into the wilderness and work from basic ground realities.
