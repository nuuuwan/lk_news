NCE renews call for clear timeline on Sri Lanka’s accession to Madrid System

The National Chamber of Exporters of Sri Lanka (NCE) has warned that Sri Lanka’s accession to the World Intellectual Property Organisation’s (WIPO) Madrid System is still awaiting the completion of the required legislative amendments.

The National Intellectual Property Office of Sri Lanka (NIPO) has reviewed the amended Bill and communicated the required corrections to the Legal Draftsman’s Department, and is awaiting the final Bill before it can be submitted to the Attorney General’s Department for legal clearance. No confirmed timeline has yet been provided for the country’s accession to the system.

The NCE has brought the matter to the attention of NIPO and the Trade, Commerce, Food Security and Co-operative Development Minister multiple times, highlighting the need for clarity on the remaining steps and a practical timeframe for completion. The Chamber has also offered to provide private-sector input to support the process.

According to the latest update, the accession process has reached an advanced stage. The Intellectual Property (Amendment) Bill, incorporating the provisions required for Sri Lanka’s accession to the Madrid Protocol, had been forwarded to the Trade, Commerce, Food Security and Co-operative Development Ministry Secretary. Following a review of the amended draft, observations submitted by NIPO had been incorporated, subject to clarifications on two sections identified by the Legal Draftsman.

The required corrections were communicated to the Legal Draftsman’s Department, with a request for the final Bill. Once the final Bill is received, the Ministry will submit it to the Attorney General’s Department for legal clearance, after which it will proceed through the standard legislative process in Parliament. Despite the lack of a definitive date at this stage, the process is moving forward.

“However, the delay has practical implications for businesses looking to register and protect their brands in overseas markets. At present, Sri Lankan companies have to apply separately in each country where they require trademark protection. The cost can vary considerably depending on the destination and the nature of the application,” the NCE said.

Estimates gathered by the NCE from member companies indicate that registering a trademark in an individual overseas market can cost from a few hundred US dollars to several thousand US dollars. These estimates include official filing fees, professional charges and other associated expenses, with the final cost depending on the country and the brand.

The expense can increase when a business seeks protection in several markets or across different classes of goods and services. This can be especially difficult for small and medium-sized exporters that are building their brands overseas and have to account for the cost of registration in each market.

Sri Lanka’s accession to the Madrid System would provide a more straightforward route to international trademark registration. The system allows an eligible applicant to submit a single international application through the country’s own intellectual property office and request protection in any or all of the member countries. This replaces the need to submit separate applications to each participating country, while providing a centralised process for managing international registrations.

The reach of the system has also grown. WIPO’s Madrid System currently has 117 members covering 133 countries and representing more than 80% of global trade. In 2025, an estimated 64,150 international trademark applications were filed through the system, with Asia accounting for 25.6% of the total.

Once Sri Lanka joins, a business that is a national of, is domiciled in or has a business in the country, and has filed for or registered a trademark locally, would be able to use the system to request protection in the markets it selects. As new jurisdictions join, the geographical coverage of an existing international registration can also be expanded.

The system is especially significant to Sri Lankan businesses whose products and services are entering international markets under local brands. This includes exporters in sectors such as tea, apparel, spices, gems and jewellery, processed foods and IT services. As companies develop value-added products and build recognition in overseas markets, protecting their names and trademarks becomes an essential part of their export plans.

Without registration in a particular market, for instance, a business may face difficulties if another party registers the same or a similar brand there. Resolving such disputes can be costly and time-consuming, and may affect the local company’s ability to use its brand in that market. A more accessible international registration process would therefore be crucial to companies expanding into several export destinations.

Trademark protection is also part of the intellectual property environment that overseas investors consider when assessing a country for business and investment. Access to an established international registration system can provide greater clarity for foreign companies looking at partnerships, manufacturing operations, franchise arrangements and other commercial activities involving Sri Lankan businesses.

The NCE has been collecting cost information from member companies and consulting sectoral representatives to support its work on the issue. The Chamber considers Sri Lanka’s accession to the Madrid System relevant to the country’s larger export ambitions, especially as local businesses seek to build internationally recognised brands and increase the value of their products and services.

The outstanding legislative work is therefore a necessary step in making the system accessible to Sri Lankan businesses. The NCE has reiterated the need for a clear timeframe for its completion, so exporters can plan their trademark registration requirements with a better understanding of when the international system will be available through Sri Lanka.
