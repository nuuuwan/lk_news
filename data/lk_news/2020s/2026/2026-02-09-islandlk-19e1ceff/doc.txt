Forest farming offers sustainable path for Sri Lanka’s farmers and fragile ecosystems, expert says

Sri Lanka’s traditional spice-based farming systems could hold the key to restoring degraded landscapes, strengthening rural livelihoods and tapping into global carbon markets, a leading international ecologist said this week, warning that continued reliance on monoculture agriculture risks irreversible environmental damage.

Speaking at the Genesis Thought Leadership Series at the Dilmah Centre for Sustainable Future, Professor Friedhelm Goeltenboth, Honorary Professor of Tropical Ecology at the University of Hohenheim, Germany, said Sri Lanka is uniquely positioned to adopt “Rainforestation Farming” — a system that integrates agriculture with forest restoration.

“Sri Lanka already practices many elements of forest farming without calling it that,” Prof. Goeltenboth said. “The challenge is to recognise its value, strengthen it scientifically and stop pushing farmers towards uniform monocultures.”

Rainforestation Farming, developed in the Philippines through a collaboration between the University of Hohenheim and Visayas State University, mimics natural rainforest structures by combining multiple layers of trees and crops.

Shade-tolerant spices such as cinnamon, cloves, cardamom, ginger and lemongrass are grown alongside fruit and timber species, creating productive systems that protect soil, water and biodiversity.

Prof. Goeltenboth said the model aligns closely with Sri Lanka’s spice-growing regions, particularly in the wet and intermediate zones, where smallholders have long cultivated mixed gardens under tree cover.

Instead of forcing farmers to abandon existing livelihoods, the system encourages gradual transition. Farmers begin with what they know best — whether spices, fruit or timber — and slowly integrate additional species over several years.

“You don’t shut down one economy and replace it overnight,” he said. “You phase out the old system and phase in the new, corner by corner.”

He warned that in monoculture expansion, once forests are cleared, it leaves little room for recovery.

“You will never get the rainforest back once it is gone,” he said. “You can only come close — but never fully restore what was lost.”

Prof. Goeltenboth also highlighted the economic potential of fibre-producing plants, which he said are largely ignored in Sri Lanka’s land-use planning.

Natural fibres, he noted, are increasingly sought after by global industries seeking sustainable alternatives to synthetic materials.

Beyond farming, forest-based systems also open opportunities for ecotourism, as visitors are drawn to biodiverse, living landscapes rather than plantations, he said.

A major opportunity for Sri Lanka lies in the voluntary carbon market, where companies offset emissions by supporting forest conservation and restoration.

Prof. Goeltenboth explained that community-managed forests and agroforestry lands can be certified for carbon storage, providing farmers with an additional income stream.

He said Sri Lankan institutions and conservation organisations already have the capacity to support such certification, with farmers themselves able to collect much of the necessary data, including tree height and diameter, under expert guidance.

“Once forests have a measurable economic value, communities are far more willing — and able — to protect them,” he said.

Citing international examples, Prof. Goeltenboth said carbon certification had helped entire villages in Southeast Asia resist pressure to sell ancestral forests to logging and plantation interests — a challenge familiar to Sri Lanka as well.

He also warned of the consequences of environmental mismanagement, pointing to regions where excessive chemical use has wiped out pollinators, forcing people to hand-pollinate crops.

“A forest is not a mine,” he said. “It is a living system. If we treat it as something to be exhausted, we will pay the price.”

Prof. Goeltenboth said Sri Lanka must focus on site-specific land-use planning, taking into account elevation, watersheds and existing ecosystems.

He cautioned against promoting reforestation indiscriminately, particularly in traditional rice-growing areas, where protecting waterways and riverbanks may be more effective.

“Watershed management is critical,” he said. “If upstream communities do not care about downstream impacts, conflict is inevitable.”

The lecture concluded with a call for Sri Lanka to move from policy discussions to action, combining traditional knowledge with science-backed systems that benefit both people and nature.

“Sustainability is no longer optional,” Prof. Goeltenboth said. “If we fail to protect soil, forests and biodiversity today, we are stealing from the next generation.”
