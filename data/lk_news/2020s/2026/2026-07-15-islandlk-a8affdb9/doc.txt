Crime, poverty and education

John Grisham’s novel, The Street Lawyer, gives a vivid picture of poverty in the US and its associated pathos, homelessness, hunger, drugs, prostitution, death due to freezing, and the stark contrast with the superrich and the callousness of the officials and the cruelty of the police. What it does not say is the reason for this incongruity, the failure of the system that produced so much riches and how it could also produce so much wretchedness and penury. A family of five, consisting of a young mother driven to alcohol, drugs and prostitution and four children including an infant, who live in a small car, are frozen to death in the cold of the night. They have been evicted from their home by a construction company which has bought the land to build high rise compartments. In the morgue they are counting the bodies of people who had similarly perished in the night, they too have been similarly evicted.

It is not just fiction. Officially there were more than 770,000 homeless people in the US in 2024 which represents an 18% increase compared to 2023. Paradoxically the per capita income in the US is about USD 360,000 per annum and there are about 1,000 billionaires; this number also represents an increase from previous years. This shows that the rich are getting richer while the poor are getting poorer in the richest country and this inequality has not been addressed for 250 years. According to neo-liberal economic theory poverty is a personal issue with no one to blame. If a person commits a crime for survival, e. g. theft, he is to be blamed.

If the situation is so bad in the richest country what could be said about poor Sri Lanka. The media, both print and electronic, paint a grim and gruesome picture of the crime situation in Sri Lanka. Recent riots in the Negombo prison is just one such incident. Available statistics show a crime rate of 3.3 per 100,000 in 2019 which is an increase by 43% compared to 2018. Poverty statistics are equally bad. We know that the poverty rate that was 14% in 2019 jumped to 24.5% in 2022 and has not improved by any significant degree in 2026. Similarly, the education system is facing seemingly insurmountable obstacles.

Though inflation is under control, the GDP is improving and  macro-economics are stable, the real incomes, particularly of the fixed salary earners and daily wage earners in relation to cost of living have dwindled. The lower middle class is teetering on the brink of poverty. Many have fallen. Middle class has shrunk significantly. Though unemployment has improved, the benefits of employment are meagre and insufficient to meet the basic necessities. Even the employed may be forced to look for sources of additional income. When options are limited, they may be tempted to try their hand in crime accessible to them, like bribery, cybercrime,  prostitution, and even narcotics which are pervasive and readily available. There are rumours that university students are involved in drug trafficking and even prostitution.

Organised crime could thrive when there is a certain amount of poverty. The mastermind may be hugely rich but he/she needs the doer who commits the crime like Kehelbaddara Padme’s hired killer, the Wele Suda’s drug trafficker, the Angoda Lokka’s collector of extortion money, the computer operator who could hack, the army deserter who could wield a gun, the law enforcer who could bend the law, etc. These operators may be poor or in need of extra money to pay for their daily needs. They are often married with children, and circumstances have forced them to take to crime.

Research in Sri Lanka shows that there is a strong correlation between crime, literacy and poverty. It has been reported that the frequency of those sentenced to death is higher in the lower income categories and rate of conviction is high among those with low literacy and no schooling (Ekanayake, 2018). Individuals forced into crime in Sri Lanka largely emerge from deep-seated socio-economic vulnerabilities, primarily extreme poverty, low educational attainment, and youth unemployment. Marginalised communities and individuals lured by transnational job scams are frequently coerced into illicit activities due to desperate financial need and a lack of viable economic alternatives. The social and economic drivers of this phenomenon manifest in several distinct areas.

A large share of individuals committing economically driven crimes (such as theft or drug-related offenses) originate from low-income communities, such as informal settlements in Colombo’s suburbs. Economic hardships often intersect with substance abuse, driving vulnerable populations into criminal networks. Research on Sri Lankan prison inmates (such as narratives from the Angunakolapelessa Prison) highlights that offenders largely come from conditions of extreme deprivation, with precarious, low-paying daily-wage work and minimal educational attainment

High youth unemployment rates and lack of formal education severely limit legitimate career avenues, creating a vacuum where criminal syndicates can exploit desperation and recruit vulnerable youth.

Structural inequalities and historical discrimination leave specific demographics, such as the community in the plantation sector, disproportionately vulnerable to exploitation and informal economies.

Economic distress has led to an increase in Sri Lankans falling victim to human trafficking. Syndicates frequently lure young, educated individuals with promises of high-paying IT or customer service jobs in countries like Thailand. Once isolated abroad, victims are often forced into modern slavery and coerced into participating in international cyber-scamming operations. Post-pandemic inflation and widespread national economic challenges have driven many into illegal activities, including human smuggling and survival crimes such as petty theft or burglary.

The recent riots in the Negombo prison was a vivid depiction of the crime situation in Sri Lanka and its wide ramifications. Criminals stationed abroad could manage the crime syndicate in Sri Lanka which seem to extend into the prisons. Prisons which are meant to be centres of reform have been transformed into centres of crime. In this regard collusion with prison authorities cannot be ruled out, not to mention corrupt politicians. Another sad aspect of this state of affairs is the fact that while the school dropout problem persists, parallely there is an increase in admissions to prisons. Prisons are holding three times their capacity while there are schools with more teachers than students.

It is seen that there is a strong correlation between crime, poverty and literacy. There is also a strong association between poverty and school dropout rates. Add to this the fact that increasing wealth of the rich and decreasing wealth of the poor are concurrent realities, then the picture is complete. Obviously this is an interconnected conglomerate of problems. It has to be addressed as such. It cannot be solved piecemeal. If one starts with the problem of crime, one sees that it cannot be solved without solving the problem of poverty and education, problems in education cannot be solved without solving the economic problems. Could the economic problems be solved without dismantling the neo-liberal global economic system that perpetuates poverty and wealth accumulation by the rich?

If we continue to adhere to the neo-liberal system we would have to endorse its position that crime is a personal issue and the system is not responsible for it, which is absolutely wrong as research shows. Inequality statistics for 2025 show the gravity of the economic problem in Sri Lanka; while the richest 20% of the population claims over 50% of the total household income, the poorest 10% receive only about 1.6% to 2.9% and the gap is widening by the year. We have a left oriented government headed by a party committed to socialism but it seems to have defaulted on its foundational tenets and embraced neo-liberalism lock stock and barrel and seems to be abiding by the IMF dictates to the letter. If we continue in this vein more would become poor, more would drop out of schools, more would turn to crime, and more schools will close while more prisons would open.
