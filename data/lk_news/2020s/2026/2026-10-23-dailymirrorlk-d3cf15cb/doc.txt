A missed opportunity to strengthen ties with Thailand

The visit of Thai Deputy Prime Minister Suphajee Suthumpun to Sri Lanka for the religious ceremony at Mihintale has raised a question that deserves consideration beyond the debate over diplomatic protocol. While the Foreign Ministry has clarified that she visited Sri Lanka in her private capacity to participate in the enshrinement ceremony, the occasion also presented an opportunity to further strengthen the longstanding relationship between the two countries. Could Sri Lanka have made greater use of her presence, even without changing the private nature of her visit?

There is, of course, a distinction between an official state visit and a private visit by a foreign dignitary. The Foreign Ministry has said that the Thai Embassy requested access to the VIP lounge and other courtesies, which were provided by the Sri Lankan authorities. It has also noted that no other officials accompanied her. These clarifications explain the arrangements made for her arrival. However, the question remains whether the government could have taken the initiative to meet her, extend its courtesies and engage her in an informal exchange of views on matters of mutual interest.

Diplomacy is not confined to formal meetings, signed agreements and carefully arranged state visits. It also involves recognising opportunities to build relationships and establish personal rapport. A private visit does not necessarily prevent an informal meeting, provided the visitor is comfortable with such an engagement and the circumstances permit it. Such a meeting need not have been presented as an official engagement or disrupted the religious purpose of her visit.

This is particularly relevant in the case of Thailand, a country with which Sri Lanka shares centuries-old Buddhist and cultural connections. These links constitute an important foundation for bilateral relations, but they need not remain confined to religion and heritage. They can also provide a basis for expanding cooperation in trade and other areas.

Suphajee Suthumpun holds the portfolios of Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Commerce in Thailand. Her presence in Sri Lanka therefore offered a possible opportunity to explore economic cooperation alongside the  cultural relationship. Even a brief exchange, without any expectation of immediate agreements or commitments, could have helped establish  identify areas for future engagement.

Sri Lanka, grappling with the challenge of revitalising its economy, needs to make the most of its relationships with countries across Asia. Thailand has experience in tourism, manufacturing, commercial development and regional economic integration. There may be scope for both countries to explore ways of increasing trade, attracting investment and promoting tourism in both directions.

This is not to suggest that the government was obliged to convert a private religious visit into an official programme. Nor should the absence of a formal reception, by itself, be taken as evidence of a deterioration in bilateral relations. The point is a more measured one: Could the occasion have been used more effectively to nurture a relationship that has considerable potential?

The government could have considered a suitable opportunity to meet the visiting Deputy Prime Minister, extend its courtesies and exchange views, while fully respecting the private nature of her visit. Even if a meeting was not possible, the occasion could have been recognised through appropriate diplomatic channels as an expression of goodwill towards a longstanding friend.

The Foreign Ministry’s clarification should therefore be seen as explaining the arrangements rather than necessarily ending the discussion about the diplomatic opportunities available. Protocol is important, but it should not become the only consideration in assessing how countries can build stronger relationships.

Sri Lanka and Thailand have much to gain from maintaining close ties. The shared Buddhist heritage provides a natural bridge, while economic cooperation offers scope to take the relationship further. A visit centred on a religious ceremony could have remained entirely private while still creating an opportunity for goodwill and dialogue.

Ultimately, diplomacy is not merely about adhering to protocol. It is also about recognising opportunities when they arise and making thoughtful use of them. In that sense, the Thai Deputy Prime Minister’s visit was an occasion worth considering not only for what it was, but also for what it might have offered Sri Lanka in strengthening a valued bilateral relationship.
