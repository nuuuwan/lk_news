How India escaped the IMF cycle - the Manmohan Singh lesson

Sri Lanka should take a leaf from India’s Dr Manmohan Singh era. When India faced a near-existential balance-of-payments crisis in 1991, it turned to the IMF but Dr Manmohan Singh did not allow the IMF programme to become the country’s economic strategy. The IMF helped India survive the crisis , India’s own reforms built the recovery.

India did not escape the IMF by rejecting the IMF. It escaped by using the IMF as a bridge, undertaking difficult reforms, rebuilding its foreign-exchange earning capacity and eventually making further IMF borrowing unnecessary.

That distinction is crucial for any country trying to emerge from a balance-of-payments crisis.

There is also an important historical correction. India’s decisive economic transformation began not when Dr. Manmohan Singh was Prime Minister from 2004 to 2014, but when he served as Finance Minister under Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao from 1991 to 1996.

In 1991, India was facing one of the gravest economic crises in its post-independence history. Foreign-exchange reserves had fallen dangerously low, the Gulf War had pushed up oil costs and disrupted remittances, fiscal imbalances were severe and confidence was deteriorating.

India turned to the IMF for emergency support. The depth of the crisis is perhaps best captured by what happened to India’s gold. With foreign-exchange reserves barely sufficient to cover a few weeks of imports, India was forced to use part of its gold reserves to raise desperately needed foreign currency. In 1991, 20 tonnes of government gold were sold abroad with a repurchase option, while the Reserve Bank of India moved another 47 tonnes to the Bank of England to raise emergency financing. For a country with such a deep cultural and economic attachment to gold, it was a powerful symbol of how close India had come to the edge.

Yet that moment of vulnerability became a turning point. India went from shipping gold abroad to survive a foreign-exchange crisis to eventually building one of the world’s largest foreign-exchange reserve positions.

The transformation did not happen because India rejected the IMF. It happened because India used the crisis and the breathing space provided by external support to reform its economy. IMF records show that India entered a Stand-By Arrangement in January 1991 and another, much larger arrangement in October 1991. The latter provided SDR 1.656 billion and expired in June 1993.

But what happened next is the real story. India Used the Breathing Space to Reform.

The Rao–Singh government did not regard IMF financing as an economic growth model. It used external assistance to buy time while restructuring the domestic economy. Manmohan Singh’s 1991 reforms attacked structural weaknesses that had accumulated over decades. Industrial licensing was substantially dismantled. Import restrictions began to be reduced. Indian companies were progressively exposed to international competition. Foreign direct investment was liberalized. Export promotion received greater emphasis. Public-sector policy began shifting toward efficiency rather than simply state ownership.

Finance Minister Manmohan Singh argued in his 1991 Budget that India needed greater domestic competition to improve productivity, efficiency and costs, and that foreign investment could bring capital, technology and access to international markets.

That principle is crucial. India stopped asking only, “How much money can the IMF lend us?” and increasingly asked, “How can India earn the foreign exchange that will make IMF lending unnecessary?”

This is the difference between managing a crisis and restructuring an economy.

The Rupee Adjustment Was Painful — But Competitiveness Mattered. India also confronted the exchange rate. In July 1991, the rupee was adjusted downward in two steps by roughly 18–19 percent. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) said the objective included restoring export competitiveness. India subsequently moved away from a government-controlled exchange rate, first introducing a dual exchange-rate system and eventually allowing the rupee’s value to be determined largely by market forces.

This was not painless. But policymakers recognized that defending an unrealistic exchange rate while reserves disappeared would merely postpone the adjustment. The strategy increasingly connected the exchange rate with exports, investment, competitiveness and external sustainability.

Then came the decisive change: dollars started returning. By the time India’s IMF Stand-By Arrangement ended in mid-1993, economic recovery and private capital inflows had strengthened sufficiently that India no longer needed IMF borrowing. Foreign-exchange reserves rose from about US$10.1 billion in 1993 to US$113 billion by 2004, while India’s use of IMF credit eventually fell to zero.

By 2003–2004, the transformation had gone even further. India was participating in lending resources to the IMF rather than depending on it for crisis financing. From emergency borrower to a country capable of supplying resources to the international financial system—that was the deeper transformation of India.

The reforms were implemented by a government led by Narasimha Rao, with Manmohan Singh playing the central economic-policy role, and were subsequently developed by successive governments.

But the direction established in 1991 was clear away from excessive licensing, protection and state control, and toward competition, private enterprise, foreign investment and deeper integration with the global economy.

Critically, India did not confuse stabilization with recovery. Stabilization stopped the bleeding. Structural reform changed the economic machinery. Productive investment, exports, services, remittances and capital inflows progressively strengthened India’s external position.

An IMF programme can restore confidence, provide foreign-exchange liquidity, impose fiscal discipline and create breathing space. But the IMF cannot permanently generate a country’s foreign exchange. A country ultimately has to earn that foreign exchange itself. It must export more goods and services, attract productive FDI, improve productivity, build competitive industries, encourage entrepreneurship and create an environment in which domestic and foreign capital wants to invest.

This is where the Manmohan Singh lesson becomes particularly relevant to Sri Lanka today.

The latest U.S. Department of State Investment Climate assessment acknowledges Sri Lanka’s economic stabilization and improved political stability, but delivers an important warning the country’s investment climate remains challenging sch as Project reversals ,Regulatory shifts, Slow Decision making, Inadequate support for established businesses etc etc; IMF also stress the need for comprehensive structural reforms.

It is reported that the Government has also halted the planned privatization of many state-owned enterprises, opting instead for turnaround reforms. At the same time, while seeking to attract foreign investment, some senior officials have publicly advocated a larger role for SOEs and greater state participation in economic activity.

For investors, these signals matter, institutional support continue to undermine investor confidence.

Sri Lanka does not need to reinvent the wheel. I have personally witnessed how One-Stop Investment Centers operate in other countries ie; in Delhi, Tamil Nadu, Bangalore and in several African countries. Investors can approach a single investment authority and obtain the approvals and services required from multiple government institutions under one roof. Representatives of the relevant agencies are brought together at the investment authority instead of forcing investors to run from ministry to ministry and agency to agency chasing approvals. Sri Lanka must urgently adopt the same approach. If the BOI is serious about attracting both foreign and local investment, it should establish a fully empowered One-Stop Centre with representatives of all key approving agencies physically or digitally integrated into a single decision-making platform. An investor should knock on one government door—not ten. Every additional desk, signature and agency an investor is forced to chase is another reason for capital to choose another country

This goes directly to the heart of Sri Lanka’s post-IMF challenge. India used its crisis to transform the economy that had produced the crisis. Sri Lanka must now do the same.

The country must make the fundamental transition from IMF-supported stabilization to investment-led, export-driven growth.

Crisis - IMF programme - fiscal adjustment - temporary stability - weak investment and exports - renewed external financing pressure - more borrowing.

India broke that cycle. Sri Lanka must now prove that it can do the same. Otherwise, when IMF financing ends and external debt repayments intensify, Sri Lanka could once again find itself searching for the next source of external financing instead of generating enough foreign exchange to stand on its own feet.

That would mean the IMF programme succeeded in stabilizing the crisis but failed to break the cycle.

Manmohan Singh understood something fundamental in 1991, the objective of an IMF programme should eventually be to make another IMF programme unnecessary.

India used the breathing space to reform, rebuild reserves, restore credibility, attract capital and open its economy. When the 1991–93 Stand-By Arrangement ended, it did not enter another borrowing programme.

This India then gradually moved away from a government-controlled exchange rate, first introducing a dual exchange-rate system and eventually allowing the rupee’s value to be determined largely by market forces. is the real Manmohan Singh lesson for Sri Lanka.

The strongest exit from the IMF is not political rhetoric against the Fund. It is building an economy strong enough that you no longer need to borrow from it. The real test of an IMF programme is not whether a country can complete it, but whether it has built an economy strong enough not to need another one.”
