Sri Lanka apparel exporters locked out of US tariff refund windfall

Gap, Abercrombie & Fitch, PVH and Nike have booked well over US$ 1bn in US tariff refunds in recent quarters; the Sri Lankan factories supplying all four say none of it reached them

US buyer commitment windows have shrunk from six to eight months down to just three, leaving manufacturers unable to plan capacity or lock in raw material purchases

JAAF says refunds go to the brand as importer of record, not the Sri Lankan exporter. Manufacturers on FCA contracts have no legal claim to the money

Sri Lanka’s apparel manufacturing sector is grappling with sharply compressed order horizons from American buyers alongside virtually zero financial relief from tariff refunds, even as the same US court ruling that unlocked those refunds has delivered a windfall to the American brands they supply, according to the Joint Apparel Association Forum (JAAF).

Addressing a recent edition of First Capital Stock Talk, JAAF Secretary General Yohan Lawrence said international buyers in the United States, which accounts for approximately 40 percent of the island’s apparel exports, have significantly altered their procurement strategies. Buyers are now trying to commit to as little as possible, reducing typical order horizons from six to eight months down to just three, a shift Lawrence attributed to prolonged volatility in US tariff classifications and buyers’ desire to preserve maximum flexibility over what they purchase.

“What that means is they will, over a period of time, probably buy the same amount of apparel, but the commitment will be much less… What may have previously been a six or eight-month commitment might now be a three-month commitment,” Lawrence said.

He noted that shorter buyer commitments prevent local garment manufacturers and fabric mills, including listed textile millers such as Teejay Lanka PLC and Hayleys Fabric PLC, from securing raw materials ahead of time or planning long-term production schedules. Apparel manufacturers operate on an open costing framework: a cost-per-minute basis of six to eight cents per standard minute, set largely by the brand rather than negotiated by the factory. That leaves virtually no scope to build in buffers or capitalise on bulk purchasing. Lawrence said manufacturers are increasingly compelled to sacrifice margin simply to keep factories running at full capacity, since capacity utilisation is what drives unit cost down.

Compounding the problem, local exporters have seen no financial recovery from the tariffs previously levied under contested US trade policy. The initial US reciprocal tariff, imposed around April 2025 at a steep 44 percent before being negotiated down to 20 percent, was ultimately ruled unlawful by the US Supreme Court. That triggered domestic expectations of refunds flowing back into the export sector, but Lawrence was pessimistic about local manufacturers seeing any of it, pointing to the mechanics of global shipping contracts.

“The majority of apparel that is sold out of Sri Lanka is sold on an FCA basis. So the tariff is paid by the importer on record, which would be the brand,” Lawrence explained. Because refunds go to the importer of record rather than the exporter, he said, “at the moment, we haven’t seen refunds coming back in our direction. I think this is the way of the world.”

Asked directly whether the local industry should expect no refunds going forward, he was blunter still: “No, I don’t think we’ve seen anything of any [refunds], despite the fact that when the tariffs came on there was a conversation that said, ‘Hey guys, we have to pay an extra 10 percent, we’ll pay five, we need you to cover the five.’ The flow back of that has been, shall we say, very slow.”

That contrast is sharpened by how directly Sri Lankan factories sit inside these brands’ supply chains. MAS Holdings, one of Nike’s largest and longest-standing apparel suppliers, also manufactures for Victoria’s Secret, Speedo, Lululemon and PVH Corp’s Calvin Klein and Tommy Hilfiger lines; Brandix supplies Gap and Marks & Spencer; and Hirdaramani supplies Levi’s, Tommy Hilfiger and Calvin Klein. None of the relief flowing through those brands’ own books appears to have reached the Sri Lankan side of the relationship.

Gap Inc. reported receiving US$ 95mn in IEEPA tariff refunds in the quarter ended August 1, 2026, a sum the company said added 11.4 percentage points to gross margin for the period. Abercrombie & Fitch booked a similar benefit in the same quarter (roughly US$ 100mn, worth about US$ 1.75 a share and nearly 790 basis points of operating margin), with a further US$ 20mn expected in the third quarter as part of a total US$120mn claim.

PVH Corp, the owner of Calvin Klein and Tommy Hilfiger, received US$ 107mn in refunds in the same quarter, adding roughly 510 basis points to operating margin and about US$ 1.80 a share. Nike’s own disclosure dwarfs all three: the company recognised a US$986mn benefit from IEEPA tariff recoveries in its fiscal fourth quarter and has since received substantially all of it, according to its latest SEC filings. Between them, these four brands alone have recovered well over a billion dollars. By Lawrence’s account, that money has gone straight into US brands’ reported margins and per-share earnings rather than back down the supply chain to the manufacturers who originally absorbed the cost.

Providing context to the tariff turmoil, Lawrence detailed the diplomatic engagements that followed the Supreme Court ruling. Washington instituted a 150-day temporary 10 percent tariff under Section 122, followed by Section 301 investigations into forced labour prevention measures. Following sustained negotiations between Sri Lankan officials and the Office of the US Trade Representative, the country secured reclassification into the lower 10 percent tier.

While the 10 percent reciprocal rate restored parity with regional competitors such as Bangladesh, Cambodia and Pakistan, Lawrence cautioned that the relief remains provisional until Sri Lanka formalises a comprehensive bilateral trade framework with the United States. In the meantime, he said domestic reforms in commercial energy pricing, power wheeling regulations and trade facilitation remain vital if Sri Lanka is to defend its export competitiveness in an increasingly fragmented global trade regime.
