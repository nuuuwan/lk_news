Sri Lanka’s farm policy carries ‘anti-export bias’

Sri Lanka’s agricultural sector is being held back by a policy framework that rewards protection more than exports, the World Bank said, calling for a shift from shielding production towards attracting investment and competing for higher-value global markets. In a strong assessment of the country’s agribusiness potential, the Bank said Sri Lanka had demonstrated that it could succeed internationally in tea, coconut, cinnamon, seafood and rubber-based manufacturing, but described these successes as “islands of excellence” rather than the norm.

“Anti-export bias in agricultural policy has favored import-competing crops over export-oriented sectors through a combination of tariffs, para-tariffs, export taxes, licensing requirements, and discretionary policy interventions,” the World Bank said. While the new National Tariff Policy was an important step towards a more open and predictable trade regime, it said deeper reforms were needed to strengthen investment and export incentives.

Primary agriculture accounts for about 8 percent of GDP and roughly a quarter of employment, but the wider agrifood economy, encompassing processing, inputs, logistics, trade and food services, contributes an estimated one-sixth of GDP and more than 40 percent of employment. Agribusiness also generates nearly 30 percent of merchandise export earnings.

The World Bank argued that the bigger opportunity is not necessarily to grow more, but to earn more from what Sri Lanka already grows.

Food and beverage processing generates more than five times the value added per worker of primary agriculture. Every dollar of demand for processed food produces an estimated US$ 1.85 of economy-wide activity, compared with US$ 1.20 from primary agriculture.

Yet much of Sri Lanka’s agricultural production continues to leave the country raw or only minimally processed, limiting the value retained domestically.

Trade protection also comes at a cost. In 2025, para-tariffs accounted for almost half of the average total import duty of 18.5 percent and roughly doubled the duty burden on intermediate goods. Agriculture and fishing, and food and beverages, faced the highest average import duties at 31.4 percent.

World Bank modelling suggests that fully eliminating the para-tariffs could increase Sri Lankan exports by as much as 11 percent and real GDP by about 4.7 percent in the long run, although it cautioned that the adjustment would not be painless. Import-competing agricultural producers would bear much of the cost, with output projected to decline in areas including dairy, vegetable oils, processed rice and paddy.

Among its proposals are replacing export CESS levies on tea and coconut with less distortionary revenue sources, or recycling the proceeds into productivity and replanting; making tariff changes more transparent; improving traceability and certification; investing in cold-chain and trade logistics; and easing land and financing constraints.

“Unlocking agribusiness growth will require a shift from protecting production toward enabling investment, productivity, and export competitiveness,” the World Bank said.
