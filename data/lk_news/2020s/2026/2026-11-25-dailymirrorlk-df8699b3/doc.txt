Orange the World Dutch ambassador urges  men  to join  fight on violence against women

Ambassador of the Netherlands Wiebe de Boer responds to questions from the Daily Mirror on ‘Orange the World’ programme, a Dutch initiative to create global awareness on gender-based violence

The Netherlands urges stronger law enforcement and greater public awareness to tackle gender-based violence, including online harassment and AI-generated abuse

Calls for men to challenge harmful gender norms, while highlighting the need for better protection and support for survivors

Q In what ways has the annual Orange the World campaign produced tangible results?

Orange the World is a 16- day long world-wide campaign to prevent Gender Based Violence. Every year it starts on November 25, the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women, and runs until 10 December, the International Human Rights Day.

The objective of Orange the World is to raise awareness, foster support and build networks to prevent women and girls being subjected to harassment and violence. I think Orange the World campaign has contributed a lot to making Gender-Based Violence a global movement that is instrumental in pushing policy and legal changes.

Its tangible results are mostly indirect - it creates political pressure, funding, and space for local groups to act. Also, freedom to address the problem without stigma is crucial.

All this has produced, around the world, better laws to protect women and better implementation of these laws. Is it enough? Certainly not so. Therefore, we need everybody to participate in this campaign. Especially the role of men is crucial.

Q What are the biggest weaknesses in Sri Lanka’s response to gender-based violence?

GBV is not specific to a country or a region. This is a worldwide problem, not specific to Sri Lanka. One in three women have experienced violence in their lifetime. Every 10 minutes, a femicide is taking place, a woman being killed because she is a woman. If you add up sexual harassment, this situation is even more serious. That’s the context. Based on that, if I respond to your original question, Sri Lanka has some progressive laws, but like in most countries around the world, a culture of shame, prejudice and stereotypes poses challenges to address GBV effectively. Law enforcement and the judicial system are, like in the Netherlands, under-resourced and overwhelmed with other cases. Even if support is available, it is mostly concentrated in urban areas and not available in estate and rural communities.

Q Does Sri Lanka have adequate laws to protect women, or is the real problem weak enforcement?

Like in most countries, including mine, laws and enforcement are both not optimal. As I mentioned, Sri Lanka has some good laws, such as the Prevention of Domestic Violence Act, but implementation is hard for victims and the police or other agencies involved in countering Gender-Based Violence. The National Commission on Women, which was established recently under the Women Empowerment Act, also has powers to monitor Gender-Based Violence.

While some of these have limitations in comprehensively addressing GBV, some other laws, such as personal laws in Sri Lanka, also sometimes pose challenges in countering GBV. There is a tendency among law enforcement officers to still consider domestic violence as family issues rather than a crime. It is easy to look the other way. Police, like everywhere, face capacity challenges.

Implementation of laws and safeguards is always difficult. That is why it is so important to work on the prevention of GBV by changing mindsets in society.

QWhat lessons can the Netherlands offer to Sri Lanka for the prevention of violence against women?

I am not sure whether the Netherlands is a perfect model in this case, as femicide takes place in my country every eight days. And 41% of women over the age of 15 have experienced physical or sexual violence. This is above the EU average of 31%.

So, I don’t think we have yet found the magic bullet. Nevertheless, what might be of inspiration is the recent change where the Netherlands state, not the survivor, is responsible for doing the heavy lifting. We provide immediate protection, one referral point, specialist care, and prevention that starts at the school level. The Dutch principle is that safety is a public responsibility, and not a private family matter. In our new law, rape is sex without explicit consent, so physical force is not the only incriminating factor. This is strengthening the position of victims.

Instead of victims going to separate and different places seeking help and justice, the system “Safe at Home” provides one national contact number available around the clock to help victims and those who are at risk, with regional centres offering assistance. Anyone- victim, neighbour, teacher, or doctor- can call and “Safe at Home” does risk assessment, safety planning, and referral.

Mayors of cities in the Netherlands have power and can also issue restraining orders temporarily removing the perpetrator from their home if there is an imminent danger to the victim. Sri Lankan law requires a woman to go to court to get a protection Order, which takes time, money, and courage. The Netherlands also has a five-step code for professionals to follow regarding domestic violence and child abuse, making violence not seen as a matter. It works, but it is not enough.

The Netherlands is a strong advocate for international collaboration on eliminating violence against women and girls. Combatting violence against women is a priority in the Dutch foreign policy and development cooperation. Through diplomacy and collaboration with civil society organisations, the Netherlands contributes to the prevention of violence against all women and girls and supports survivors of violence.

In the current digital era, online harassment of women, particularly young girls, has become a serious issue. That is another alarming issue. GBV has encroached into cyberspace as well. Technology-Facilitated Gender-Based Violence is its latest form. All around the world, law enforcement agencies are facing challenges in responding to TFGBV, such as online harassment, non-consensual image sharing, AI-generated nude pictures, stalking, and hate speech, due to resource and capacity issues. Lack of awareness and digital literacy are also contributing. On the other hand, social media platforms are lax in taking responsibility for moderating hate content, particularly in vernacular languages. Their reporting mechanisms are lengthy and complicated.

This has added more pressure to states in countering GBV in digital platforms. The EU wants to target the platforms to take more responsibility.

Q How should governments tackle the growing problem of online harassment and digital violence against women?

Countering TFGBV requires more collective efforts rather than pressing only the authorities to act. I am not saying it’s less value; indeed it’s the key, yet other stakeholders also need to get together in tackling online harassments against women and girls. This is one of the areas our embassy has focused on more recently. We provided a comprehensive training, possibly for the first time in Sri Lanka to women journalists.

The training was ‘Enhancing understanding and knowledge of AI among women in the media’, where women journalists were empowered with necessary knowledge and skills to counter weaponising AI against women and to get the best out of AI for their daily work. We are also implementing a project countering hate speech and empowering women to counter GBV in the North. Similarly, I think digital literacy and AI literacy needs to be widened, necessary laws needs to be updated and more importantly a robust implementation of rules and regulations are keys to counter TFGBV.

Q As a male advocating women’s rights, how do you see men play their role in challenging patriarchal attitudes and preventing violence against women?

That’s exactly what we want to talk about and push. Preventing Gender based Violence cannot be done merely by educating women and girls and developing their capacity to respond. It is crucial to get the support of men to address the issue and make it their responsibility to prevent GBV. We must address society as a whole and change the attitude, cultural barriers, bringing perpetrators to book and ensure our mothers, sisters and daughters can live in a world free of violence and harassment. The Dutch theme this year of the Orange the World Campaign is: “Everyone takes action: with men against violence.” Through its Pledge, it invites men to turn this into action by publicly committing to challenge harmful gender norms, speak up against violence against women and girls, and support women and girls in their own environment.

Q Is the Netherlands prepared to expand its support for Sri Lankan organisations working to protect women’s rights?

One of our three priorities in Sri Lanka is human rights. Under Dutch Foreign policy, human rights is a priority for us. In Sri Lanka, we have three human rights priorities: equality for women and girls, equal rights for LGBTIQ+ community and freedom of expression. We have been doing several projects in different parts of the country to ensure gender equality and women’s empowerment. We currently have ongoing projects specifically addressing GBV in specific districts as well as more generally across the country.

We recently concluded some projects addressing female genital mutilation, and are continuing programmes on legal and counselling support to victims of GBV, providing digital skills, vocational skill support to women in tourism, and political empowerment of women. We also work with the National Commission on Women. We look at supporting making more structural changes to make a real change. One example is the project we are currently implementing with the Family Planning Association of Sri Lanka to Integrate Gender Based Violence (GBV) Screening in Antenatal Care in Sri Lanka.

Q What concrete action would you like to see from the Sri Lankan Government to demonstrate its commitment to ending violence against women?

Like I said, we are in this all together. Sri Lanka is active in preventing violence against women. Let’s learn from each other’s experiences and best practices. When compared with the regional neighbours, Sri Lanka recorded the lowest score in Intimate Partner Violence. However, there is still more to do to ensure a much safer Sri Lanka for women and girls. The vast majority of women face harassment on public transport. Let’s work all together on including men in the equation to prevent GBV.

Q What is the latest progress on the preservation of Dutch heritage sites in Sri Lanka?

Our engagements are on technical support and capacity building relating to the preservation and management of such heritage. Of these, digital approaches to documenting Dutch-built heritage are a focus. There is currently an ongoing project between the Central Cultural Fund of Sri Lanka, the University of Amsterdam and the City of Amsterdam in documenting VOC forts using remote sensing technology, a key objective of which is to capture 3D information for better management. This project also focuses on the local Kandyan kingdom forts as well, which have escaped scholarly attention. We are also engaged with the Sri Lankan Authorities and the World Bank on a development programme for the Jaffna Fort, where we are trying to see how Dutch urban heritage expertise could be used to revitalise this important site for all Sri Lankans. This cultural heritage cooperation goes beyond the Dutch built heritage as well and focuses on Museum objects and archives.

Currently, there is a Dutch Research Council-funded programme headed by the Sri Lanka National Archives to document the Sri Lankan palm leaf manuscripts in the collections of Dutch museums. This is a very important programme in which Dutch and Sri Lankan partners work collaboratively to document and digitise this corpus. Last November, the Netherlands provided the Sri Lanka National Archives with a high-tech industrial scanner for digitisation of the 300 metres of VOC archives. This will in time provide everybody with access to (machine-translated) archive material of that period.

Q What is the latest on the plan to return artefacts taken during the colonial time?

After the first repatriation in 2023, the Sri Lankan Government had submitted a second Repatriation request of six more artefacts housed in the Rijksmuseum and the World Museum. This request is currently being processed by the Colonial Collections Committee of the Netherlands together with the newly appointed Sri Lankan Restitution Committee.

Q As a member of European Union, how do you see the new counter terrorism law- Prevention of State from Terrorism Bill and the Organised Crimes Bill?

The Netherlands welcomes the efforts of the Sri Lankan government to modernise its legislation on counter terrorism and combating organised crime. We are following the debates in Parliament, within the government and civil society with interest. The recent proposal of the President to engage in a new round of consultations with all stakeholders is encouraging. We expect the new legislation to meet all international standards Sri Lanka committed itself to.

Sri Lanka will apply in the coming months for the extension of GSP+ after 2029. The new laws will certainly be considered. If your economy keeps on growing like this, you will graduate from GSP+ within six to seven years to the level where you need an FTA, which is something most people are not aware of yet.

QIn Europe, youth activists create violence, posing fresh challenges to democratic governance. How does the Netherlands view it?

Recent protests in Europe find their roots in calls from young people to improve the educational systems and changes in the labour market, as well as the high cost of living. In the Netherlands, protests are mostly conducted in an orderly way and without great violence and looting. The Netherlands has one of the most affordable educational systems that facilitates social mobility and is better prepared for an ageing society because of a robust pension system. The economy is doing relatively well, and there is still a great demand for workers and low youth unemployment. But we need to make sure that this relatively successful economic model also keeps on delivering for young people because the model is under pressure by, for instance, housing shortages and AI.
