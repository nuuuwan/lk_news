A new life in Saudi Arabia and Romance en route in Sri Lanka

A telegram brought the news that I’d been hoping for: Dr. Reda Al-Thiga, Dean of the Graduate School at Dhahran University, would be in Boston and wished to interview me. All my travel and accommodation costs for the interview would be reimbursed. On the scheduled day, I left Pittsburgh and checked into a luxurious five-star hotel which was also the interview venue. The meeting was set for six in the evening so I took a shower, got ready and went to the room I was summoned to.

When I entered the room, Dean Al-Thiga was watching the English news (CNN) on television. He kindly offered me a seat for me and asked me to wait until the newscast was over. So, I sat beside him, watching the news.

A week later after, another telegram arrived—I had been selected for the position of Associate Professor of Mechanical Engineering at the university in Saudi Arabia. The message included details of my salary and benefits. I was asked to come to Dhahran that August to assume duties. In response, I sent a telegram expressing my gratitude but also saying that the offered salary was not sufficient.

After some discussion on the telephone, I was told that higher salaries were paid to American citizens and I did not qualify. I responded that I was a green card holder (permanent resident of the US) when I applied for the job and had since become a naturalized citizen of the United States. On hearing this, they offered me a much higher salary and the matter was resolved to my satisfaction.

My plan was set: attend a technical conference in San Francisco in July, where I had to present a paper, then travel to Sri Lanka via Tokyo for a month before continuing on to Dhahran, Saudi Arabia. When I informed Carnegie Mellon University that I was considering a visiting appointment for one year in Saudi Arabia, the Department Head of the university told me that it was an unwise decision. However, after explaining my reasoning, CMU granted me leave, requesting that I return at the end of my leave period.

As planned, after the conference in San Francisco I flew to Sri Lanka for a holiday before going on to Saudi Arabia to take my faculty position there. Unexpected romance started during that visit. Was it a rebound, because I had left Audrey—with whom I shared a loving relationship in Pittsburgh? I couldn’t say.

In Sri Lanka, my two brothers came to meet me in Colombo. After dinner, my brothers suggested we go to the home of the sister of their friend Hema. He was a science graduate from University of Ceylon, Colombo, and was a teacher in a government school in the capital. When we arrived there, we were told that Hema had gone to the home of a friend (also a friend of my oldest brother), to tutor the children in that household. So we headed for that house.

Hema was not there but the house-owner, who was a widow, had just returned from England where her husband who was ill with a liver disease had passed away. Her mother greeted us and insisted we had a cup of tea before leaving. The mother went inside the house and I heard her telling the daughter about us and the lady came out to greet us. My oldest brother had known her. I remember her wearing a black skirt and a cream color blouse and looking depressed.

After listening to her at length, I advised her to go for physiotherapy—a term she later admitted she didn’t know. I felt sorry for her because she had three young children (a girl and two boys) and seemed helpless. Even though she appeared older than I, she was pretty, pleasant and kind.

She told us that they had a business of sending workers to the Middle East and earning a commission from the agents there. That was their only income. Since I was bound for Saudi Arabia, I told her that I could help and she was very grateful. In Saudi Arabia, I placed advertisements in English language newspapers describing the business and calling for local agents. I received several responses mostly from Indians or Pakistanis. They wanted to know how much I would pay them per worker they placed in a job.

I was confused, and called the lady in Sri Lanka. She explained that the actual business process was for her to collect money from each worker applying for overseas employment, send half of that to the overseas agent and have the workers pay their own airfare. I told her that I did not like that scheme and advised her to stop that business. But she needed an income until she found some other line of work. She was a university graduate. I agreed to help her—I was single, had plenty of money and also held a high-paying job. Gradually, our relationship turned into a romance.

While working in Saudi Arabia, I would visit Sri Lanka most weekends. Also, I had bought a nice house in a pleasant neighborhood where I could stay when I visited. One morning, while in Sri Lanka, a Sub-inspector (SI) of Police visited my house looking for my friend. I discussed the reason for his visit and was told there were complaints of fraud against my friend from some prospective workers who had paid her money but did not get overseas employment. I took the SI for lunch and asked him to settle the cases with the workers saying I would meet the necessary cost.

I thought that the problem had ended there. I was wrong. Months later I visited Singapore with my friend for a holiday. There, she had contacted a South Indian acquaintance who invited us for dinner. He did not take us to his apartment in a working class high-rise but seated us at a table in a food court attached to his building and ordered several bottles of beer—large and warm, and food. I warned my friend not to drink as I suspected the intentions of the man.

He told us about his lucrative business of bringing workers to Singapore from Sri Lanka and other Asian countries. After that trip we headed back to Sri Lanka, and I warned my friend not to have any business dealings with him as he was a suspicious character. As usual, I returned to Saudi Arabia for my work. Months later I found out that my friend had given that guy £5,000 from the money that I had left with her.

She had arranged with a travel agent friend to send workers to Singapore. The agent in Singapore who took the money, had not met the workers at Singapore airport and the airline sent them back to Sri Lanka. The travel agent had sued my friend for the money for the air tickets. That case was settled later out of court, and our romance blossomed.

After my holiday in Sri Lanka, I flew from Colombo to Karachi and from there arrived at Dhahran Airport on a flight of Pakistan International Airlines (PIA). The head of the Department of Mechanical Engineering of the Saudi University had come to the airport to meet me. First, he took me to the house that had been assigned to me, where I left my belongings, and from there, he took me to the university.

The university land was vast, just as I had read in the manual. There were trees newly planted in fresh soil, tall date palms, and massive concrete buildings. There were also neatly arranged and modern staff quarters and a well-planned road network. On one side, I could see the Arabian Sea, part of the mighty Indian Ocean. The scorching heat could not be eased even by the sight of water. The buildings and vehicles were air-conditioned, which was a blessing. Whenever a slight breeze passed, fine sand would rise and enter my nostrils, mouth, ears, and eyes.

The vast desert, punctuated by oil wells, even within the campus itself, weighed heavily on me. As I stood there, I felt an almost irresistible urge, which I controlled with difficulty, to board the next available flight and either go back to Sri Lanka or to the United States.

The house provided to me was spacious—large enough for a family—and fully air-conditioned. It had everything that a home would need—furniture, refrigerator, electric stove, pots, pans, wooden cupboards, crockery, cutlery, storage boxes, mats, and even linen (bed sheets, pillowcases, towels). All I needed to buy was a vehicle, a television set, and a music system.

There were a few small shops near the university where I could buy groceries. A bus service was available to travel to the city. In the bustling marketplace known as the souq or souk in Dhahran, one could buy vehicles, electronic appliances, clothes, and other essentials. Since there were no taxes, and because Asian workers were paid low wages, along with the abundance of cheap fuel, goods were remarkably inexpensive. There were similar marketplaces in other cities including the nearby Al-Khobar.

The university had workers from India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka. Many of them were Muslims, and they preferred traditional Arab attire—robes and head coverings—in place of the pants and shirts they wore at home. This they did, I was told, in order to curry favour with the Saudis.

In the school of engineering, I was the only Sri Lankan professor. Office workers and staff were men. Due to the strict religious police, women had to follow Arab cultural norms—they had to cover their heads with a hijab, and were not allowed to wear sleeveless or short dresses, drive vehicles or work in public jobs. Alcohol consumption and possessing inappropriate books were also forbidden. If anyone was caught bringing such items into the country, they would face severe punishment. The same applied to theft and other crimes. Even the mention of non-Islamic religious figures was not allowed. For example, when playing the song Fire and Rain by the US singer James Taylor, the radio station would blot out the word Jesus!

As a routine, I would have my breakfast at home before leaving for work and would usually have lunch at the university cafeteria, where the food was very affordable. After finishing work, I would return home in the evening and later go back to the university cafeteria for dinner. On the days I went to the city, I got used to eating at Indian or Pakistani restaurants. Parents of some of my students owned restaurants in the city. Whenever I ate there, they would refuse to accept payment for the food.

A Pakistani worker from my office had a young daughter who attended a school near my house. Every morning, his wife would drop the child at school and then come to my house to clean it and do the laundry. I paid her a monthly stipend for her services. Since my workload was light and I didn’t have to focus on research as I had done in the US, I found myself with much free time. However, there were no entertainment activities to indulge in. So, I decided to restart something I had put aside for a long time: writing technical books.

During my time in Saudi Arabia, I was able to visit Sri Lanka at least once a month, and often once a week. The related administrative process was time consuming because the university did not allow non-Arabs to leave the country without getting approval from them. I had to hand over my travel documents to the university office soon after I returned to Dhahran after a trip outside the country. Then, whenever I wanted to leave the country, I had to fill out a detailed application form and get approval from several officials, including the Head of the Department. Only then would I receive my travel documents from the university.

At that time, there were no direct flights from Dhahran to Colombo. So, I had to first travel to an airport in Bahrain, Bombay (Mumbai), Karachi, or Dubai and then board another flight to Colombo. Dhahran airport itself was chaotic—check-in counters mobbed by desperate passengers hoisting luggage high, pressing forward, and pleading with Indian or Pakistani airline staff for seats on the next flight. In this manner, typically, after about an hour of waiting and pleading, I would finally receive my boarding pass, even though I had a reserved seat on the flight. If a fellow Lankan worker or someone I knew was working at the counter, which happened sometimes, I could get the boarding pass quickly and without much trouble.

David Da Cunha was an associate professor in our department in Dhahran. He was from the Goa in India and was a Catholic. He often mentioned that his ancestors came from Portugal and asked me whether mine did too. I said no, my ancestors had to change their names for the convenience of the rulers. David liked his smokes and drinks much like the Russians. On many a weekend he would fly to nearby island country of Bahrain, where liquor is legal for foreigners. Also, whenever there was a university vacation, he would visit places like Bangkok and Bombay to enjoy himself.
