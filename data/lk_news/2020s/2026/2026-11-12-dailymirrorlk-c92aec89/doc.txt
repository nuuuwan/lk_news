Lights, Camera, Legal Action: What Every Filmmaker Should Know When Making a Celebrity Biopic

A “life-rights” agreement is a contract, not a universal statutory licence

An actor’s impression of a pop star is not necessarily unlawful. But a deliberately copied voice used to sell goods can present a very different legal problem

United Kingdom and Europe: English law provides no general inheritable celebrity publicity right. Estates may enforce copyrights, trademarks, contracts or passing-off interests

Celebrity biopics — from Bohemian Rhapsody and Rocketman to films about celebrated actors—invite audiences behind the public persona. Yet the filmmaker who acquires a fascinating story has not necessarily acquired the legal rights needed to tell it.

Can a producer dramatise a singer’s life without consent? Can an actor recreate a distinctive voice? Who controls hit songs, recordings and concert footage? Are the family’s wishes binding after a celebrity dies? The answers depend on the law of the country concerned and, crucially, on the distinction between biographical facts, protected creative works and personal rights. This article addresses musicians, pop stars, actors and other entertainment celebrities—not biographical films about public figures generally.  1. Must the celebrity authorise the film?

A “life-rights” agreement is a contract, not a universal statutory licence. It may provide interviews, access to private archives, cooperation, rights to authorised publicity and agreed contractual protections. In de Havilland v FX Networks LLC (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 845, the California Court of Appeal rejected actress Olivia de Havilland’s challenge to her portrayal in Feud,  and stressed First Amendment protection for expressive works. Famous people do not automatically control every dramatic portrayal of their lives.

The central legal principle emerging from the decision may be summarised as: The unauthorised portrayal of a real person, including a celebrity, in a biographical film, television drama or other expressive work does not, merely by reason of the absence of consent or the commercial nature of the production, constitute an actionable infringement of the right of publicity.

Such portrayals enjoy substantial First Amendment protection. This is a very wide interpretation. Nevertheless, its protection is not absolute, particularly where independently actionable defamatory statements or other legally recognised wrongs are established such as the recently enacted two important California laws on protecting performers’ publicity rights, voice, image and likeness against unauthorised digital replication, particularly through artificial intelligence (i.e. the Use of Likeness: Digital Replica Law and Contracts Against Public Policy: Personal or Professional Services: Digital Replicas Law)

The Indian Supreme Court in R Rajagopal v State of Tamil Nadu (1994) 6 SCC 632 distinguished material drawn from public records from private matters. The judgment establishes that filmmakers may portray events documented in public records without obtaining the individual’s consent. However, portraying intimate aspects of someone’s private life beyond those records may constitute an invasion of privacy, even where the information is truthful.

In Sri Lanka, section 8 of the Intellectual Property Act No. 36 of 2003 excludes mere ideas and data from copyright protection. There is no general statutory “life copyright” merely because an individual is famous. On the other hand,  neither the US nor Indian judgments automatically determine a Sri Lankan claim,  nor give the film makers the freedom of the wildness in the guise of free speech. Consent is still commercially wise and useful—and may be indispensable for private access or specific licensed material.  2. A hit song is not a single copyright

A musician’s biopic may involve at least four interests: copyright in lyrics, copyright in the composition, rights in the sound recording and performers’ rights. The singer may own none of the first three. US Copyright Act, 17 USC §§106 and 114, and the UK Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 distinguish musical works from recordings; India’s Copyright Act 1957 does likewise under sections 13–14 and 38–38B.

Sri Lanka’s Intellectual Property Act protects literary and musical works through sections 6 and 9, author’s moral rights through section 10, performers through section 17 and sound-recording producers through section 18. Therefore, the pop star’s signature on a life-rights agreement cannot automatically authorise songs written, published or recorded under third-party contracts.  3. Synchronisation and master-use clearances

To place a song into a film soundtrack, producers ordinarily negotiate a synchronisation licence for the composition and lyrics; using an existing famous recording ordinarily requires a separate master-use licence. Re-recording the song with an actor can avoid licensing that particular master, but does not ordinarily dispense with synchronisation permission. Examine co-writers, publishers, record-label agreements, neighbouring rights, territories, streaming, trailers, soundtrack albums and duration. US compulsory mechanical licensing for certain audio cover recordings under 17 USC §115 does not supply a general right to synchronise songs with films.

Sri Lankan section 9 gives the copyright owner rights over reproduction, adaptation, performance and communication; sections 17–19 separately address performances and recordings. Sri Lanka has no equivalent general compulsory synchronisation licence for biopics. Producers should not confuse permission to perform a cover song with permission to incorporate it into a film.  4. What about concert recreations and choreography?

Recreating a singer on stage is different from inserting an existing television broadcast or concert recording. The latter may involve audiovisual copyright, the song, the recording, performers’ rights and a broadcaster’s rights. In Zacchini v Scripps-Howard Broadcasting Co, 433 US 562 (1977), the US Supreme Court recognised protection against appropriation of a performer’s entire commercially valuable act. That was not a ruling banning ordinary dramatic re-enactments. Under Sri Lanka’s Intellectual Property Act, sections 17, 18 and 20 are pertinent to performances, sound recordings and broadcasts. Choreography, stage artwork, photographs and distinctive graphic designs may also involve separate rights. A newly staged sequence should therefore be built from cleared musical and visual elements rather than assumed to be free because the original concert was public.  5. Voice imitation, likeness and personality

An actor’s impression of a pop star is not necessarily unlawful. But a deliberately copied voice used to sell goods can present a very different legal problem. In Midler v Ford Motor Co, 849 F.2d 460 (9th Cir. 1988), the court permitted a claim arising from imitation of Bette Midler’s distinctive voice in a car advertisement. The advertising setting is critical: the case does not create an absolute ban on dramatic portrayal. Indian courts have also protected commercial personality interests; in Anil Kapoor v Simply Life India (Delhi High Court, 20 September 2023), the court restrained specific unauthorised exploitation of the actor’s identity, including technologically manipulated uses. Sri Lanka does not have a directly equivalent comprehensive celebrity-publicity statute. However, misleading commercial exploitation may raise passing-off or unfair-competition issues, notably section 160 of the Intellectual Property Act, alongside other causes of action as facts permit.  6. Names, images, brands and promotional merchandise

A film may identify the real star without automatically becoming an “official” production. Yet posters, product tie-ins, T-shirts and advertising can create a false impression of endorsement. In Fenty v Arcadia Group Brands Ltd [2015] EWCA Civ 3, Rihanna succeeded in passing off against Top shop over a particular T-shirt presentation. The UK court did not recognise an automatic monopoly over every celebrity photograph.

In Sri Lanka, copyrighted photographs and album artwork engage sections 6 and 9 of the Intellectual Property Act; registered trademarks and section 160’s unfair-competition provisions may become relevant to unauthorised branded merchandise or deceptive association. Producers should separately clear official logos, promotional photography, album sleeves and third-party marks where their intended use requires it.  7. Privacy is not extinguished by fame

Celebrity films routinely dramatise addiction, illnesses, sexual relationships and family disputes. In Campbell v MGN Ltd [2004] UKHL 22, the House of Lords balanced Naomi Campbell’s privacy interests against legitimate public-interest reporting. In P J S v News Group Newspapers Ltd [2016] UKSC 26, the UK Supreme Court considered privacy even after some material had circulated elsewhere. A famous singer’s children, partners or medical advisers may have rights independent of the star. Sri Lankan filmmakers should consider the Roman-Dutch-law-based civil protection of reputation and dignity, confidentiality duties and constitutional free-expression values, rather than assuming that every detail publicly gossiped about may safely be dramatised. Sri Lanka’s Personal Data Protection Act No. 9 of 2022, amended by Act No. 22 of 2025, has staggered commencement: the regulator states key operative provisions will begin on 1 January 2027. Its future relevance to production research should be monitored, without treating not-yet-commenced obligations as presently enforceable.  8. Defamation and Invented Scenes: Legal Risks in Celebrity Biopics and Online Distribution

A celebrity biopic may become defamatory when fictionalised scenes, dialogue or allegations falsely portray an identifiable individual as dishonest, immoral or criminal. Even a film described as “based on true events” may attract liability if viewers reasonably interpret damaging fictional incidents as facts.

In Sri Lanka, civil defamation remains actionable under Roman-Dutch law. In De Costa v. The Times of Ceylon, the courts affirmed the application of Roman-Dutch principles. In Mendis v. Chulanie [1999] 3 Sri LR 328, the Court of Appeal recognised that defamation may occur through words, pictures or other representations. These principles may extend to biographical films and online publications.

In the United Kingdom, the Defamation Act 2013, sections 1–4, addresses serious reputational harm and defences of truth, honest opinion and public interest. In litigation concerning The Lost King (2022), the English High Court found that the film’s overall portrayal of an identifiable individual carried a defamatory meaning. The case subsequently settled in 2025.

In the United States, De Havilland v. FX Networks LLC (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 845  although held First Amendment protection for expressive works and famous people do not automatically control every dramatic portrayal of their lives it established important constitutional protection for fictionalised celebrity portrayals, while recognising that actionable defamatory factual implications may still arise.  In India, section 356 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 retains criminal defamation, including specified imputations concerning deceased persons. In Phoolan Devi v. Shekhar Kapoor (1994), the Delhi High Court examined objections to allegedly distorted and intrusive cinematic portrayals, principally concerning privacy rather than a final finding of defamation.

Online defamation: Publication through YouTube, Netflix, social media, trailers and promotional interviews can likewise create liability. Digital republication may amplify reputational harm across jurisdictions.

Filmmakers should verify allegations, distinguish fact from dramatic invention and undertake legal clearance before theatrical or online release.  9. Adapting biographies, interviews and archives

Historical events themselves are generally not copyright-protected, but another writer’s original wording, dramatic arrangement or photographs may be. A purchased biography does not give the producer ownership of every interview or photograph included in it; nor does buying an old letter necessarily transfer its copyright. Sri Lanka’s Intellectual Property Act section 8 excludes ideas and mere data, while sections 6–7 protect original works and qualifying derivative works, and section 9 governs reproduction and adaptation. Sections 11–12 recognise fair use in appropriate circumstances, but this is a case-specific exception—not a blanket licence for an entertainment biopic. Comparable distinctions exist under US and UK copyright legislation. Licence the material actually being copied and keep documentary proof of chain of title.  10. Fair use is not a production strategy Some filmmakers assume that brief clips, familiar melodies or album covers can always be inserted because the film is “historical”. That is unsafe. Under Sri Lanka’s section 11, the fairness inquiry considers purpose, the nature of the work, the amount used and market impact. Section 12 deals with specified acts, including certain quotations and current-events reporting. The US fair-use doctrine under 17 USC §107 and UK fair-dealing exceptions operate differently. Commercial biopics do not automatically qualify, even if they contain commentary. A lawyer should assess individual clips or extracts against the precise jurisdictional exception; otherwise secure a licence.  11. Deceased stars and their estates Death does not automatically make relatives owners of a celebrity’s life story. Yet it is equally inaccurate to say that families can never enforce rights after death. Distinguish inherited publicity rights from survivors’ privacy and estate copyrights.

United States: In Estate of Presley v. Russen, 513 F. Supp. 1339 (D.N.J. 1981), concerning an Elvis Presley impersonation show, the federal court held under its interpretation of New Jersey law that Presley’s commercial right of publicity survived and became part of his estate. The Georgia Supreme Court reached a similar conclusion in Martin Luther King, Jr. Center for Social Change, Inc. v. American Heritage Products, Inc., 250 Ga. 135, 296 S.E.2d 697 (1982): the publicity right was inheritable and devisable. California Civil Code §3344.1(b) expressly makes specified posthumous publicity rights transferable or descendible; and §3344.1(d) allocates rights among qualifying relatives, generally for 70 years. New York Civil Rights Law §50-f similarly provides a transferable posthumous publicity right, generally lasting 40 years, for qualifying New York-domiciled personalities and performers. Expressive-work exemptions mean there is no automatic veto over biopics.

Survivors may also possess independent privacy interests. In National Archives and Records Administration v. Favish, 541 U.S. 157 (2004), the US Supreme Court recognised surviving relatives’ own privacy interests in death-scene photographs under the federal Freedom of Information Act. That does not confer film-approval rights.

United Kingdom and Europe: English law provides no general inheritable celebrity publicity right. Estates may enforce copyrights, trademarks, contracts or passing-off interests. In Putistin v. Ukraine (2013), Application No. 16882/03, the European Court of Human Rights accepted that harm to a deceased relative’s reputation could, in certain circumstances, affect a survivor’s own private life under Article 8; on the facts, no violation was established. It created no general biopic veto.

Germany offers protection: in the Marlene Dietrich decision, Bundesgerichtshof, 1 December 1999, I ZR 49/97, the court held that economically valuable personality interests pass to heirs. Germany’s Kunsturhebergesetz §22 also generally requires relatives’ consent to publication of portraits for ten years after death, subject to §23 exceptions, including contemporary history and artistic interests.

India takes a contrasting approach. In Krishna Kishore Singh v. Sarla A. Saraogi (Delhi High Court, 11 July 2023), concerning the late actor Sushant Singh Rajput, the court held that the asserted privacy, personality and publicity rights were not inheritable by his father.  Sri Lanka has no equivalent general posthumous publicity statute as yet although it is time such law sees the light of the day. Copyright can survive under section 13 of the Intellectual Property Act No. 36 of 2003 and devolve according to ownership and succession rules; family privacy, confidentiality and unfair competition require separate analysis. Filmmakers must identify rights holders. However, in an overall analysis, the progressive approach that has been taken both by the legislature and the Supreme Court of United States seems to give justa causa to posthumously protecting the privacy and the publicity rights of celebrities or any other for that matter in a technologically advancing world privacy, and publicity rights of human are eroded in an unprecedented manner.   12. AI replicas and digital resurrection

Modern technology can reproduce an actor’s face or synthesise a deceased vocalist’s singing voice. That does not mean it is legally unrestricted. The Anil Kapoor litigation illustrates Indian judicial concern over deepfakes and exploitative commercial impersonation. California’s updated §3344.1 and New York §50-f address particular digital-replica uses with exemptions.

In Sri Lanka, even without a dedicated general digital-replica statute, the use of training footage, recordings, images and branded promotion can engage copyright, performers’ rights, contractual duties and unfair competition under the Intellectual Property Act. Obtaining explicit digital-replica provisions in actor, estate and archival agreements, and assess whether audiences could be misled into believing the celebrity genuinely spoke or sang new material is therefore the wise correct path. Contracts must distinguish rights actually granted from artistic approval or mere cooperation. Sri Lankan producers making films for global streaming must also account for laws of major distribution territories; a Sri Lankan clearance alone may not settle US, UK or Indian risks.

A celebrity’s public life provides material for powerful cinema, but not a free licence to exploit the creative work and protected interests surrounding it nor to ride on the celebrities dramatic life story for box office success. Music biopics particularly demand separation of the story from song copyright, master recordings, performers’ rights and concert footage. Films about actors and other entertainment stars similarly require scrutiny of images, archives, privacy, reputation and misleading endorsement.
