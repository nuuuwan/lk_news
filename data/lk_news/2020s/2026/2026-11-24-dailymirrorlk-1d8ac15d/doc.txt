The Elephant  Doesn’t Need Us…  We Need the Elephant

It is estimated that around 65–70% of Sri Lanka’s wild elephants range outside protected areas

More than 400 wild elephant deaths recorded in some recent years, and around 100–150 human lives lost

Much has been written about why we need to save the elephant. We speak about its intelligence, complex social behaviour, and close family bonds and, of course, its iconic status as one of the world’s most magnificent and charismatic wild animals. In Sri Lanka, the elephant has an even deeper significance, woven into our culture, religion, history and, more recently, tourism.

But perhaps there is another, more fundamental reason why the elephant matters. It is not merely another large animal inhabiting our forests. It is an animal actually helping to shape and maintain the very ecosystem in which it lives.

Elephants are mega-herbivores and are the largest mammals living on earth. They are also considered an important keystone and flagship species. An adult Asian elephant may spend 17–19 hours a day feeding and consume around 150 kg or more of fresh vegetation each day.1

Elephants are hindgut fermenters, which means that much of the fermentation of the plant material they consume takes place in the large intestine, unlike in a complex multi-chambered stomach such as that of cattle and other ruminants. Their digestive efficiency is relatively low, so this allows them to process very large quantities of vegetation quite rapidly. An elephant’s seemingly endless search for food is an inevitable consequence of being such a massive animal, living predominantly on vegetation of relatively low nutritional value.2

Many of the seeds contained in the fruits and vegetation eaten by elephants survive their journey through the digestive tract and emerge intact in the dung. Research on Asian elephants has recorded a mean seed retention time of about 40 hours, with some seeds remaining within the digestive system for considerably longer. Combine this with the distances a wild elephant can travel during that period, and the elephant becomes a very large mobile seed transport system.3

Studies involving Asian elephants in Sri Lanka have estimated that about half of the seeds dispersed by elephants were transported more than one kilometre from where they were consumed, while maximum dispersal distances in the study approached six kilometres.4

Thus, elephants, because of their huge food intake, long gut-passage time and extensive movements, can transport large numbers of seeds across considerable distances.

The seed is then deposited in piles of moist elephant dung containing organic matter and nutrients. It is well known that seeds of many species remain viable after passing through an elephant, while in some cases passage through the digestive tract can enhance germination.

So as an elephant moves across the landscape feeding, walking and defecating, it is effectively ‘planting the forest as it walks along’.

There is an interesting and very appropriate term sometimes used by scientists to describe elephants in this role as “mega-gardeners of the forest”.5 The elephant does not consciously plant anything, of course, but over its lifetime it may transport enormous quantities of seeds from one part of the landscape to another. In doing so it can contribute to forest regeneration.

An elephant moving through dense vegetation also physically alters its surroundings. It pushes over small trees, breaks branches, strips bark, uproots shrubs and opens pathways through thick vegetation. Over time, these develop into pathways called “elephant corridors” (mankada).

At first sight some of this can appear to be a rather destructive method of gardening. But ecosystems are not gardens that need to remain neat and tidy, and in nature, sometimes disruption is an important ecological process. By opening dense vegetation, elephants can create gaps through which sunlight reaches the ground, encouraging new plant growth and changing the structure of the vegetation.

Their movements also create and maintain pathways used by other animals. Around water sources, their repeated movements can modify river banks and access points, while their digging and disturbance of soil can alter small patches of habitat. Their dung, produced in very large quantities, returns partly digested plant material and nutrients to the environment and provides food and habitat for an entire community of insects and other small organisms.

The elephant therefore does not simply live in an ecosystem. To a great extent, it helps create and continuously modify that ecosystem.

This is what ecologists mean when they describe the elephant as an ecosystem engineer.6 It physically changes its environment in ways that influence other species. Elephants achieve this over larger landscapes, through their cumulative effect of feeding, trampling, digging, breaking vegetation, dispersing seeds and transporting nutrients.

A wild elephant cannot survive indefinitely in small isolated pockets of forests. It requires access to adequate food and water, but also, more importantly, it requires the ability to move. Elephants have evolved to range across landscapes according to rainfall, vegetation, water availability and seasonal changes. The Sri Lankan male elephants have a “home range” of about 100 sq.km during much of the year, but they may expand their ranges dramatically during musth, to even almost four times their normal range. Females and herds, on the other hand, have a much smaller range of about 50-70 sq.km.7 Some traditional movement routes may be used repeatedly and, in some instances, passed down through generations.

This is why fragmentation is such a serious problem for elephant conservation. When a road, village settlement, agricultural scheme, fence or other development suddenly cuts across an elephant’s traditional range, the problem is not simply that a few hectares of habitat have been lost. The connectivity of the landscape itself may have been disrupted.

It is in this wider ecological context the human-elephant conflict (HEC) takes on a different perspective. Sri Lanka suffers from the highest annual elephant mortality rate globally relative to its small population, alongside the second-highest human death toll from elephant encounters. With more than 400 wild elephant deaths recorded in some recent years, and around 100–150 human lives being lost annually due to HEC, this is indeed a crisis of very large proportions. In fact, HEC is said to be Sri Lanka’s most acute socio-environmental crisis. Unfortunately, despite all the discussion and interventions over the years, there still seems to be no focused, overarching solution to the problem in sight.

A farmer whose crop is destroyed in a single night suffers a very real economic loss, and a family that loses someone to an elephant attack is a tragedy that cannot be dismissed with ecological arguments. Equally, hundreds of elephants are being killed through shootings, electrocution, explosive devices, poisoning, train collisions and other causes associated directly or indirectly with the conflict.

Sri Lanka also presents a particularly difficult problem related to HEC. Wild elephants in Sri Lanka today survive not only inside national parks and protected areas, but across extensive landscapes shared with people. It is estimated that around 65–70% of Sri Lanka’s wild elephants range outside protected areas for most part of their lives, sharing increasingly fragmented landscapes with people.8 Consequently, the old idea that elephants can simply be confined within national parks, while people occupy the land outside, does not reflect ecological reality. Elephants do not recognise the administrative boundaries we have drawn on maps.

But the elephant did not suddenly decide to enter into conflict with humans. In many instances, it is we who have gradually moved agriculture, settlements and infrastructure into landscapes that elephants were already using, thereby restricting their ability to move elsewhere.

For most of that evolutionary history, elephants did perfectly well without wildlife departments, conservationists, management plans, electric fences or national parks. They responded to droughts, climatic changes, changing vegetation and other natural pressures through movement, adaptation and natural selection.

Today we speak constantly about the need to “manage elephants”. We translocate them, fence them in or fence them out, drive them away from settlements, construct barriers across their ranges and debate endlessly about how many elephants a particular area should contain. Some intervention has, of course, become unavoidable because the landscapes in which elephants now survive, have been so drastically altered by people.

But this “management” has now become necessary because we have changed the landscape.

The irony becomes even greater when we consider what healthy natural ecosystems provide for us. Forests and grasslands help regulate water, protect soils, store carbon, recycle nutrients and sustain biological diversity, and are part of the natural life-support systems, on which human societies and economies depend.

Elephants are one component of these complex systems. Where elephants occur naturally, they are major ecological actors. Their enormous body size, huge food requirements, mobility and ability to modify vegetation give them an ecological influence quite disproportionate to their numbers. Remove such an animal from an ecosystem, and there will be significant change.

The irony is that ecologically, the elephant does not need mankind. Given sufficient habitat, food, water and the freedom to move, wild elephants would probably manage perfectly well without us. Indeed, their ancestors did so for millions of years before modern humans transformed their landscapes.

We, on the other hand, need functioning natural ecosystems. We need forests, water catchments, fertile soils, biodiversity and climatic stability. And elephants, where they naturally occur, are one of the many species helping to maintain the ecological processes that sustain those systems.

So perhaps we need to change the way we look at elephant conservation. Saving elephants is not simply about being kind to a magnificent animal, nor is it only about preserving an iconic species for our children to see. It is about understanding that the elephant is part of an intricate ecological machinery that has evolved over an immense period of time, and that dismantling pieces of that machinery inevitably will have serious consequences.

Perhaps therein lies one of the great contradictions of elephant conservation today. We speak constantly about “managing elephants”, when in reality, much of what we are trying to manage are problems that we ourselves have created.

Maybe it is time we started managing ourselves a little better.
