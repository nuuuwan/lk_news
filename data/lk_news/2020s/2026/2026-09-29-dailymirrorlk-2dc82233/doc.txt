Middle East escalation threatens Sri Lanka on 6 economic fronts: ADB

Flags risks to fuel, freight, fertiliser, food and subsidy costs

Warns exports, remittances, tourism and reserves could come under pressure

Remittances remain key buffer, rising 21.4% in first 7 months

Sri Lanka faces a multi-pronged economic threat from any further escalation of the Middle East conflict, the Asian Development Bank (ADB) said, as it warned that the fallout could simultaneously hit the import costs, exports, remittances, tourism, foreign reserves and the government’s fiscal position.

In its Asian Development Outlook September 2026, the ADB said the risks to Sri Lanka’s economic outlook remain tilted to the downside, with the Middle East conflict posing a particularly broad threat to an economy still rebuilding its external buffers.

“The escalation of the Middle East conflict could raise the fuel, freight, fertiliser, food and subsidy costs and weigh on the exports, remittances, tourism and reserves,” the ADB cautioned.

The warning indicates a potential double hit for Sri Lanka: the higher costs flowing into the economy through the energy, shipping and food channels, while the key sources of foreign exchange could weaken at the same time.

Sri Lanka’s external position has already deteriorated in 2026. The current account recorded a US $ 387 million deficit in the first seven months, reversing a US $ 1.7 billion surplus in the corresponding period of 2025.

The merchandise trade deficit widened 69 percent to US $ 6.5 billion, as the imports surged 25.8 percent while the exports grew by just 4.4 percent. Total goods and services exports rose 2.2 percent to US $ 12.3 billion but were insufficient to offset the higher import bill and a narrower services surplus.

Foreign reserves have also come under some pressure. The gross official reserves declined from US $ 6.8 billion at end-2025 to US $ 6.6 billion by end-July, equivalent to around 3.2 months of imports.

The rupee depreciated 7.8 percent against the US dollar by end-July before recovering in late August, narrowing its year-to-date depreciation to 5.5 percent.

Against this backdrop, the ADB stressed that “rebuilding reserves and sustaining investor confidence remain priorities”, particularly with the public debt still high.

The Middle East risk is especially significant, given Sri Lanka’s dependence on imported energy and its exposure to the Gulf economies through labour migration, aviation and tourism.

The ADB said the geopolitical pressures on the global energy markets have spread beyond crude oil, with disruption affecting the refined petroleum products and key shipping routes. The diesel and gasoline refining margins have risen sharply, while the higher security and insurance costs have kept the freight costs elevated.

This means relief in the international crude prices alone may not immediately translate into lower costs for the import-dependent economies. The ADB noted that the refined-product prices could remain elevated even if the crude prices ease, sustaining pressure on transport, industrial and agricultural costs.

Across Asia and the Pacific, economies have sought to diversify the crude supplies away from the Middle East but the region remains reliant on the Gulf oil and vulnerable to the disruptions along the major shipping routes.

Tourism is another channel through which the Middle East conflict is already being felt.

“The conflict’s impact on tourism in the region has been more evident than on the remittance flows so far,” the ADB said, pointing to the airspace closures, flight rerouting, longer flying times, higher airfares and elevated jet fuel costs.

The impact has been particularly pronounced in South Asia because of its dependence on the Gulf carriers and transit hubs.

For Sri Lanka, tourist arrivals declined 1.8 percent in the first seven months of 2026, while the earnings fell 11.5 percent on the lower spending per visitor.

The remittances, however, have so far provided an important counterweight. The inflows increased 21.4 percent year-on-year during January-July, remaining the main buffer against the deterioration in the external account.

The ADB nevertheless warned that the risks to the remittance flows could increase if the Middle Eastern employment opportunities weaken or worker repatriation rises. Regionally, the remittance flows from the Middle East have remained resilient so far, despite the conflict.

The Middle East shock is also colliding with another emerging threat to Sri Lanka’s recovery: a very strong El Niño.

The ADB said El Niño is already materialising, damaging crops and threatening the hydropower supply in Sri Lanka. Regionally, the phenomenon is expected to strengthen through late 2026 and persist into 2027, potentially adding further pressure on the food and energy prices.

Despite these risks, the ADB retained Sri Lanka’s GDP growth forecast at 4 percent for both 2026 and 2027, while projecting inflation at 6 percent this year and 5.2 percent next year.

The combination of geopolitical and weather-related shocks, however, leaves Sri Lanka navigating its recovery with less room for external slippage, particularly as the current account weakens and the country seeks to rebuild the reserves.

Sri Lanka’s target of attracting three million tourists in 2026 appears increasingly out of reach, according to the Asian Development Bank (ADB), due to the weaker arrivals and a sharper drop in earnings from the sector.

“With only 1.5 million arrivals by end-August, the three million target for 2026 appears distant,” the ADB said in its latest Asian Development Outlook September 2026.

The tourist arrivals fell 1.8 percent year-on-year (YoY) during the first seven months of 2026, while the tourism earnings declined by a much steeper 11.5 percent, reflecting lower spending per visitor.

The divergence between the arrivals and earnings adds another concern for the industry, suggesting Sri Lanka is not only struggling to build visitor volumes fast enough to reach its annual target but is also generating less foreign exchange from tourists.

The weakness comes at a time when tourism is expected to play an important role in strengthening Sri Lanka’s external buffers, following the economic crisis.

The ADB said the country’s current account slipped into a US $ 387 million deficit during the first seven months of the year, against a US $ 1.7 billion surplus a year earlier, making the foreign exchange earnings from tourism and other services increasingly important.

The worker remittances have fared considerably better, rising 21.4 percent YoY during January-July, providing what the ADB described as the main buffer for the external account.

Sri Lanka’s tourism industry is also navigating a more difficult external environment, particularly due to the continued instability in the Middle East.

“The conflict’s impact on tourism in the region has been more evident than on the remittance flows so far,” the ADB said.

The airspace closures and flight rerouting have increased the travel times and airfares, while the higher jet fuel costs and weaker outbound travel from the Gulf economies have added to the pressure on tourism.

The ADB noted that the decline in tourist arrivals in April was the steepest in South Asia, which is particularly dependent on the Gulf carriers and transit hubs.

For Sri Lanka, the lender warned that any further escalation of the Middle East conflict could weigh on tourism alongside the exports and remittances, while adding pressure on the foreign exchange reserves.
