The next phase of the West Asian conflict: A world war in the making

Houthi rebels ride a motorbike during a recruitment drive in Yemen’s capital, Sanaa

The Middle East is exploding into what could be described as a world war, given the sheer number of countries involved or becoming involved in the region’s multiple conflicts and the devastating global consequences.

The United States and Israel are gearing up once again to unleash their aggression against Iran, threatening to strike the country with far greater destructive force than they have in the eight-month-old conflict. Although kinetic action has eased since the US attack on Iran on August 31, heightened military activity in the region points to an escalation on an unprecedented scale.

Washington is sending more warships to the area, while Israeli fighter jets are provocatively conducting rehearsals close to Iran’s western border and in the skies above the strategic Strait of Hormuz. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, the principal instigator of the war, reminds US President Donald Trump in a telephone conversation that they have an unfinished job in Iran. It may be an election gimmick, but Trump responds: “Let’s finish the job.” Trump is wooing Russia by lifting sanctions on diesel exports and ratcheting up pressure on Ukraine’s President Volodymyr Zelensky, hoping these friendly measures will prompt Russia to halt military support for Iran. The United States has issued a travel advisory urging its citizens to avoid the region, while some diplomatic personnel have been advised to leave. Gulf countries have begun to regard the coming escalation as a fait accompli.

In the meantime, military movements in Saudi Arabia indicate a major regional flare-up, as Yemen’s Houthi forces continue to make gains against Saudi-backed forces. Senior Pakistani and Turkish military officials are already in Saudi Arabia. Pakistan has sent thousands of troops, including infantry and artillery brigades, to the kingdom. Further deployments are on the cards following the activation of the Mecca Defence Pact signed by Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Türkiye. Egyptian President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi has revealed plans to send military assistance to Saudi Arabia, while Syria has agreed to dispatch thousands of fighters to join the kingdom’s war against the Houthis. Saudi Arabia has also mended fences with the United Arab Emirates to develop a common strategy on Yemen.

Perhaps no words better describe the military buildup against the Houthis than the English idiom “Fools rush in where angels fear to tread.”

Since the war broke out in 2014, Saudi Arabia has relied on its superior air power to force a Houthi surrender. But the Iranian-backed Houthis have survived thousands of air strikes, not only by Saudi Arabia but also by the United States and Israel. The Saudis have avoided a land war for fear of suffering large-scale casualties at the hands of battle-hardened Houthi fighters in Yemen’s treacherous mountainous terrain. In the past, military invasions and occupations by the Ottomans, the Egyptians and the British have turned into humiliating Waterloos in Yemen, both in the north and the south.

Besides, the Saudi leadership feared that a land invasion and a protracted war would keep investors away from its ambitious Vision 2030 programme aimed at modernising and industrialising the kingdom.

If the war with Iran escalates, Saudi Arabia’s all-out war against the Houthis is also likely to escalate in parallel. The question is no longer whether an escalation will occur; rather, it is a matter of when. The most likely timeframe would be after, or thereabouts, the Israeli elections on October 27 and the US midterm elections on November 3.

Meanwhile, whatever behind-the-scenes talks Iran and the United States have conducted through mediators Pakistan and Qatar appear to have reached a stalemate, with both sides showing no willingness to meet the other’s demands while continuing to harden their positions.

After the November elections, the political pressure Trump faces from within the Republican Party and from voters opposed to the unpopular war will ease, leaving him virtually free to create havoc in the global order. He is unlikely to pay much attention to the war’s adverse consequences that Americans and people around the world, especially the poor, will suffer in the form of rising fuel and food prices.

Iran has said it is well prepared to respond in kind to any military strike and has even warned regional countries that partner with the US. The worst-case scenario could plunge Iran and the rest of the region back into the Stone Age, with oil facilities and civilian infrastructure, including power and water installations, reduced to rubble and their economies left in ruins. The United States, which Gulf Arab leaders once thought, and some still think, would protect them, will not be able to shield them from Iranian attacks. Nor will it be able to defend them indefinitely with expensive and sophisticated air-defence systems, the ammunition for which the United States itself is running short. The only country that would benefit from this scenario is Israel.

Even if the Democrats secure control of Congress in the midterm elections, it will be nearly impossible to check Trump’s drive towards war, despite the nominal requirement for congressional approval. However, Congress will meet only in the first week of January next year. This gives Trump and his war party a long enough leash to devastate the Middle East.

But analysts also predict another scenario that could give Trump an off-ramp to end the war. This is likely to involve the US bombing or dropping the “Mother of All Bombs” on Iran’s Pickaxe Mountain, beneath which the Islamic Republic is alleged to have built a secret uranium-enrichment facility. After the strike, the US will declare that it has achieved its objectives and that the war is over.

But Iran has not agreed to this pre-arranged script. Instead, it has warned that no attack will go unpunished. Moreover, if Israel becomes involved in the attack, such a formula for an early end to the war will be doomed.

With the parties to the conflict showing greater inclination towards war than peace, global economic devastation is the likely outcome. More people could die from poverty and a lack of access to affordable medicine than from the wars in West Asia themselves.

Clinging to one’s pound of flesh during negotiations is unlikely to bring peace. The Iran war and the Houthi war have become increasingly interconnected. Peace in one could lead to peace in the other. Similarly, an escalation in one would spark an escalation in the other. Peace can come only through compromise, especially on the part of the United States, since it started the illegal war.

If war is what the United States is after, the bulk of Iran’s retaliation will be directed at the Gulf nations. If Iran attacks Saudi Arabia, accusing Riyadh of facilitating the attacks by allowing the US to use Saudi airspace and military bases, the Mecca Defence Pact will be activated, requiring Pakistan and Türkiye to defend Saudi Arabia. This could trigger a regional war. Should Pakistan become bogged down in the Iran war or the Houthi war, its defence posture against two other hostile neighbours, India and Afghanistan, would be compromised, especially if it becomes deeply involved in Saudi Arabia’s war against the Houthis.

When Muslims fight Muslims, the question that arises is whether they are spurning the Quran’s message of brotherhood and making it easier for Israel to achieve its expansionist goals.

In verses 9 and 10 of Chapter 49 of the Quran, God commands Muslims to make peace when believers fight one another: “The believers are but one brotherhood, so make peace between your brothers and fear God so that you may receive mercy.”

Unfortunately, there appears to be no Muslim leader in the Muslim world willing to act upon Islam’s message of peace and avert war.
