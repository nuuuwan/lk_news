Commonwealth Lawyers Association raises questions over Supreme Court proceedings

Colombo, Sep. 8 (Daily Mirror) - In a nine-page report, President of the Commonwealth Lawyers Association (CLA) Steven Thiru has raised several concerns over procedural fairness, transparency and the composition of the Supreme Court bench that heard petitions challenging the constitutionality of the proposed 22nd Amendment to the Constitution.

Thiru, who visited Sri Lanka at the invitation of the Bar Association of Sri Lanka (BASL), observed the proceedings before the Supreme Court on September 2 and subsequently issued a report highlighting matters which, he said, warranted careful scrutiny in the interests of judicial independence and public confidence in the administration of justice.

The proposed Constitutional amendment seeks to increase the retirement age of Supreme Court judges to 67 and Court of Appeal judges to 65.

In the case of the Chief Justice, the proposed amendment provides that retirement would occur on the earlier of reaching 67 years of age or completing six years from the date of appointment as Chief Justice.

A total of 67 petitions were filed against the proposed amendment, including petitions by the BASL, with the Attorney General named as the respondent. There were also 27 interveners opposing the petitions.

The petitions were heard on September 1 and 2 by a five-member bench headed by Chief Justice Preethi Padman Surasena. The other members of the bench were Justices Achala Wengappuli, Arjuna Obeyesekere, Sampath B. Abeykoon and Gihan Kulatunga.

The BASL had sought the recusal of the Chief Justice and requested that the petitions be heard by a full bench comprising all sitting Supreme Court judges. Both applications were dismissed by the five-member bench on September 1.

According to the report, no reasons were given for the dismissal other than that the Court had jurisdiction to hear the matter.

Thiru identified the recusal application as one of the principal issues of concern. The petitioners had argued that the Chief Justice could potentially be the first Supreme Court judge to benefit from the proposed amendment and, on that basis, sought his recusal.

While Thiru did not suggest that the Chief Justice was actually biased or that the recusal application was necessarily well founded, he stressed the importance of addressing such an application with sufficient care and transparency and providing justifiable reasons for accepting or rejecting it.

He also questioned the decision not to constitute a full bench to hear what he described as a matter of critical Constitutional importance.

Although the Chief Justice has authority concerning the constitution of benches, Thiru said the exercise of that authority should be consistent with Constitutional and legal principles and should not give an appearance of being arbitrary or irrational.

He noted that larger or full benches are not unusual in matters of exceptional Constitutional importance in other common law jurisdictions, citing the landmark Indian Supreme Court case of Kesavananda Bharati vs. State of Kerala as an example.

The CLA President also drew attention to the absence of several senior Supreme Court judges from the five-member bench. The four judges immediately senior to Justice Achala Wengappuli - Justices Yasantha Kodagoda, A.H.M.D. Nawaz, Shiran Gooneratne and Janak De Silva, were not included.

Thiru stressed that seniority alone does not determine the composition of a bench. However, given the Constitutional significance of the proceedings, he said the basis upon which the bench was constituted should be transparent and discernible in order to maintain public confidence.

Another concern related to the refusal to allow petitioners to make oral submissions in reply after submissions by the interveners and the respondent.

At the conclusion of the hearing on September 2, several counsel for the petitioners sought an opportunity to respond orally. The Chief Justice declined the request but permitted the petitioners to submit written replies by 3 p.m. on September 3.

Thiru observed that the opportunity to make an oral reply is commonly afforded in litigation as a matter of procedural fairness. He described the denial of an oral reply as unusual in the circumstances, particularly considering the complexity of the Constitutional issues and the extensive oral submissions made by the interveners and the respondent.

The proceedings were conducted within a particularly tight constitutional timeframe. Hearings continued from 10.30 a.m. until 10.45 p.m. on September 1 and from 10 a.m. until 5.30 p.m. on September 2. Under Article 121(2) of the Constitution, the Supreme Court is required to make its determination and deliver it by September 8.

Following the hearings, Thiru said he met Opposition Leader Sajith Premadasa and discussed the importance of maintaining public confidence in the independence and impartiality of the judiciary.

He also sought meetings with Justice and National Integration Minister Harshana Nanayakkara and the Secretary to the Ministry of Justice and National Integration, Ayesha Jinasena PC.

According to the report, the Minister was unable to meet him as he was not in Colombo, while no response was received from the Ministry Secretary.

Meanwhile, the BASL filed a motion before the Supreme Court on September 3 stating that it would not tender post-hearing written submissions, referring, among other matters, to the Court’s refusal to permit oral submissions in reply. The report also notes that a number of other petitioners were reportedly taking a similar position.

Thiru emphasised that his observations did not constitute a concluded opinion on the merits of the petitions or the issues ultimately to be decided by the Supreme Court.

He said the observations were intended to identify procedural matters that merit scrutiny in the interests of procedural fairness, transparency and public confidence in the administration of justice.

The CLA President stressed that, particularly in Constitutional matters involving judicial independence, public confidence depends not only on the outcome of a case but also on confidence in the process through which judicial decisions are reached, including the impartiality of judges, the composition of the bench and the opportunity given to parties to present their cases.
