The Government is preparing to award contracts worth a staggering Rs. 235.5 billion for the Kurunegala to Dambulla section of the Central Expressway, but a senior civil engineer has raised concerns that the tender conditions could result in too few contractors competing for the work, potentially putting billions of rupees of public money at risk.

According to the senior engineer, the fundamental issue is not whether the Road Development Authority (RDA) has followed procurement procedures, but whether the conditions set for the tender will actually create sufficient competition.

“The basic rule of public procurement is simple. The more qualified contractors competing for a project, the greater the pressure on them to offer competitive prices. If you have only a handful of contractors competing for a large number of packages, that pressure disappears,” the engineer told the Daily Mirror.

The RDA published the Invitation for Bids for Central Expressway Project Section 4 (CEP 4), Kurunegala to Dambulla, on July 30, covering 12 contract packages with a combined estimated value of Rs. 235.529 billion.

The bidding documents are available until August 25, with bids scheduled to close on August 28.

The engineer said the concerns become more significant when the CEP 4 criteria are compared with the recently tendered CEP 3 Section 2, where only six local contractors reportedly qualified for seven packages.

“What happened with CEP 3 should be a warning. There were seven packages but only six qualified contractors. That means the number of contractors was already smaller than the number of contracts available. When you then increase the value of the project to Rs. 235 billion and introduce criteria that are, in several respects, more demanding, the obvious question is how many contractors will actually qualify,” the engineer said.

Under CEP 3 Section 2, the seven packages had a combined estimated value of around Rs. 93.6 billion and required a minimum CIDA grade of CS1.

The senior engineer pointed out that CEP 4 is significantly larger, with 12 packages worth around Rs. 235.5 billion.

However, six of the CEP 4 packages require a CS2 grade, while the remaining six require CS1 together with additional qualifications relating to bridges and, in some cases, buildings.

The prior similar-contract value required under CEP 4 also ranges from Rs. 7.1 billion to Rs. 9.2 billion, compared to around Rs. 5.8 billion to Rs. 7.7 billion under CEP 3 Section 2.

Annual turnover requirements for CEP 4 range from Rs. 4.9 billion to Rs. 9 billion, while financial resource requirements range from Rs. 670 million to Rs. 1.25 billion, depending on the package.

“If six contractors were able to qualify for CEP 3 under criteria that were less demanding in several respects, how many contractors will qualify for CEP 4? That is something the RDA should be able to answer publicly before the bids are opened,” he said.

The engineer stressed that the concern was not necessarily that the RDA had violated procurement rules.

“I am not saying that the RDA has broken the procurement rules. That is not the point. You can follow every procedure correctly and still end up with a tender that does not provide sufficient competition. Procedure and value for money are two different things,” he said.

According to him, the ultimate test of a tender should be whether enough qualified companies are able to compete.

“The purpose of procurement is not simply to tick boxes and say the procedure was followed. The purpose is to get the best possible value for the taxpayer. If you have 12 packages and only six companies qualify, you have a fundamental competition

He argued that contractors competing against one another would normally have a stronger incentive to sharpen their prices, potentially bringing bids closer to or below the Engineer’s Estimate.

Conversely, a limited field of bidders could reduce that pressure.

“When contractors know there are only a few companies capable of bidding, the competitive pressure is naturally reduced. On a Rs. 235 billion project, even a relatively small difference between the Engineer’s Estimate and the final contract prices can translate into billions of rupees,” the engineer said.

He questioned whether opening the tender to international companies, particularly through partnerships with local contractors, could widen the pool of bidders.

“Why not allow qualified international contractors to compete? Why not allow local companies to form joint ventures with experienced foreign contractors? If the objective is genuine competition, the Government should be looking at every legitimate avenue to increase the number of qualified bidders,” he said.

He added that international participation could also allow local companies that may not independently meet certain experience requirements to participate alongside experienced international construction companies.

“The most important question is very simple: when the RDA prepared these criteria, did it know how many companies in Sri Lanka could actually meet them? If the answer is six, then why are we designing a Rs. 235 billion tender with 12 packages around a pool of six contractors?” he questioned.

He said the RDA should also explain what steps it had taken to broaden participation.

Among the questions that should be answered, he said, were how many contractors the RDA expects to qualify, why foreign contractors have been excluded, why local–foreign joint ventures have not been permitted and what measures have been taken to increase competition.

With the sale of bidding documents continuing until August 25 and bids closing on August 28, the engineer said there was still an opportunity for the authorities to review the situation.

“There is still time. Before the tender closes, the Government can look at whether the criteria are producing the level of competition required for a project of this magnitude. This is public money. There should be no hesitation in asking whether we are getting enough bidders and the best possible price,” he said.

He stressed that restricting participation could be justified where a project genuinely required highly specialised expertise or capacity.

However, the impact of those restrictions on competition should also be assessed.

“There is nothing wrong with having high standards. The Government must ensure that companies undertaking a project of this scale are capable. But there is a difference between setting high standards for quality and setting criteria that leave only a handful of companies capable of bidding,”

The engineer said the Government’s stated commitment to prudent public financial management would ultimately be tested by major infrastructure projects such as CEP 4.

“Rs. 235.5 billion is not a small amount of money. Every percentage point matters. Every rupee saved through genuine competition is a rupee that can be used elsewhere. The Government therefore owes the public a clear explanation of how these tender conditions will protect the taxpayer,” he said.

As the CEP 4 tender moves towards its August 28 closing date, the central question remains whether the 12 packages will attract a sufficiently broad field of qualified contractors.

For a project costing Rs. 235.5 billion, the stakes are simply too high for competition to become an afterthought.
