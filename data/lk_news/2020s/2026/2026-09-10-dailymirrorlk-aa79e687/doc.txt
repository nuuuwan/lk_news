Extreme weather puts Sri Lanka’s unique wildlife and ecosystems at risk

Colombo, Sep. 10 (Daily Mirror) - Sri Lanka’s unique wildlife, plants and ecosystems are facing growing pressure from extreme weather, with droughts, floods, landslides, rising temperatures and changing rainfall patterns posing serious challenges to species that depend on fragile and limited habitats.

Research Professor D.S.A. Wijesundara of the National Institute of Fundamental Studies highlighted these concerns in a presentation titled “Impact of Extreme Weather Events on Sri Lanka’s Biodiversity and Ecosystems.”

The report was recently presented to the Parliamentary Climate Causes Expert Committee, highlighting the growing impact of extreme weather events and climate-related changes on Sri Lanka’s biodiversity and ecosystems.

Sri Lanka is recognised for its rich biodiversity and high level of endemism. Its forests, wetlands, mangroves, coral reefs and other ecosystems provide habitats for many species, including plants and animals found nowhere else in the world.

However, changing climate conditions are putting increasing pressure on these natural systems. The country is experiencing rising temperatures, greater changes in rainfall, longer dry periods and more intense rainfall events.

Extreme weather events such as droughts, floods, landslides, heat waves and cyclones can affect ecosystems in different ways. El Niño events can also change monsoon rainfall patterns, increasing the risk of drought and heat stress and affecting the normal functioning of ecosystems.

One of the major concerns is the vulnerability of species that are found only in very small geographical areas. Prof. Wijesundara said that landslides and floods can have a serious effect on such point endemic species, as damage to a small habitat can directly affect an entire local population.

The country’s Central Highlands are particularly important, with 689 endemic species recorded in the identified endemic-zone assessment. Of these, 106 are unique endemic species, representing 77% of Sri Lanka’s total endemics in the assessment.

The South West Wet Zone has 527 endemic species, including 46 unique endemic species, while the Northern Highlands have 426 endemic species, including 28 unique endemic species. Other identified endemic zones include the Eastern Highlands, Ritigala, Dolu Kanda, Yala, Wilpattu and Jaffna.

Several smaller areas are also important centres of endemic biodiversity. Adam’s Peak has 510 endemic species, including 20 unique endemic species, while Sinharaja has 406, Horton Plains 302, Knuckles 294 and Kandy 203. These areas could face increasing pressure if extreme weather damages or changes their natural habitats.

Sri Lanka’s plant biodiversity is also facing serious challenges. According to the figures presented by Prof. Wijesundara, nearly 48% of the country’s flowering plants are threatened, while about 58% of fern species face a risk of extinction.

The situation is more serious among endemic plants, with 60% of endemic flowering plant species considered threatened and 66% of endemic fern species under threat.

The assessment also records two species as extinct, while another two survive only in cultivation and are extinct in the wild. A further 128 species are considered probably extinct, including 54 endemic species.

Drought can reduce flowering and fruiting and increase the death of young plants, while floods and landslides can destroy habitats, remove soil and increase sediment in rivers.

Montane forests are particularly sensitive to changes in moisture and temperature. Moisture stress, rising temperatures and habitat degradation can contribute to forest decline and dieback.

Climate-related damage can also create conditions that allow invasive species to spread more easily. Invasive alien species were introduced into Sri Lanka mainly during the colonial period from 1505 to 1948, while global trade, transportation and other human activities have contributed to their spread.

Prof. Wijesundara referred to Totapolakande Forest, where significant tree mortality and forest dieback have been observed.

Changing environmental conditions can also cause some native species to spread aggressively. The presentation highlighted Ochlandra stridula, which has affected forest regeneration in the Indikada Mukalana and Meethirigala Forest Reserves.

Some Strobilanthes species have become aggressive in disturbed montane areas, while Kuruna densifolia has shown rapid spread in Horton Plains National Park.

The effects of extreme weather are already visible in Sri Lanka’s natural environments. The current drought has placed additional pressure on wildlife, particularly in dry-zone areas. In Yala National Park, authorities have had to provide water to wildlife as natural water sources shrink. The most heavily visited areas of the park are also being temporarily closed to give animals easier access to limited water sources.

The drought is also affecting communities, agriculture and water supplies in several parts of the country, with some areas experiencing severe rainfall shortages and drying wells, rivers and tanks.

These developments show how climate-related pressures can affect both ecosystems and the people who depend on them.

While drought threatens water availability, heavy rainfall can bring another set of risks. Floods and landslides can destroy habitats, cause soil erosion and increase sedimentation in rivers. For species restricted to small areas, the loss of even part of their habitat can have serious consequences.

Sri Lanka experienced widespread floods and landslides during November and December 2025, affecting large areas of the country.

Freshwater and marine ecosystems are also vulnerable. Changes in river flows can affect fish and other aquatic species, while wetland degradation, coral bleaching, sea-level rise and coastal erosion threaten coastal and marine ecosystems.

Pollinators such as bees and butterflies can also be affected by changing environmental conditions, potentially reducing important pollination services.

The loss or weakening of ecosystems is not only a concern for conservation. Healthy ecosystems provide clean water, store carbon, support pollination, contribute to agriculture and fisheries, and support tourism and local livelihoods.

Prof. Wijesundara warned that while some generalist species may adapt or expand under changing conditions, species with specialised habitat requirements and limited ranges may face greater difficulties.

This makes the protection of biodiversity an important part of climate adaptation.

Prof. Wijesundara stressed the need for stronger action to protect Sri Lanka’s natural ecosystems as climate pressures increase. Among the measures highlighted are ecosystem restoration, expansion and strengthening of protected areas, ecosystem-based adaptation, improved monitoring and climate studies, and regular assessments of species at risk.

He also stressed the need to include biodiversity protection in national policies, establish effective early warning systems and increase investment in scientific research.

The central message from the presentation was that biodiversity conservation should not be treated separately from climate adaptation.

Protecting forests, wetlands, rivers, coastal ecosystems and the species that depend on them can help reduce the long-term effects of climate change while protecting the natural resources on which Sri Lankan communities depend.
