Temporary ETA waiver needs proof travellers can show at check-in

When an official travel website goes down, the passenger at the airline counter becomes the final test of government communication. Sri Lanka’s temporary Electronic Travel Authorisation arrangement is a sensible response to an unavailable portal. It will protect visitors and businesses only if the instruction given to airlines reaches the person checking a passport at an overseas airport.

Daily FT reported on 25 November that the Immigration and Emigration Department had allowed travellers from most eligible countries to board without obtaining prior ETA approval while its online system undergoes maintenance. A circular dated 23 November directs airlines and ground handlers to accommodate covered passengers. Eligible travellers may receive an ETA or Visit Visa on arrival at Bandaranaike International Airport or another relevant port of entry. The report also noted a significant unresolved question: announcements did not clarify whether the existing free tourist ETA arrangement for citizens of 40 countries applies to authorisations issued on arrival during the interruption.

That uncertainty deserves a precise answer, but the challenge begins before arrival. A traveller may encounter an airline agent who has not seen the circular, a website still displaying old instructions or a booking system that insists on an authorisation number. The passenger cannot reasonably be expected to negotiate immigration policy at a departure gate. A public rule has little value if the people responsible for applying it see different versions.

The department could issue a dated, downloadable travel notice specifically for passengers and carriers. It should state the start of the temporary measure, the eligible nationalities or clear eligibility criteria, what documents must still be carried, where arrival permission is processed and which questions remain subject to normal immigration discretion. The notice should avoid promising that every traveller will be admitted merely because the advance ETA is waived. Eligibility to board and permission to enter are different decisions.

A separate one-page operational instruction for airline agents could use the same version number and provide a verified official route for checking unusual cases. Government authorities, aviation partners and Sri Lankan diplomatic missions could link to a single current notice instead of maintaining several slightly different copies. If the policy changes, an archived version and effective time would help settle disputes without leaving travellers to rely on a screenshot.

Fee clarity is part of the consumer problem. If free tourist ETAs normally apply to specified nationalities but temporary arrival processing is treated differently, the government should say so before travellers leave home. Any applicable fee, method of payment and official receipt process should be published. It is better to explain an unsettled point openly than to let private travel companies present conflicting guesses as official rules.

The system also needs a correction route. An eligible traveller turned away at check-in because an airline’s records are outdated should have a way to report the incident, including the flight, date and nature of the problem, without sharing passport images publicly. Airlines should be able to ask for urgent clarification through recognised operational channels. A useful complaint service is one that can fix the rule’s application, not merely apologise after a holiday has been lost.

Tourism operators have a role to play without becoming immigration advisers. Hotels and tour companies can direct guests to the authoritative notice, remind them that ordinary passport and entry requirements still apply and avoid charging for unofficial solutions. They should not be expected to guarantee an immigration outcome that only the competent authority can decide.

Once the portal returns, a brief service review could ask how long it was unavailable, how quickly the carrier instruction reached participating airlines, which traveller questions recurred and what was changed to prevent confusion next time. It need not expose security details about the system. Such a review would be especially useful for a country whose tourism and business travel depend on predictable entry procedures.

Jesus’ teaching about truthful speech is a reminder that a promise should be dependable in the ordinary situations where people act upon it. Here, good public service means more than announcing flexibility. It means ensuring the check-in desk has the same clear answer as the ministry.

Sri Lanka’s temporary waiver can spare visitors unnecessary disruption. The strongest next step is a current public notice that a passenger can show, a carrier can verify and the authorities can promptly correct. That is the difference between a good policy and a journey that actually works.
