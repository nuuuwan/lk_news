Guardians of the silent kingdom: Sri Lanka’s wild soul and urgency of protection

In the half-light before sunrise, when the land still belongs to shadows and silence, Sri Lanka’s wilderness begins its ancient ritual of awakening. A solitary leopard melts into the golden grasses of Yala National Park. A herd of elephants moves like living monuments through the forest corridors.

High above, an eagle rides invisible currents of air, surveying a world that has existed long before humanity learned to name it.

This is the silent kingdom. A realm without borders, without politics, without human ambition. Yet it is a kingdom increasingly under threat.

As Sri Lanka joins the global community in observing World Wildlife Day 2026, the occasion arrives not merely as a symbolic event, but as a solemn reminder of what is at stake. Declared by the United Nations, World Wildlife Day is a global call to protect the extraordinary diversity of life that sustains our planet—and ourselves.

Sri Lanka, an island celebrated for its astonishing biodiversity, stands at a critical crossroads. Within its forests, wetlands, and oceans lives a concentration of species found nowhere else on Earth. From elusive amphibians hidden beneath rainforest leaves to mighty whales roaming the surrounding seas, the island’s wildlife forms a living tapestry of evolutionary wonder.

Speaking to The Island, Hasini Sarathchandra, Head of Communications at the Department of Wildlife Conservation, emphasised the deeper meaning behind World Wildlife Day and the urgency of conservation.

“Wildlife is not separate from us. It is part of the same ecological system that sustains human life,” she said. “World Wildlife Day reminds us that every species plays a role in maintaining balance. When we lose wildlife, we lose part of the natural system that supports our own survival.”

Her words echo with quiet conviction, shaped by years of engagement with Sri Lanka’s natural heritage and the mounting pressures it faces.

The origins of World Wildlife Day are rooted in a historic agreement signed on 03 March, 1973—the CITES convention, formally known as the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora. This landmark treaty recognised the urgent need to regulate wildlife trade and prevent species from being driven to extinction by human exploitation.

Sri Lanka became a signatory to the convention in 1978, affirming its commitment to conservation and international cooperation.

Since then, the Department of Wildlife Conservation has served as the guardian of the island’s wild spaces. Its officers patrol remote forests, monitor wildlife populations, combat illegal trade, and work tirelessly to protect fragile ecosystems.

Sri Lanka’s wildlife faces a complex web of threats—habitat loss, climate change, illegal poaching, pollution, and human encroachment. Expanding settlements and infrastructure have fragmented habitats that animals have used for centuries. Climate shifts alter rainfall patterns, affecting food availability and breeding cycles. Illegal wildlife trade continues to target vulnerable species.

Yet despite these pressures, Sri Lanka remains one of the most biologically rich nations on Earth.

The island is home to iconic species, such as the Sri Lankan elephant, the Sri Lankan leopard, and countless endemic birds, reptiles, and amphibians. Many of these species exist nowhere else, making their survival a global responsibility.

Sarathchandra stressed the importance of public awareness and participation in conservation.

“Conservation cannot be achieved by government institutions alone,” she said. “It requires the support of communities, the involvement of young people, and a national commitment to protecting our natural heritage. Every citizen has a role to play.”

Her message reflects a fundamental truth: conservation is not only a scientific or legal effort—it is a cultural and moral one.

This year’s World Wildlife Day theme, “Medicinal and Aromatic Plants: Conserving Health, Heritage and Livelihoods,” highlights the profound connection between biodiversity and human wellbeing. Many of Sri Lanka’s traditional medicinal systems rely on plants found in forests and natural ecosystems. These plants support healthcare, livelihoods, and cultural traditions that have endured for centuries.

However, overharvesting, habitat destruction, and illegal trade threaten these vital resources.

“Medicinal plants are an important part of our heritage,” Sarathchandra noted. “Protecting them ensures not only ecological balance, but also the continuation of traditional knowledge and sustainable livelihoods.”

Across Sri Lanka, a series of events and educational programmes are being organised to mark World Wildlife Day. Schools, conservation groups, and government agencies are working together to raise awareness and inspire a deeper appreciation for wildlife.

In classrooms, children learn about endangered species. In communities, conservationists share knowledge about coexistence. In protected areas, Wildlife officers continue their vigilant work.

The choice of Yala National Park as the focal point for national celebrations carries a symbolic significance. Yala is more than a tourist destination—it is a living sanctuary where ecosystems function in their natural harmony.

Here, predators and prey maintain the balance of life. Forests protect watersheds. Wetlands support birds and aquatic species. Each component contributes to the stability of the whole.

Standing within such a landscape, one cannot escape the profound realisation that wildlife conservation is ultimately about preserving life’s interconnected web.

Sri Lanka’s conservation journey has achieved notable successes. Protected areas cover significant portions of the island, and legal frameworks, such as the Fauna and Flora Protection Ordinance, provide strong safeguards. Dedicated Wildlife officers risk their lives to protect animals from poachers and illegal traders.

Human–wildlife conflict remains a serious issue, particularly in rural areas where elephants and humans compete for land and resources. Climate change introduces new uncertainties, altering ecosystems in ways that scientists are still striving to understand.

Illegal wildlife trade, driven by global demand, continues to threaten vulnerable species.

Sarathchandra emphasised that awareness and education remain key tools in addressing these challenges.

“When people understand the value of wildlife, they are more likely to protect it,” she said. “Education helps build a culture of conservation that can last for generations.”

Hope that future generations will inherit forests filled with birdsong. Hope that elephants will continue their ancient migrations. Hope that leopards will still roam free beneath Sri Lanka’s starlit skies.

World Wildlife Day serves as a reminder that conservation is not a distant or abstract concept. It is a daily responsibility shared by all.

Every forest protected, every illegal activity prevented, every young person inspired to care about nature, contributes to a larger effort.

Sri Lanka’s wildlife is more than a collection of species. It is part of the nation’s identity. It shapes landscapes, supports ecosystems, and enriches culture.

In the quiet hours of dawn, when the wilderness awakens once more, the silent kingdom continues its ancient rhythm. It asks for nothing, demands nothing.

As Sri Lanka marks World Wildlife Day 2026, the message is clear and urgent: the time to protect wildlife is now.

For in safeguarding the wild, we safeguard the future—not only of animals, but of humanity itself.
