Galle Fort emerges as top destination for foreign tourists as travel patterns shift

Colonial ramparts capture 54% of visitor itineraries in latest SLTDA survey, displacing Colombo and Kandy

Yala nearly doubles its share to 32.8% as safari demand concentrates into a single park

Southern coastal belt now anchors itineraries, with beach towns holding visitors twice as long as heritage sites

Galle Fort has overtaken the long-standing cultural and urban fixtures to become Sri Lanka’s most visited tourist attraction, capturing over 54 percent of foreign traveller itineraries, according to the latest survey by the Sri Lanka Tourism Development Authority (SLTDA).

The survey, covering 11,731 departing tourists over the one-year period to June 2025, marks a decisive repositioning in Sri Lanka’s traditional destination hierarchy.

Galle Fort drew 6,340 respondents (54.0 percent), closely followed by Ella and Sigiriya Rock Fortress, both tied at 53.1 percent. In doing so, the southern colonial fortress displaced the commercial capital Colombo and Kandy’s Temple of the Tooth, which had historically formed the twin anchors of the inbound tourist routes.

In the previous 2018/2019 survey, Colombo held the top spot at 68.3 percent, followed by the Temple of the Tooth at 64.9 percent.

In the latest findings, Colombo slipped to the fifth place, with 46.2 percent, while the Temple of the Tooth fell to the fourth, with 51.3 percent. By contrast, Galle Fort expanded its penetration from 48.9 percent, reflecting the evolving preferences among the international holidaymakers who increasingly favour visual appeal and walkable historic quarters over the urban transit hubs.

The SLTDA’s analysis attributed the ascendance of Galle Fort, alongside Ella and Sigiriya, to a structural pivot toward experiential and “Instagram-worthy” travel.

The modern travellers, heavily dominated by younger and independent cohorts, prioritise photogenic, accessible locations with strong social media resonance, where Galle’s ocean-facing ramparts, preserved colonial streetscapes and boutique cafes offer compelling backdrops.

This preference has elevated the coastal fortress above the older Cultural Triangle landmarks such as Anuradhapura and Polonnaruwa, which saw their visitor shares drop to 14.2 percent and 13.8 percent, respectively.

Galle Fort also emerged as a rare bright spot for visitor retention within the cultural network. It registered 772 repeat visitors, the highest count recorded at any surveyed site, surpassing Kandy at 604 and Sigiriya at 539. While the first-time travellers still formed the vast majority of its arrivals at 5,567, the fortress demonstrated a stronger capacity to pull returning tourists back into the Southern province.

Despite its top ranking, Galle Fort faces the same turnover bottleneck prevalent across Sri Lanka’s heritage circuit. The average duration of stay at the fortress was recorded at 1.63 days, indicating that most visitors continue to treat the landmark as a brief excursion or single-night stopover rather than an extended base.

The SLTDA report highlighted that converting this traffic into higher economic value will depend on linking Galle Fort with the surrounding southern hubs.

The neighbouring coastal destinations already command significantly longer stays, led by surfing centres such as Weligama at 3.15 days, Hikkaduwa at 2.35 days and Mirissa at 2.14 days.

Packaging Galle Fort alongside the regional marine and culinary offerings into integrated multi-day circuits could help stretch visitor stays to five or seven days, unlocking higher spending across local hospitality and retail without overburdening the historic ramparts.

That southern belt has strengthened around the fortress. Mirissa lifted its share to 34.4 percent, from 32.6 percent, Unawatuna to 17.1 percent, from 14.8 percent and Bentota to 16.2 percent, from 13.1 percent, placing four of the country’s 10 most visited destinations within roughly 60 kilometres of Galle.

The gains were not uniform, however, with Hikkaduwa slipping to 18.9 percent, from 21.0 percent and Weligama to 15.7 percent, from 17.6 percent, suggesting that traffic is redistributing between resort towns on the same stretch of coast rather than expanding the coast as a whole.

The retention data underscores why the coastal linkage matters. The five principal southern beach towns average 2.43 days against 1.72 days across the five most visited cultural and urban sites, a 41 percent advantage in nights held.

Negombo stands apart from that pattern, easing to 25.8 percent, from 27.1 percent on an average stay of 1.60 days, the shortest of any coastal destination surveyed and consistent with its function as an airport transit point rather than a holiday base.

Wildlife has been the other engine of the shift. The Yala National Park nearly doubled its penetration to 32.8 percent, from 17.5 percent, the sharpest proportional gain recorded in the survey, lifting it into the top 10 alongside Dambulla and Mirissa.

The gain came as the rival parks lost ground, with Udawalawe falling to 17.6 percent, from 22.2 percent, Minneriya to 3.4 percent, from 6.5 percent and Kaudulla to 3.5 percent, from 4.6 percent, pointing to a concentration of safari demand into a single park rather than a broad growth in wildlife tourism.

Wilpattu was the exception, almost tripling its share to 5.9 percent, from 2.0 percent. Yala nonetheless holds visitors just 1.31 days and the report flags overcrowding at the park among the sustainability concerns raised by the eco-conscious travellers.

The underlying activity mix confirms the reorientation. Beach and sea pursuits ranked first at 67 percent of tourists, wildlife safaris second at 47 percent and sightseeing excursions third at 32 percent, while hiking and trekking drew 22 percent, culinary tourism 21 percent and surfing 20 percent, the last placing ahead of visits to religious sites at 15 percent.

Snorkelling registered 7 percent, watersports 4 percent, whale and dolphin watching 3 percent and scuba and shipwreck diving 2 percent.

The east and north remain outside the reordered circuit, despite holding the visitors longest. Arugam Bay records the longest average stay in the country, at 3.88 days, yet its share has fallen to 7.7 percent, from 11.4 percent, while Jaffna holds visitors 3.61 days on a 3.6 percent share, Passikudah 2.83 days on 2.8 percent and Trincomalee 2.62 days on 9.7 percent. Those four average 3.24 days against 1.72 days across the five most visited sites, leaving the destinations best placed to lift length of stay carrying the least traffic.
