The Eighth Colombo International Maritime and Logistics Conference (CIMLC) to be held from September 9 to 11 is designed to enhance Sri Lanka’s status as a major maritime hub in the Indo-Pacific region.

It will bring together global maritime leaders, regional policymakers, maritime sector professionals and captains of trade and industry to discuss new horizons and challenges facing the global shipping community.

This year’s conference will have Sri Lanka as its prime focus, given the fact that the island nation is South Asia’s leading hub for transhipment and logistics.

Colombo is well-placed strategically. Its importance derives from its position as the primary transhipment hub for the Indian subcontinent. India-the world’s fifth-largest economy with 1.4 billion people- generates enormous container trade but has limited deep-water transhipment infrastructure on its own coastline. An estimated 30% of Indian containerised trade transhipment takes place through Colombo, particularly cargo from India’s southern and eastern ports destined for mainline services on the Asia-Europe and transpacific routes.

Colombo port operates several major container terminals: Colombo International Container Terminal (CICT), South Asia Gateway Terminals (SAGT), and the Sri Lanka Ports Authority (SLPA) terminals, with the new Colombo West International Terminal (CWIT) and Colombo East Container Terminal (CECT) expanding capacity. The total annual throughput exceeds 7.7 million TEUs, with CICT alone handling over 3 million TEUs.

CICT and CWIT are deep-water facilities, with CWIT featuring advanced automation-eight automated ship-to-shore cranes and 18 automated yard cranes. Automation is highest at CWIT, while other terminals use a mix of manual and semi-automated systems.

Containers from Indian ports (Nhava Sheva, Chennai, Mundra, Visakhapatnam, Kolkata) are consolidated at Colombo for loading onto mainline Asia-Europe vessels transiting to the Suez Canal and European ports. Feeder services connect Colombo to ports across India, Bangladesh, Pakistan (Karachi), Maldives, and Myanmar.

The majority of Colombo’s container traffic is relay cargo with no connection to the Sri Lankan domestic market. This cargo encompasses the full spectrum of containerized trade-consumer goods, electronics, garments, machinery, chemicals, and agricultural products.

International businesses have now recognised Sri Lanka’s ability to establish world-class distribution and fulfilment centres that can provide value-added services for all logistics providers. Fulfilment centres are third-party service providers which process and ship products directly to customers after managing the seller’s inventory and storing it. These facilities utilise advanced technology to store inventory and efficiently handle pick, pack, and ship while providing real-time inventory management, reducing operating and delivery costs, and also saving physical space for sellers.

The 2026 edition of the CIMLC centres on the theme “Building a World Around Us,” emphasising Sri Lanka’s role as a critical hub in global supply chains. With India projected to grow fast and consistently, the conference will serve as a platform for shipping and logistics companies and investors to explore “regional investment” dynamics and connectivity with backward integration, along with new policies such as public -private partnerships in developing terminals and ports in Sri Lanka.

Discussions are expected to highlight Sri Lanka’s massive port capacity increase. The aim is to secure nearly 100% new container capacity by the end of 2027 and double the same by 2040, both in Colombo and Hambantota. This should open up avenues for international investments in container terminals with shipping lines and other terminal operators.

Opportunities to secure efficient logistics services and multimodal transport connectivity options will be a key theme for discussions. The conference will evaluate opportunities and application of the latest technologies to all logistics-related industries. International best practices will be discussed, including AI and cybersecurity.

Development agencies, such as the World Bank, ADB, JICA, KOICA, USAID, EU, and US State Department, will speak on the current and future developments in the region and Sri Lanka’s prospects for infrastructure and project development.

This conference will give alternative options for the stability of inventory management and shipping services to provide uninterrupted services during unforeseen geopolitical and environmental disruptions.

In this context, the participation of the United States Federal Maritime Commission (FMC) Chairperson Laura DiBella and FMC Commissioner Robert J. Harvey assumes significance.

The FMC is an independent US body meant to ensure a competitive and reliable international ocean transportation supply system that protects the US public from unfair and deceptive practices.

The FMC ensures competitive and efficient ocean transportation services for the shipping public by reviewing and monitoring agreements among ocean common carriers and marine terminal operators (MTOs) serving US maritime trade to ensure that they do not cause substantial increases in transportation costs or decreases in transportation services.

The FMC maintains and reviews confidentially filed service contracts to guard against detrimental effects to shipping. It provides a forum for exporters, importers, and other members of the shipping public to obtain relief from ocean shipping practices or disputes that impede the flow of commerce.

The FMC ensures that common carriers’ tariff rates and charges are published in automated tariff systems and electronically available to the public. It monitors rates, charges, and rules of government-owned or controlled carriers to ensure they are just and reasonable.

It takes action to address unfavourable conditions caused by foreign governments or business practices in US-foreign shipping trades.

No port can grow in isolation in this era of global trade and interdependence. Therefore, Colombo would only gain if South Asian ports also improve in facilities and efficiency. The proceedings at the Colombo conference will discuss this aspect also.

According to the World Bank, South Asian ports have improved over the years, but still do not match world standards. The region is still a small player in world trade, partly because shipping a container into or out of South Asia is relatively expensive and slow. The region is still a minor player in global container port traffic, with a market share that grew from 2.1 percent in 2000 to 2.9 percent in 2013.

South Asia has reduced the gap with East Asia, but some ports in India and Bangladesh take more than three times longer to turn around a container ship than the world’s most competitive ports – Hong Kong, Shanghai and Singapore.

The World Bank says that South Asian ports must encourage private sector participation within a well-regulated and administered landlord port model; must strengthen the governance of port authority boards; and promote competition between and within ports, in part through transparent and competitive bidding.

Tariffs and terminal handling charges at most large South Asian container ports are lower than those at ports such as Dubai, Salalah in Oman and Singapore. But indirect costs associated with delays, loss of markets and customer confidence have had a big effect on customer choices.

However, the World Bank found that some ports-specifically Colombo, Mundra and Jawaharlal Nehru Port in India and Port Qasim in Pakistan-improved the use of their facilities in the decade after 2000, while India’s Mumbai and Tuticorin fell behind. Chittagong (Bangladesh) and Kolkata (India) had the longest vessel turnaround times in the region.

The World Bank recommends more private sector participation within a well-regulated and administered landlord port model. In this arrangement, which has been increasingly adopted, the port authority acts as a regulatory body and landlord while private companies operate the port, provide the services and employ the workers.

Improvements in the governance of port authorities, with a more transparent appointments process and independent board members are needed.

A competitive environment must be fostered between ports. The Bank recommends stimulating competition at the initial concession stage – through open bidding – and through port policy objectives that introduce new operators as the port expands. Governments should also encourage competition between ports to attract business within the country, as has happened in northwest India.

The Bank also states these reforms would be most effective if carried out together, rather than piecemeal.
