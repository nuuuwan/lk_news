External deficits ease from April’s peak, but remain sharply wider than a year ago

Trade and current account deficits narrow for a second straight month in June, easing from April’s record highs

Fuel import costs, which surged after global oil prices climbed on escalation of the ME conflict, have driven the moderation

Year-on-year and cumulative H1 deficits remain substantially wider than a year earlier, pointing to a slower pace of deterioration rather than a reversal

Sri Lanka’s current account and trade deficits narrowed for a second consecutive month in June 2026, easing off the peaks recorded in April, even as both measures remained substantially wider than a year earlier - underscoring that the external sector’s improvement is a moderation in the pace of deterioration rather than a reversal of the underlying pressure, according to the Central Bank of Sri Lanka’s (CBSL) External Sector Performance report for June.

The trade deficit has narrowed in each of the past two months, from a record US$ 1.38 billion in April — the widest monthly trade gap since CBSL records began in 2002 — to roughly US$ 1.0 billion in May and US$ 828.5 million in June.

The current account deficit followed the same trajectory, easing from US$ 525.2 million in April to US$ 194.5 million in May and US$ 148.7 million in June. Fuel import costs, the single largest driver of April’s blowout, have led the moderation: fuel expenditure fell from US$ 886 million in April to US$ 536 million in May and US$ 465 million in June, even as it remained 40.2 percent higher than a year earlier.

Global oil prices began climbing sharply after the Middle East conflict escalated in late February 2026, and it was this spike, feeding through into Sri Lanka’s import bill with a lag, that produced April’s outsized fuel bill and, in turn, the record monthly trade gap.

Set against June 2025, however, the picture looks considerably worse. The current account swung from a US$ 148.7 million surplus in June last year to a near-identical deficit this June, while the trade deficit widened by 53.3 percent year-on-year, as goods imports grew 17.3 percent against export growth of just 0.2 percent.

The cumulative picture for the first half of the year tells the same story: the current account moved from a US$ 1.44 billion surplus in January-June 2025 to a US$ 245.4 million deficit in the same period this year, a swing of close to US$ 1.7 billion, while the cumulative trade deficit widened by 67.9 percent to US$ 5.49 billion, from US$ 3.27 billion a year earlier — already approaching the roughly US$ 6 billion trade gap recorded for the whole of 2024.

The deceleration, therefore, reflects fuel prices retreating from their April spike rather than a broader easing of the pressures the Central Bank has attributed to the Middle East conflict since it escalated in late February.

Those pressures are still visible elsewhere in the external accounts. The services surplus — traditionally a cushion against the trade gap — contracted by 33.8 percent year-on-year in June and by 22.4 percent  cumulatively in H1, as outbound travel spending by Sri Lankans rose 70.4 percent in June and 82.7 percent for the half-year, outpacing inbound receipts. Tourist arrivals fell 9.9 percent year-on-year in June to 124,551, with earnings down 10.8 percent to US$ 151.1 million, extending an 11.8 percent cumulative decline in H1 tourism receipts.

Terms of trade also deteriorated further in June, as import prices continued to rise faster than export prices.

Two factors have partly offset the widening: workers’ remittances rose 23.2 percent year-on-year in H1 to US$ 4.6 billion, though the Central Bank notes this may include one-off transfers linked to Cyclone Ditwah, which could overstate the underlying trend; and the primary income account deficit narrowed by 11.4 percent to US$ 894.5 million in H1, from US$ 1,009 million a year earlier.

Gross official reserves, which include the swap facility with the People’s Bank of China, stood at US$ 6.5 billion at end-June, covering 3.2 months of imports. The rupee had depreciated 7.8 percent against the US dollar on a year-to-date basis by end-July, closing at Rs. 336.05, though the Central Bank says the pace of depreciation has moderated following recently implemented monetary, fiscal and macroprudential measures.

A 27.1 percent month-on-month fall in vehicle import expenditure in June, to US$ 182 million, offers a further early sign of import compression, though it will take several more months of data to establish whether this marks a genuine turning point or simply a pause before the conflict’s effects reassert themselves.
