Controversy over extension of judges’ retirement age: Rage Over Age: the Need for Comprehensive Reform

The doctrine of separation of powers in democratic societies operates in a constitutional ecosystem of checks and balances of one another’s power, however less ideal the ecosystem is.

In some constitutional or legal systems, there is a glaring absence of a built-in institutional mechanism or avenue for ‘judging judges’ in the higher judiciary

In Sri Lanka, parties in power often treat weakening the judiciary as an easy route to sustained, smoother governance. Of late, an aspersion has arisen as to whether the NPP is trying this tactic using the retirement age of judges.

Attempts at weakening the judiciary, especially since the 1970s, have followed a clear set of interests and priorities underpinning the agendas of governing parties. It is ironic, however, that they were often clothed in salutary principles.

There is also a speculation whether the current attempt represents a larger move, of which weakening the judiciary remains only a part. The government has failed to cogently articulate rational justification or an objective need for the change it seeks, thereby providing grist to the rumour mill. No rationale has been set out in a policy/position paper, amplified by wider consultations.

In fairness to the judiciary, no evidence suggests that a need for increasing the age of retirement has been raised by it although it is perhaps not the judiciary’s call to make.

Between JR Jayawardena’s rendering of the Supreme Court judges “jobless” overnight, using the technicality or requirement of swearing-in following the adoption of the Second Republican Constitution in 1979, and the ‘impeachment’ of the Chief Justice in 2013 following the Supreme Court’s determination of certain provisions of a bill as inconsistent with the Constitution, the people of Sri Lanka have witnessed a few failed attempts to haul judges before Parliament or to issue veiled threats affecting their independence.

In some constitutional or legal systems, there is a glaring absence of a built-in institutional mechanism or avenue for ‘judging judges’ in the higher judiciary – a mechanism or avenue operating before, or independently of, the ultimate removal process. A Parliament-initiated impeachment or removal process can only be the last resort. When a judge of the higher judiciary is alleged to conduct himself/herself in such a manner or indulge in such activities that may risk undermining public confidence in his/her impartiality, integrity or independence, the need for such an institutional mechanism or avenue, short of impeachment or removal, appears more pronounced.

In Sri Lanka, such a mechanism is available for lower court judges only, and the Judicial Service Commission, despite its drawbacks, is designed to serve that purpose. It is public knowledge, however, that such drawbacks have led to considerable criticism from time to time.

There are several reasons why the NPP’s move to increase the retirement age of judges of the higher judiciary – later, other echelons, too, have been added – should be viewed with caution. They are iterated without going into the legal complexities surrounding the issue.

First, there was no draft policy/position paper from the government that explains the rationale for the proposed increase in the retirement age, or on what basis the need for extension of the retirement age has arisen. This may be rather uncharacteristic of a government which dwells on the virtues of transparency and accountability for hours on end both in and out of Parliament.

Second, seeking to increase the retirement age in particular, without simultaneously addressing other urgent issues affecting the judiciary as a whole, could only reflect a narrow approach. The citizens of Sri Lanka need to be taken into confidence as to why the government thinks that by addressing this particular aspect, other systemic and structural issues affecting the judiciary will be remedied, and how.

Third, the constitutional principle of judicial independence is sacrosanct, and it cannot be addressed in a haphazard manner, specifically focusing on retirement age. In case it is to be done for an objective need, it should be ensured in a manner that doesn’t affect the promotional prospects of judges in the lower echelons of the system (even though, perhaps as an afterthought, their age of retirement was also proposed to be increased). Although appointments are made following the approval of the Constitutional Council, that doesn’t extinguish the principle of legitimate expectations operating in a moral domain when it comes to the appointment of judges to the superior courts.

Fourth, the doctrine of separation of powers in democratic societies operates in a constitutional ecosystem of checks and balances of one another’s power, however less ideal the ecosystem is. When one aspect pertaining to a particular branch of government is addressed without any rational justification or objective need – there is no evidence in the public domain that the judiciary is seriously constrained by the existing requirements relating to the age of retirement – it cuts deeply to the core of that constitutional ecosystem.

Besides all these, a number of other concerns also persist. The more significant among them is that the retirement age shouldn’t be increased immediately, but prospectively, meaning with effect from a date in the future. Further, the manner in which the disciplinary authority of the Judicial Service Commission is exercised has received wide attention in recent times.

Considering an institutional mechanism or avenue which could consider complainants/appeals from aggrieved parties may strengthen safeguards in favour of the independence of the judiciary as well as the security of judicial tenure.

However, these and any other challenges affecting the judicial system would require reforms that go far beyond the increase in the age of retirement alone. The constitutional ecosystem is larger and would correspondingly emphasise reforms to other branches of government as well. It would undoubtedly involve enlarging freedom and human security. Constitutionalism in particular calls for wide consultations and participation.

Strikingly, however, this is where promises were made from time to time by successive governments including the incumbent one. And this is where they still remain yet to see the day of light.

A.L.A. Azeez is a former Permanent Representative of Sri Lanka to the UN in Geneva & Former Ambassador to Vienna
