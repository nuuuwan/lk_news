Is Sri Lanka’s rabies strategy failing?

Colombo, Oct. 5 (Daily Mirror) - Sri Lanka’s decades-long approach to controlling rabies has come under fresh scrutiny, with veterinarian Dr. Chamith Nanayakkara questioning whether the country is spending too much public money treating people after dog bites instead of eliminating the disease at its source.

Nanayakkara, an Executive Committee member of the Animal Welfare Coalition and Convener of the Veterinarians’ Forum for Humane Animal Population Control, raised the issue at a media briefing amid an ongoing dispute over the Government’s proposed Rabies Elimination and Dog Registration Bill.

He questioned why Sri Lanka, which began its national rabies control programme in 1953, was still recording human deaths from rabies despite decades of vaccination and treatment programmes.

According to WHO, Sri Lanka recorded 14 laboratory-confirmed human rabies deaths in 2025, while nearly 168,000 people received post-exposure prophylaxis following possible exposure to the disease. More than 1.6 million dogs were vaccinated during the same year, with estimated national coverage exceeding 60%.

Nanayakkara argued that continuing to treat people after they have been bitten cannot by itself eliminate rabies.

“If we remove the rabies virus from dogs, we can solve this problem. Otherwise, the virus will remain, dogs will continue to bite people and we will continue giving injections to people after they are bitten,” he said.

He said dog vaccination, sterilisation and proper management of the dog population should therefore receive greater attention.

Nanayakkara has previously claimed that nearly Rs. 1 billion in public funds is spent on post-exposure treatment for people bitten by animals, arguing that greater resources should instead be directed towards preventing transmission among dogs.

However, the Health Ministry’s programme is not limited to treating bite victims. Official figures show that more than 1.28 million dogs were vaccinated and nearly 30,000 female dogs were sterilised under the national programme in 2024.

WHO has also said that Sri Lanka’s progress has been supported by post-exposure treatment, surveillance and sustained dog vaccination, while stressing that stronger coordination between human health, animal health, local authorities and communities is needed to reach zero human deaths by 2030.

The dispute has intensified following the proposed new legislation, which would place dog vaccination under the supervision of veterinary surgeons.

The Government Medical Officers’ Association (GMOA) has opposed the proposed arrangement, warning that it could disrupt the existing vaccination programme because of the limited number of government veterinary surgeons.

The GMOA says dog vaccination is currently carried out by trained vaccinators across 362 Medical Officer of Health areas under the supervision of health authorities. It claims vaccination coverage is around 60%, against a target of 70%.

Nanayakkara, however, maintains that veterinarians should take primary responsibility for the animal component of rabies control because they are trained to deal with animal diseases, vaccination and population management.

He said Sri Lanka should move towards a “One Health” model in which veterinarians focus on eliminating rabies among animals while medical professionals continue to prevent and treat human infections.

The WHO has also endorsed the need for closer coordination between human health, animal health and local authorities as Sri Lanka moves towards its 2030 elimination target.

The debate therefore goes beyond a dispute between doctors and veterinarians. At its centre is a more fundamental question: after more than seven decades of rabies control, is Sri Lanka using its limited public funds and resources in the most effective way to eliminate the disease?

While the country has made considerable progress in reducing human rabies deaths, the continued presence of rabies among animals and the number of people requiring post-exposure treatment indicate that the final stage of elimination remains a major challenge.
