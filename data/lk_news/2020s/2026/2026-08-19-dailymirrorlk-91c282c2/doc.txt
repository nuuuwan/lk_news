Business leaders clash over role of foreign capital and democracy in Sri Lanka’s economic recovery

The role of international capital and the efficiency of the democratic system in driving Sri Lanka’s economic recovery became a point of intense debate among leading corporate figures and economists, highlighting the growing tension between the need for rapid policy execution and the preservation of institutional resilience.

During a panel discussion hosted by the Sri Lanka-Korea Business Council last week in Colombo, titled “Sri Lanka’s Future – Forecast, Scenarios and Challenges,” a sharp contrast in economic philosophies emerged. Aroshi Nanayakkara, a global consulting company CEO and immediate past chairperson of the Sri Lanka Institute of Directors, challenged the conventional economic approach, questioning whether a democratic system with its frequent electoral cycles and shifting mandates is conducive to maintaining the long-term, single-minded vision necessary for economic transformation.

Drawing on her extensive background across strategy, human resources, and risk management, alongside her experience serving on the boards of major financial institutions, Nanayakkara pointed to the prolonged policy consistency seen in other Asian economies with centralized planning.

“I’m going to challenge saying is democracy the right way to go? Every five years when you have a new president coming in and a whole new parliament coming in, how are you going to maintain that vision for a country?” Nanayakkara asked.

She further challenged the widely held belief that the nation is starved of capital, arguing that the domestic financial system already holds sufficient funds.

“I completely agree with Murtaza, we don’t need capital. I’m from the banking sector, there’s a lot of liquidity out there, and there’s a lot of money, capital, which is not getting deployed where it should be deployed,” she noted. “So we really do not need foreign capital coming in, but as you said, we need that knowledge base coming in from foreign markets.”

However, this perspective drew a strong and impassioned rebuttal from CAL Holdings Group CEO Kanishka Mannakkara, who vigorously defended the necessity of both foreign capital and the democratic process. Mannakkara warned that isolating the economy from global capital flows would be a severe miscalculation, particularly as the world undergoes a massive technological and industrial shift.

“First, a few people mentioned that we don’t need foreign capital. I disagree with that for a couple of reasons,” Mannakkara stated. “We are entering a new economy, it’s really akin to the Industrial Revolution where the growth is very capital intensive, and we are seeing one of the largest phases of investment globally right now. To then imagine that Sri Lanka can somehow operate in an inter-economy ecosystem outside this global norm is crazy. So we do need that.”

He further elaborated that the benefits of international capital extend far beyond mere financial liquidity, acting as a critical enforcement mechanism for institutional discipline.

“That’s only part of the story about international capital. You see, international capital also forces governance,” he explained. “With that capital comes knowledge anyway, and also remember international capital will help reduce interest rates domestically because of the liquidity that it brings. So for all of these reasons, I think it is important for us to think about what makes international capital conducive.”

Addressing the skepticism surrounding the country’s governance model, Mannakkara firmly pushed back against the idea that authoritarian efficiency should be favored over democratic processes. He cautioned that dismissing the foundational structures of the state in pursuit of rapid economic gains is a perilous approach.

“Finally, I think we should be careful. While it’s tempting for us to rubbish the public sector and to question the value of democracy, I think these are really dangerous things to say,” Mannakkara emphasised. “Because democracy is the ultimate safety valve. Yes, it’s messy, yes, it’s annoying, but it’s what gives us resilience.”

The exchange underscored a deeper ideological divide within the business community regarding the optimal path forward for the economy, as policymakers and corporate leaders continue to grapple with balancing the demand for swift, unhindered reforms against the essential checks, balances, and global integrations required for long-term stability.
