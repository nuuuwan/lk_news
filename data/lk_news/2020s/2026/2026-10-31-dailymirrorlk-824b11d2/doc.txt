Two decades on, Aditya’s quiet blueprint still shapes Sri Lanka’s boutique luxury

Long before boutique villas became the calling card of Sri Lanka’s southern coast, a small circle of friends turned plans for a four-bedroom holiday home on a secluded beach at Rathgama, between Hikkaduwa and Galle, into an all-suite resort that today holds registration number 001 on the Sri Lanka Tourism Development Authority’s (SLTDA) register of boutique hotels.

Last week, Aditya Resort’s founders, staff and a gathering of long-standing partners, many of whom had made the drive down from Colombo, came together at the property to mark its 20th anniversary, in an afternoon that doubled as a reflection on how a passion project helped define a segment now central to the island’s pitch for high-spending travellers.

“Without your continuous support, we wouldn’t be here,” Aditya Resort Managing Director Husain Dawoodbhoy told the gathering, thanking guests for keeping the resort going for two decades “through some good times and some very bad times”.

Aditya Co-founder and Chairman Asgi Akbarally, who is also Managing Director of Akbar Brothers, recalled that when the project took shape some 22 years ago, people called the entire undertaking “a mad idea”. What was meant to be a four-bedroom holiday home “turned out to be this”, he observed, crediting his classmate and the resort’s architect, the late Bernard Gomez, who “decided to break every rule of architecture”.

Following a structural review, the team chose to retain just one wall of the old house, Akbarally explained, and it remains the only trace of the building that once stood on the site.

Gomez, a Fellow of the Sri Lanka Institute of Architects and principal architect of BGJF Consultancy Services, did not live to see the milestone. Dawoodbhoy asked guests to stand for a minute’s silence in his memory, recalling a warm exchange with the architect the last time they spoke.

A product of the University of Moratuwa’s architecture school, Gomez apprenticed under modernist pioneers Valentine Gunasekara and Minnette de Silva before building his own practice. He regarded architecture as a sequence of experiences in which the outdoors flows seamlessly into the interior, a conviction carried throughout Aditya’s open-plan halls and in suites that spill onto gardens, courtyards and terraces.

The Managing Director was candid that the venture was never built around returns.

“Passion was always the motive behind Aditya; profit was a secondary thing because all of us had other main avenues of income,” he pointed out.

He paid tribute to the small group who poured “their hearts and their passion” into the project during construction, including interior designer Patricia Mamelka, who was in the audience.

Each of the original suites takes its name from the Adityas, the solar deities born of the goddess Aditi in Hindu mythology, whom later tradition counts as twelve, matching the dozen suites with which the resort opened. Guests today sleep in Mitra, Varuna, Aryaman or Indra. Surya, the sun itself, crowns the Old Wing as a 1,800 square foot penthouse spread over two levels, with a private sundeck and an open-air plunge pool.

Below it, the Shanthi suites, of between 950 and 1,100 square feet, open onto private courtyards and plunge pools. The slightly larger Sagara suites occupy the mezzanine and first floor, their verandahs facing straight out to the Indian Ocean.

The interiors, by Patricia Mamelka, draw together Rajasthani antiques, Balinese contemporary pieces and the craft of the south. Ambalangoda masks are set against polished cement floors, alongside intricately carved bedheads and expansive open-plan spaces that let sea breezes and daylight move freely through the building. When the hotel was new, Mamelka described the idea as “a mansion with big ‘rooms’ ... each having a character of its own”, one that would offer foreign guests a window into Sri Lankan art and culture without leaving the property.

The New Wing, opened in 2018, pushed the scale of the offering further. Its two villas, each of roughly 7,500 square feet, were described at launch as likely the biggest standalone two-suite units offered by any hotel in the country. Each pairs a ground-floor Pool Suite, Aditi or Shakti, fronted by a nine-metre lap pool facing the beach, with an upper Sky Suite, Vayu or Rudra, topped by a heated rooftop jacuzzi. The villas can be let suite by suite or taken whole, and together they bring the resort’s inventory to 16 keys across its 12 suites and two villas.

Aditya also dispensed with fixed meal times from the outset. In its first year a co-manager remarked that breakfast at 6 p.m. was entirely possible, with tables laid wherever a guest chose, whether on the beach, in the garden or in the suite.

Outside the property, the resort arranges whale watching off Mirissa, boat safaris on the Madu Ganga, village cycling tours and guided visits to the Galle Fort. A spa and a preventative health and wellness programme cater to guests who would rather not leave the grounds at all.

When Aditya opened, boutique hospitality in Sri Lanka was still in its infancy, with properties of the kind only beginning to appear around the turn of the century. Dawoodbhoy pitched the resort as an answer to a shortage of private getaways for upmarket travellers seeking seclusion.

Its formula was simple: very few keys, unusually generous suites with private pools, design-led interiors and unscripted personal service on a secluded beach. That formula has since become a familiar template down south, where boutique hotels and private villas now court the same affluent traveller along much of the coast between Galle and Tangalle. UK specialist operator Experience Travel Group, for one, credits Aditya with raising the bar on the island’s early boutique concept.

Condé Nast Traveller’s Readers’ Choice Awards 2024 gave the resort a near-perfect score of 98.0, placing it among the top two Sri Lankan hotels in the Rest of Asia category. That came on top of the Condé Nast Johansens Award for Excellence it won for 2019.

As per SLTDA data, the island had 39 registered boutique hotels with 877 rooms in 2025, up from 38 hotels with 835 rooms a year earlier, alongside 50 boutique villas with 353 rooms. Together, those 1,230 rooms account for barely 2 percent of Sri Lanka’s 59,578 registered tourist rooms. Yet the Galle district alone holds 436 of them, or about 35 percent, underscoring how firmly the format has taken root down south. The SLTDA has also acknowledged that a share of the luxury boutique stock operates outside its register, saying in 2024 that it hoped to bring such properties into the formal sector to cater for rising high-end demand.

Gomez himself set out the underlying philosophy in 2021, arguing that Sri Lanka’s coastal tourism should embrace a less-is-more approach. He called for plots of roughly an acre carrying a handful of carefully designed units over less than half the land, and buildings no taller than five storeys so as to stay beneath the line of the coastal coconut palms. He also wanted hospitality that leans on local cooking and local character. Conceived some 15 years earlier, Aditya reads as an early built argument for the same view.

“We are so proud to be the front runners of the boutique hospitality space, which Sri Lanka has come to be known for,” Dawoodbhoy noted in a statement issued to mark the anniversary.

Dawoodbhoy revealed that 26 members of staff have been with Aditya for more than a decade, some for as long as 20 years and many for over 15. On a 16-key property, that amounts to more than one and a half long-serving employees for every suite.

“Their skills are in demand in industries today. The fact that they choose to be with us ensures the quality and consistency of our product,” he added.

Director and General Manager Shamindra Fernando, who was already co-managing the hotel in its first year, embodies that continuity. The event’s master of ceremonies observed that repeat guests often arrive asking for staff members by name, describing it as a culture built on passion that has carried the property through two decades.

That loyalty was tested during the economic crisis. Akbarally told guests the team had braced for a 20 percent salary cut, a cost he said the Managing Director absorbed himself, with salaries paid in full throughout the downturn.

“We know their families, we know their children, and they are part of our family,” he remarked.

He added that the management continues to meet staff to improve and add facilities for them.

Fernando noted that the team is drawn almost entirely from the resort’s immediate surroundings, describing the jobs as a source of income and professional standing for the community around Rathgama.

Aditya’s chefs lead cookery demonstrations that begin with a visit to the local fish market. When the hotel opened, it made its own chocolates, pastries and jams in house, along with its soaps and shampoos. More recently, the resort has switched to environmentally friendly packaging with the aim of becoming plastic-free.

Fernando kept his own remarks brief, insisting that “Aditya is all about all of you, our staff, and our partners”. He pledged to carry those relationships into the resort’s third decade so that guests continue to leave with “a memorable, beautiful experience”.

Aditya’s average occupancy stands at around 60 percent, Fernando told Mirror Business, rising to as much as 95 percent in the peak season, when the resort is at times fully booked. Across its 16 keys, that translates to roughly 10 suites taken on an average night, climbing to 15 and, on some nights, all 16 at the height of the season.

The resort stands on seven acres of land, of which 5.5 acres, close to four-fifths, can be developed. Fernando revealed that the company is exploring possibilities and weighing proposals for that space. How the land is eventually used will show whether the less-is-more thinking that shaped the original resort carries into its next chapter.

Fittingly for a resort that began as a holiday home planned by a group of friends, the anniversary unfolded less as a corporate function than as a reunion of the people who built it, staff it and keep returning to it. As Akbarally put it in his address, “So that’s Aditya for you.”
