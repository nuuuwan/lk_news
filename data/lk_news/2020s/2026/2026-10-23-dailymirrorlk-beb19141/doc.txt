Preventive Strategies and Consular Protection: Learning Lessons

Sri Lankan legal team meeting with representatives from Saudi Arabia’s legal firms discussing the appeal before the Saudi Supreme Court with regards to Anojan’s case. File photo

It is to the nation’s great relief that the news came out that Anojan Sivarasa is now off the gallows. His death sentence has reportedly been overturned by the Supreme Court of Saudi Arabia. It’s a welcome sign of warm and vibrant bilateral relations and a judicious understanding of the situation and its complexities.

Nevertheless, questions abound on the actual scope and effect of the Supreme Court’s judgement.

It appears that when, under its extraordinary or inherent jurisdiction, the Saudi Supreme Court overturns a death sentence as an immediate relief in exceptional circumstances, it remands the matter to a different circuit of the Court of Appeal for reconsideration on merits, or disposal of the appeal preferred against the original court’s decision.

It is yet to be known, however, if this was the procedure adopted in Anojan’s case. If it were, is the question of acquittal, actual sentence or fine bound to come up for deliberation in the Court of Appeal? Will the records of proceedings including the Court of Appeal’s decision go thereafter directly to the SC for consideration/approval? Apparently the Saudi Supreme Court is not only the master of its own procedure, but that of the lower courts as well.

What actually is the SC’s decision or directive on the matter will be known only when the official record/copy of its decision is available in public.

As the Deputy Minister of Foreign Affairs broke the news of Saudi SC’s decision to overturn the death sentence, he thanked all officials as well as the legal team and diplomatic personnel for the efforts taken to save Anojan’s life.

As the government activated its diplomatic, legal, and consular machinery following the imposition of the death sentence by the Saudi Court of Appeal – which now stands overturned – an intensive campaign of public and international appeals seeking Anojan’s release or clemency built up simultaneously. Community organisations, religious bodies, other civil society groups, and people’s representatives from different parties, individually or jointly, appealed to the Saudi government to save Anojan’s life, commute his sentence, or set him free. The Government, to its credit, went to the extent of meeting and reimbursing all expenses in relation to appellate proceedings. It rushed a legal team to Riyadh as well.

In a significant move, the UN and international human rights organisations also engaged the Saudi government through both diplomatic channels and public appeals. Importantly, the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights reportedly engaged with senior Saudi diplomats, strongly urging action to spare Anojan’s life. He was believed to have received assurances of prompt consideration.

Increased negative publicity could only be counter-productive to the pursuit of objectives considered vital to the long-term interests of any country. Further, sensitivity to international criticism and a tendency to address serious concerns when a country’s external perception requires positive improvement are nothing new in international relations.

However, there can never be celebration until Anojan’s fate – through acquittal, clemency, or by way of removal or deportation – is resolved, sooner than later.

Lessons are out there already. One hopes that the government, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Embassies abroad, the SLBFE, migrant workers, and the different communities have all drawn lessons appropriately and will reflect on them seriously for future guidance.

The lessons learned warrant that the government ensure the Foreign Ministry, Embassies abroad, and other relevant ministries, departments and agencies evolve appropriate preventive strategies for the future – including awareness creation, sensitisation, and multicultural and multi-religious orientation. Developing a comprehensive understanding of the duty of consular assistance and protection, keeping in view the provisions of the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations (1963) and the Migrants Workers’ Convention (1996) is imperative.

Sri Lanka’s domestic laws including the Consular Functions Act and the SLBFE Act fall far short of the standard of duty expected of a state party to these international treaties. Nonetheless, this should not constrain an Embassy of Sri Lanka providing basic consular assistance, in a timely manner, while respecting the applicable laws of a host country.

The Ministry and Embassies abroad need to identify key entry points for the delivery of consular assistance depending on the severity of alleged offence whenever such instances are reported.

In the volatile environment that prevails in much of the Middle East and other regions today, the government’s duty of consular assistance and protection, exercised through its Embassies abroad, risks being tested to its limits unless adequate preparations are made.

Consular assistance is both a right and a responsibility. The right-holder does not need to assert his or her right in order for the government to act in their aid. The responsibility is automatic, even in the absence of an explicit assertion. This is because a person whose liberty is restricted – such as when under arrest – has no way of effectively exercising that right at the material time.

Pending clarity as to whether the Saudi Supreme Court issued any directives regarding an alternative form of punishment, it is possible to assume that Anojan is left with the original sentence (5 years and a fine). If so, legal proceedings may decide on what awaits him in terms of imprisonment, fine, or both. The Foreign Ministry and the Embassy must explore arrangements that could help bring him back to Sri Lanka.

The Embassy is already on record stating it has discussed with Saudi authorities the early conclusion of an agreement relating to the transfer of sentenced persons. It is important to finalise the agreement with a scope broad enough to cover instances like Anojan’s.

It is high time that the fountainheads of hate in Sri Lanka, and the streams that carry hatred both inland and abroad, be tackled as effectively as possible. The lessons learnt may necessitate this precautionary approach.

It is well-known that there was a sudden surge of videos and Facebook posts, trading hate for hate, preceding, during and immediately following the incident in question. Much of it could have been deterred, or their impact minimised, had early warning signals been promptly received and acted on. The importance of keeping the country safe from the devastating effects of hate-mongering and violent extremism on social harmony, stability, and reconciliation is a compelling lesson that both the government and the society as a whole cannot ignore.

The writer is the former Permanent Representative of Sri Lanka to the UN in Geneva and former Ambassador to Vienna.
