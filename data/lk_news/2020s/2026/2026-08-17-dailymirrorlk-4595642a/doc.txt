Why is Sri Lanka importing its fish?

Sri Lanka is an island. We are surrounded by the sea and have some of the richest fishing waters in the region. Yet, despite this natural advantage, we continue to import large quantities of fish every year.

The latest figures show just how much we have come to depend on imports. During the first nine months of 2025, Sri Lanka imported 42,796 metric tonnes of fish and fish products, spending nearly Rs. 26 billion. During the same period, local fish production was around 281,755 metric tonnes. This means imported fish was equal to about 15 percent of local production.

There will always be a need to import certain varieties of fish. There may also be times when local production is not enough to meet demand. But becoming heavily dependent on imported fish should not be the long term answer. The government needs to take a serious look at the fishing industry and ask why local fishermen are not able to produce more. Our fishermen face many difficulties. Many still operate with old boats and equipment and often have limited access to modern technology.

A fisherman may go out to sea, spend hours trying to catch fish and then return to find that he has nowhere suitable to store his catch. This is where government investment is urgently needed.

Sri Lanka needs modern fishing harbours, proper ice and cold storage facilities and better transport systems to get fish from the harbour to markets around the country. Our fishermen also need access to affordable loans to purchase better boats and equipment. They need modern navigation systems, weather information and technology. The government has already recognised the importance of technology in fisheries. The 2026 Budget allocated Rs. 100 million to develop a system to identify fishing grounds. This is a good step, but much more needs to be done.

Sri Lanka must also look seriously at aquaculture and fish farming. But while we expect our fishermen to protect and develop the industry, the government must also protect our waters.

The continuing problem of Indian fishermen entering Sri Lankan waters cannot be ignored. Sri Lanka has repeatedly arrested Indian fishermen who cross into Sri Lankan waters. In the latest incident, nine Indian fishermen were arrested near Talaimannar for allegedly entering Sri Lankan waters and engaging in illegal fishing.  According to figures provided by the Indian Government to its Parliament, Sri Lanka has arrested more than 1,300 Indian fishermen since 2023 and seized hundreds of fishing boats. Sri Lanka must protect its territorial waters. This is not about being hostile towards India or Indian fishermen.

The issue of bottom trawling is even more serious. Bottom trawling involves dragging heavy fishing nets across the seabed. It can destroy marine habitats and damage the areas where fish breed and grow. Sri Lanka banned bottom trawling in 2017, yet incursions by Indian trawlers continue to be reported. The government therefore needs to take a firm position on illegal fishing and continue discussions with India to find a lasting solution. Arresting fishermen every few weeks and then releasing them does not solve the problem. At the same time, Sri Lanka must not use the Indian fishing issue as an excuse for ignoring its own fishermen.

We need better boats, better harbours, better storage, and better technology. We also need to reduce the amount of fish that is wasted between the time it is caught and the time it reaches the consumer.  If we increase production and improve quality, we can export more seafood.

Sri Lanka already exports seafood, particularly tuna. With proper investment, there is no reason why the country cannot become a much stronger seafood exporter. This requires political will. The government should set clear targets to reduce fish imports every year while increasing local production. Most importantly, fisheries must be treated as a major part of Sri Lanka’s economy.

Our sea should be feeding our people, supporting our fishermen and strengthening our economy. It should be a warning that we are failing to make use of one of our greatest natural resources.
