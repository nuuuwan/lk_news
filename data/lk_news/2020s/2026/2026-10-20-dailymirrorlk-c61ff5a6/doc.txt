Social media influence on elections

The Election Commission has expressed its grave concern over social media and artificial intelligence (AI) influence on voters, which the commission fears could reach a dangerous level in  future.

This is an undeniable fact given the politicisation of social media platforms, especially  Facebook, where vulgar language used by  content creators, especially when it comes to politics,   results in grave misleading of viewers, and   the nature of posts uploaded on those platforms is hateful as well.

Although the commission had quoted the findings of a research conducted by Democracy Reporting International and Factum as saying that  social media and artificial intelligence had influenced voters in the 2024 Presidential election, Parliamentary election and the 2025 local government elections, it is not in fact a recent phenomenon. Leaving aside AI, social media has been influencing  voters in Sri Lanka at least since the  elections in 2015, though not to the extent we see today.

Globally, we have witnessed the impact of social media and networked citizens on politics during the 2011 Arab Spring.

If one casually opens a few Facebook pages or a few X (formerly twitter) accounts, it would be vividly clear to him/her that discussions on any subject on social media -- even if it is a scientific phenomenon like the extremely rare visibility of Halley’s Comet -- soon end up in a political debate, forgetting the original subject matter within minutes, if not seconds.

In contrast to social media posts published in the English language, vernacular posts are frustratingly more hateful, toxic, and vulgar when discussing politics and, regrettably, religion.  Despite Sri Lanka having enacted laws such as the Online Safety Act to control  social media contents, their capacity to contain hate speech, disinformation and misinformation is limited, especially in the light of the  widespread nature of  social media networks.

Dr. Ranga Kalansooriya, the Asia Regional Advisor of Denmark-based International Media Support (IMF),  stated in an interview with the Daily Mirror last month:  “We have a huge vacuum here because the new information ecosystem has no borders. But our systems are taking decisions according to geographic or legal perimeters –- legal borders. Those are not in parallel.” Besides, tracing malpractices in vernacular language social media is relatively difficult  for practical reasons.

Social media is primarily in the hands of  youth, especially Gen Z who do not have first-hand knowledge about their country’s political history beyond around 2015. Yet, Political leverage remains in the hands of older generations who still can distort the history and manipulate the political thinking of the younger generation. “A Parliamentarian from the United National Party (UNP)—which was in power during the second JVP insurrection—recently stated unabashedly that over 60,000 people were killed by the JVP, even though the facts incriminate his own party.”

Also, the younger generation craves systemic change, yet they lack the experience and knowledge required to identify or implement it. For instance, the youth who engaged in Sri Lanka’s Aragalaya in 2022 demanding “Gota go home” were at a loss for an alternative when “Gota” actually went home. This provided an opportunity to the old guard in several countries in South Asia to reenter the scene in “sheep’s clothing,” ironically using the same social media.

Needless to say the significance of political discussions and debates in public fora would qualitatively and quantitatively increase the awareness of the people in a democracy. It is inevitable for informed decisions by  voters at elections. Nevertheless, information it brings to you is not the plain truth; rather,  it is a combination of truth as well as total distortions of facts, utter lies, emotionally charged content and misleading interpretations of situations.

In many cases, you would not identify the content authors  on social media and websites or their motives. Hence, if a corrupt group takes the upper hand in content handling on social media platforms during the time leading to a national election, the result of that election will be influenced, and it can drastically alter democratic outcomes with long-lasting consequences. Since these groups are using the democratic space, the challenge that society faces is to find a democratic way to prevent the harm they inflict and do inflict.
