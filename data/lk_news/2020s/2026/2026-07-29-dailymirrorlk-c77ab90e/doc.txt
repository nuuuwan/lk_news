Sri Lanka was recently officially reclassified by the World Bank as an “Upper Middle-Income Country.” This new classification is based on the country’s 5% economic growth recorded in 2025 and the macroeconomic recovery achieved following the economic crisis of 2022. Accordingly, Sri Lanka has been upgraded from the “Lower Middle-Income” category to the “Upper Middle-Income” category under the World Bank’s income classification.

Against this backdrop of economic growth, however, the manner in which business transactions are being conducted in the country is regrettable. This is due to the issuance of dishonoured cheques during commercial transactions. The practice of issuing dishonoured cheques has now become widespread across Sri Lanka. At times, it even appears to be part of an organised fraudulent scheme, given the extent to which dishonoured cheque transactions have spread throughout the country.

Business owners, who have fallen victim to dishonoured cheque frauds, told the Daily Mirror that the issue poses a serious threat to the future of their businesses. They further stated that, as a result of this type of fraud, many small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are at risk of being forced to cease operations.

Many business owners, already burdened by taxes, high electricity costs, and the risk of losing their properties under the Parate Execution, have fallen victim to this dishonoured cheque fraud. Typically, a businessperson conducts transactions on a credit period ranging from one to three months. As a result of these transactions, they often end up holding dishonoured cheques worth between LKR 5-10 million, issued in settlement of earlier transactions carried out during that credit period. Business owners pointed out that a large number of small and medium-sized enterprises across the country have now fallen victim to such fraudulent transactions.

The legal provisions relating to dishonoured cheques were strengthened through the Bills of Exchange (Amendment) Act, No. 13 of 2025. Under the Act, a person responsible for issuing a dishonoured cheque may be liable to a fine equivalent to the value of the cheque, imprisonment for a term of up to two years, or both. A cheque may be dishonoured for several reasons, including insufficient funds in the account, exceeding an overdraft facility, issuing a cheque from a closed account, or instructing the bank to stop payment without reasonable cause. Such practices are commonly encountered in the business sector. However, when these actions are carried out deliberately as part of a fraudulent scheme, they constitute a serious offence. The dishonour of a cheque, issued by a company, partnership or corporation, as well as in the course of a business transaction, constitutes a serious financial and legal issue.

Although penalties for dishonoured cheques were strengthened under the Bills of Exchange (Amendment) Act, No. 13 of 2025, those engaged in this fraud appear to have little regard for the law. Lodging a complaint with the police often proves futile, as the perpetrators evade the authorities. Even when the police summon them to appear for investigations, some fail to comply. Business owners said that these individuals have no fear of legal consequences.

Those who issue dishonoured cheques often evade payment by repeatedly promising to settle the amount “tomorrow” or “the day after.” Alternatively, they gain the confidence of business owners by signing written undertakings promising to pay LKR 50,000 per week, but subsequently fail to honour those commitments.

Evidence of this can be observed in a letter issued on 05.06.2026 by an individual from Tangalle to a business owner in relation to a commercial transaction. According to the business owner, the issuer has neither paid the amount due under the dishonoured cheque nor complied with the payment schedule set out in the letter. Only a single payment of LKR 25,000 has been made. The business owner further alleged that the remaining amount has not been paid.

The individual, who provided the letter in relation to a transaction worth LKR 492,000, had issued two cheques. Both cheques, drawn on an account maintained by the individual at a well-known bank in Tangalle, were dishonoured.

Another business owner told us that dishonoured cheque frauds have now spread across the country and is having a severe impact on businesses. According to him, some individuals issue dishonoured cheques for relatively small amounts. However, when these unpaid cheques accumulate, the total financial loss can reach between LKR 5-10 million.

Cases of dishonoured cheque frauds have been reported across Sri Lanka. The following are only a few examples of such transactions. Dishonoured cheques amounting to LKR 429,160 were reported from Kalutara, LKR 308,450 from Horowpothana, LKR 483,060 from Batticaloa, LKR 474,560 from Muttur, LKR 1,013,900 from Kantale, and LKR 318,550 from Sooriyawewa. These cheques were issued over the course of several business transactions. According to business owners, the same individuals have issued additional dishonoured cheques as well. When the value of all such cheques is aggregated, a single business owner may suffer losses amounting to nearly LKR 5 million. Business owners describe this as a serious threat to the commercial sector and stress that the law should be enforced more rigorously.

Inquiries made by this scribe further revealed that although complaints regarding dishonoured cheques are lodged with the police, the individuals who issued the cheques frequently fail to appear for the relevant investigations. The Daily Mirror also revealed that those who issued the dishonoured cheques have been evading the police investigations. It also appears that some of those individuals do not even accept summons issued to them under Section 109(6) of the Code of Criminal Procedure Act, No. 15 of 1979. If a person refuses to accept or deliberately evades such a summons, the Officer-in-Charge (OIC) of the police station that issued the summons has the authority to seek a warrant for that person’s arrest. In addition, legal proceedings may be instituted under Section 172 of the Penal Code.

Under the Bills of Exchange (Amendment) Act, No. 13 of 2025, it is an offence to issue a cheque that is dishonoured due to a stop-payment instruction or insufficient funds (a bounced cheque). There are several legal procedures that must be followed in relation to such a cheque. A cheque must be presented to the relevant bank within six months from the date of issue, or within its period of validity, whichever is applicable. If the cheque is dishonoured or returned unpaid, the holder must notify the drawer of the cheque in writing. Upon receiving such notice, the drawer is required to settle the amount due within 90 days. If payment is still not made within that 90-day period, legal proceedings may be instituted before the Magistrate’s Court within 30 days from the date of default.

In addition, instructing a bank to stop payment on a cheque without reasonable cause in the course of a legitimate commercial transaction may also be regarded as a fraudulent act. A cheque issued on a closed bank account cannot be used to complete a transaction or obtain payment. When such a cheque is presented to the bank, payment will be refused because the account has already been closed. This constitutes a dishonoured (bounced) cheque. Issuing cheques from a closed bank account and attempting to obtain money through such cheques are unlawful acts under Sri Lanka’s Bills of Exchange Act.

To recover the amount due under a dishonoured cheque (cheque bouncing), the holder should first obtain a Return Memo from the bank stating the reason why the cheque was dishonoured. The person or business that issued the cheque should then be notified in writing and requested to make payment. If the drawer fails to settle the amount, legal action may be instituted. The holder should obtain the bank-issued Return Memo accompanying the dishonoured cheque, which specifies the reason for its return. The drawer of the cheque should be informed of the dishonour both by telephone and in writing, and payment should be formally demanded. If payment is not received after allowing a reasonable period such as 14 days from the date of the written notice legal proceedings may be initiated under the applicable legal provisions. In such proceedings, the original cheque, the bank’s Return Memo, and documentary evidence establishing the underlying transaction for which payment was due should be produced before the court.

With these documents, a complaint may be lodged at the relevant police station or the Fraud Investigation Division, and legal proceedings may be instituted before the Magistrate’s Court through an attorney-at-law. A cheque remains valid for a period of six months from the date of issue, or until the last date on which it may be presented to the bank, as applicable. Accordingly, it is important to commence legal proceedings without undue delay.

Under the new legal amendments, the dishonouring of a valid cheque due to reasons such as insufficient funds in the account constitutes an offence. Furthermore, where the value of a dishonoured cheque is less than LKR 1 million, the dispute must first be referred to a Mediation Board in an attempt to resolve the matter before legal proceedings are instituted in court. If the police refuse to accept a complaint relating to a dishonoured cheque or fail to take appropriate action, a private plaint can be filed before the Magistrate’s Court. This may be done either through an Attorney-at-Law or personally with the assistance of the relevant court officials.

“Enterprises are facing severe financial hardship as a result of dishonoured cheque transactions”

Commenting on the issue, Tania Abeysundara, Chairman of Sri Lanka United National Businesses Alliance, stated: “This has been happening for quite some time. We raised this issue with previous governments and repeatedly called for action. It was at our request that a decision was taken to deactivate a bank account when dishonoured cheques are issued. Even now, if several cheques issued by a person are dishonoured, that person’s bank account is closed. However, the individual simply opens a new account at another bank and, after some time, continues issuing dishonoured cheques. That is extremely unfair.

“There should be a system whereby, before a new bank account is opened, the individual’s transaction history is examined. Just as banks check whether a person has been blacklisted before granting a loan. Such a system would make it far more difficult for repeat offenders to commit fraud. It would also encourage cheque issuers to conduct their business responsibly and honour their financial obligations. As a result, when a business receives a cheque, it can have confidence that the cheque will be honoured within the agreed credit period, whether two weeks or one month.

“Accordingly, if, when opening a new account, a bank statement covering the preceding three months is obtained and used to assess whether the individual is suitable to engage in business transactions, such problems could be reduced. Many small and medium-sized enterprises are currently facing severe financial hardship as a result of dishonoured cheque transactions”.
