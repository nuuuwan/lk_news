Whither justice as UN marks solidarity day with Palestinians?

A drawing shared by children in Gaza as part of the “Gaza We Want” initiative launched by UNICEF.

Diplomatic coaxing and arm-twisting reached fever pitch at the United Nations headquarters in New York in the days leading up to a crucial vote on November 29, 1947, to partition historic Palestine, a land inhabited by various peoples long before the biblical exodus of the Israelites from Egypt in

While all the major post-World War II powers supported the partition of Palestine, some countries saw a colonial agenda behind the move. They were reluctant to back United Nations General Assembly Resolution 181, which was based on the majority recommendation of the United Nations Special Committee on Palestine (UNSCOP). There was no Arab voice, let alone a Palestinian one, on the committee. The United States, one of the principal proponents of the resolution, threatened countries such as Liberia and the Philippines with aid cuts if they failed to vote in its favour. The resolution was eventually adopted, with 33 countries voting in favour and 13 against. Ten countries abstained, while one was absent.

Significantly, the Soviet Union, then regarded as the citadel of socialism, also voted in favour of the resolution and did not view the partition of Palestine and the creation of Israel as a colonial project driven by an imperialist agenda. It was only much later that the Soviet Union came to support the Palestinians’ struggle for self-determination, perhaps more within the context of the Cold War than out of any commitment to peace and justice.

In contrast, the Fourth International, led by Leon Trotsky’s followers after their break with Joseph Stalin’s brand of socialism, rejected the partition plan.

In Sri Lanka, the Trotskyites, particularly the Lanka Sama Samaja Party, viewed the creation of Israel as a colonial project and opposed it, while members of the local elite, including D.S. Senanayake, Sir Arthur Ranasinghe and others like them, sought closer cooperation with the Zionists to develop the agricultural sector. Perhaps they were unaware of, or had not encountered, Theodor Herzl’s disparaging views of Asians. In his 1896 book Der Judenstaat (The Jewish State), Herzl, regarded as the father of modern Zionism, wrote that a Jewish state in Palestine would serve as a European defence line against Asia, an outpost of civilisation as opposed to barbarism.

Britain, which made the greatest contribution to the Zionist cause through the issuance of the Balfour Declaration in November 1917, abstained when Resolution 181 came before the United Nations. It is said that this was partly in deference to the growing pro-Palestinian sentiment in its colonies and throughout the Commonwealth of Nations, particularly in newly independent India and Pakistan. In India, Mahatma Gandhi’s views on Palestine commanded broad support. Gandhi wrote: “Palestine belongs to the Arabs in the same sense that England belongs to the English or France to the French. It is wrong and inhuman to impose the Jews on the Arabs.”

While the horrors of the Holocaust were still fresh in public memory, Gandhi also wrote: “My sympathies are all with the Jews ... But my sympathy does not blind me to the requirements of justice.”

Yet Resolution 181 was anything but an act of justice. It legitimised the Zionist invasion of Palestine, the displacement of the indigenous Palestinian people and the appropriation of their land. Resolution 181 was perhaps the first indication that the United Nations, established in 1945 to usher in a world order founded on peace and justice, would fail to fulfil its lofty ideals. Dominated by the victorious powers of World War II, the organisation has failed to secure a just and lasting peace in Palestine, notwithstanding the significant humanitarian work carried out by the UN Relief and Works Agency (UNRWA) and other UN bodies among Palestinian people.

To mark the United Nations International Day of Solidarity with the Palestinian People on November 29, UN Secretary-General António Guterres said in a message: “I repeat my call for an end to the unlawful occupation of the Palestinian Territory [...] and for irreversible progress towards a two-state solution, in line with international law and relevant UN resolutions.”

Whatever statements the UN may issue, the abuse of the veto by the United States has repeatedly scuttled UN-led efforts to find a just solution to the Palestinian question. The use of the veto, nearly on 50 occasions, to obstruct peace lends credence to accusations that US foreign policy is driven not by universal moral values but by power politics, which in turn rests on the use of force, the threat of violence and the intimidation of weaker nations.

In the early 19th century, when Palestine was under Ottoman rule, Jews comprised less than 11 per cent of the population. These indigenous Jewish communities lived in harmony with Arab Muslims and Christians. With the migration of European Jews to Palestine following the Balfour Declaration and the defeat of the Ottoman Empire in World War I, the demographic balance changed. By the time of partition, Jews made up slightly more than 30 per cent of the population, while Arabs accounted for about 68 per cent.

In a travesty of justice, Palestine was partitioned under UN Resolution 181 with the minority Jews, most of whom were European migrants, being allocated 55 per cent of the territory, while the majority Arabs were allotted only 43 per cent. Jerusalem and its environs were to be placed under international administration.

Needless to say, a partition plan that disregarded the principles of natural justice and undermined the very objectives behind the creation of the United Nations became a trigger for a continuing Nakba, or ethnic cleansing of Palestinians, the 1948 Arab-Israeli war, and subsequent conflicts in 1967, 1973 and beyond, culminating in Israel’s genocide in Gaza following a Hamas attack on Israeli communities near the border on October 7, 2023.

Today, the Palestinians have been left with only a fraction of the territory once envisaged as their state. The area administered by the Palestinian Authority under the 1993 Oslo Accords constitutes less than 17 per cent of the territory allocated to Palestinians under the partition plan. Palestinian land continues to shrink as the Israeli government vigorously pursues an expansionist policy in blatant disregard of international law, amid growing criticism that Israel has become an apartheid state.

For many years, the international consensus was that East Jerusalem should serve as the capital of a future Palestinian state. However, Israel has annexed the whole of Jerusalem, and the United States has recognised it as Israel’s undivided capital, demonstrating a willingness to disregard international law and principles of justice in support of Israel.

Directing criticism solely at the United States and Britain for the Palestinian plight tells only half the story. The story is incomplete without a critical examination of the role of certain Arab states, which stand accused of betraying the Palestinian cause, colluding with Israel, and failing to exert sufficient diplomatic pressure to halt the violence in Gaza. Arab resolve to confront Israel has become so feeble and ineffective that one could bet one’s life that even if a camel were to pass through the eye of a needle, the Arab nations would still not go to war with Israel.
