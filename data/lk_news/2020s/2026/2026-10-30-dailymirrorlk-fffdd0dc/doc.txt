Several petitions challenge constitutionality of Prevention of Organised Crime Bill

Colombo, Oct 30 (Daily Mirror) - Several Special Determination petitions have been filed in the Supreme Court challenging the constitutionality of the proposed Prevention of Organised Crime Bill, claiming that certain provisions of the legislation would undermine judicial independence, deprive suspects of their right to a fair trial and create conditions conducive to torture.

Among the petitioners is the Law and Society Trust (LST), which has sought a determination that the entire Bill, or several of its provisions, is inconsistent with the Constitution and therefore requires a two-thirds majority in Parliament and approval by the People at a referendum to become law.

The Prevention of Organised Crimes Bill, published as a Gazette Supplement issued on October 6, 2026 and tabled in Parliament on October 20, aims to give effect to the United Nations Convention Against Transnational Organised Crime and facilitate national and international cooperation in preventing, investigating and prosecuting serious offences.

The LST contends that the proposed bill was prepared without proper and adequate public consultation, despite its potential impact on individual liberties and the criminal justice system.

The organisation argues that the proposed legislation introduces uncertainty by failing to define the term “organised crime”, while granting extensive powers to the police to arrest, search and question individuals suspected of committing serious crimes.

The petitioner states that Clause 3 limits the application of the proposed law to serious crimes committed by members of organised criminal groups. However, Clause 17 empowers police officers to arrest any person on reasonable suspicion of having committed a serious crime, regardless of whether that person is a member of an organised criminal group.

The petitioner stated that clause 17 grants any police officer the power to arrest any person for suspicion of committing a serious crime, while clause 20 removes the power of the Magistrate to grant bail or question the grounds of arrest when the suspect is first produced before him.

The petition further contends that the Bill would effectively transfer judicial powers to the Executive by restricting the discretion of Magistrates when suspects are produced before them.

Under Clause 20, a Magistrate is required to give effect to a detention order issued by the Executive or remand the suspect if no such order is produced. The petition states that this would prevent Magistrates from independently examining the grounds for arrest and exercising their judicial discretion.

Alienation of judicial power by permitting facts not to be reported to Court

It also challenges clause 32(2), as a police officer who produces a suspect arrested under this law before a Magistrate in the first instance is not required to furnish a B report to court.

According to the petitioner, this would deprive Magistrates of essential information required to examine the circumstances of an arrest.

The petitioner also raised concerns over provisions governing the admissibility of evidence. It argues that Clauses 46 to 50 remove important safeguards under the Evidence Ordinance, including restrictions on the admissibility of confessions and statements made by suspects.

The petition further claims that Clause 65 could undermine attorney-client confidentiality by requiring individuals to disclose information relating to serious crimes notwithstanding obligations of secrecy imposed by law or professional duties.

It argues that this provision could expose Attorneys-at-Law representing suspects to criminal liability for maintaining professional confidentiality, thereby discouraging suspects from obtaining legal representation.

Another major concern raised in the petition relates to detention powers under Clauses 21, 22, 25 and 27.

The petitioner states that the proposed law permits suspects to be detained at police stations for periods extending up to one year, for up to six months in certain circumstances involving persons already in remand or prison custody, or until the conclusion of a trial.

It argues that prolonged detention at police stations, coupled with the proposed admissibility of certain confessions and statements, could create conditions conducive to torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment, in violation of Article 11 of the Constitution.

The petitioner further maintained that the cumulative effect of these provisions would undermine the separation of powers, the sovereignty of the People and fundamental rights guaranteed by the Constitution.

Counsel Ermiza Tegal with Thahira Cader, Mark Schubert, Vihangi Liyanagamage, Nisara Wickramasinghe, Oshadhi Ganisha Hapuarachchi, Alagaratnam Jinoshan instructed by Tharmarajah Tharmaja appeared for the petitioner.
