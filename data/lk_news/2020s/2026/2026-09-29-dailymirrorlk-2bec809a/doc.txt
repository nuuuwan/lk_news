Do not politicise the Anojan issue

Now, people attempt to  gain political mileage out of this poor man’s misery.

Sri Lanka has launched a diplomatic cum legal battle to save the life of one of its migrant workers who has been sentenced to death in Saudi Arabia over blasphemy against Islam.

Sivarasa Anojan, a 26-year-old Sri Lankan migrant worker from Kalmunai, faces a death sentence by a Saudi appellate court after prosecutors successfully appealed to have his initial cybercrime sentence upgraded under strict Sharia law.

Anojan was working as a quantity surveyor in Saudi Arabia when he was arrested on July 2 over a Facebook comment deemed derogatory toward Islam and the Prophet Muhammad. On July 15, the Al-Ahsa Criminal Court initially convicted him under Saudi Arabia’s Anti-Cyber Crime Law, sentencing him to five years in prison and a fine of 3 million Saudi riyals, the maximum punishment given under that law.

While an appeal was lodged to reduce the penalty, the Saudi prosecution filed a parallel appeal arguing the offense warranted prosecution under strict Sharia law for blasphemy. An appellate court subsequently overturned the initial custodial sentence and reportedly handed down a death penalty.

According to available reports, Anojan is the seventh Sri Lankan sentenced to death by Saudi Arabian courts. In the first case, four Sri Lankan youth, Ranjith de Silva, Victor Corea, Sanath Pushpakumara and Sermila Sangeeth Kumara were beheaded in 2007 for highway robbery of a Bangladeshi man in 2005.

In a second incident,   Saudi Arabia executed a Sri Lankan domestic worker, Rizana Nafeek for allegedly killing a baby in her care in 2005. Rizana was convicted in 2007 of murdering four-month-old baby Naif al-Quthaibi and executed in January, 2013, despite an eight year-long effort by international human rights organisations and the Sri Lankan government which made several pleas to the Saudi king for clemency.

The third incident involved a 45-year-old married Sri Lankan mother of two who worked as a domestic helper in Riyadh. She was convicted of adultery in August 2015 by a court in Al-Dwadmi which also sentenced her to capital punishment. Following diplomatic interventions, In December 2015, a Saudi appeals court agreed to reopen the case, following the Sri Lankan government’s requests to the Saudi authorities for clemency. The execution order was then cancelled, and her sentence was reduced to three years in prison.

Anojan’s case is the fourth incident. Those who politicise everything under the sun seem to compare the cases involving the 45-year-old woman and Anojan for political reasons, even though the four cases were filed under four different charges. The four youth were charged with robbery, while Rizana was charged with homicide. The 45-year-old woman whose identity was not revealed either by the Sri Lankan or Saudi authorities had been sentenced for adultery. Now Anojan has been sentenced to death for blasphemy.

Anojan’s case is a classic example of mishandling social media. The events leading to this sad episode commenced with this year’s Tamil pilgrims’ annual pada yathra from Jaffna to Kataragama. Influenced by the Story of ‘Aloka’ the dog that accompanied  Buddhist monks on an international Walk of Peace, the Tamil pilgrims named a dog that accompanied them to Kataragama ‘Subramani’.

It is said that a Muslim in Kalmunai had kicked the dog after it urinated in his garden,  and this triggered  Facebook  religious insults between  Sri Lankan Hindus and Muslims. Although it is difficult to find out who started the exchange of derogatory posts;  it was Anojan who ultimately faced the worst of the consequences.  Now, people attempt to  gain political mileage out of this poor man’s misery. Some politicians want to score points in the event of Anojan’s release or  commutation of his sentence. Some people attempt to use his sentence to further religious strike. These shortsighted actions might further aggravate the situation.

In essence, the irony is that religion — whatever it may be — is being used to divide humanity, directly opposing its primary objective of bringing people together. Happily,  a large majority of Sri Lankan Muslims also seem to support clemency for Anojan.

Social media users and those obsessed with politics must have two concerns at this juncture: not disrupting  efforts to save the life of the man languishing in the Saudi jail, and preserving  highly fragile communal harmony.
