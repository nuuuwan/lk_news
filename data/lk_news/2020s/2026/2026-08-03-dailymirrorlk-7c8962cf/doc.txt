Overreacting to student anger during India’s ‘Cockroach’ Protests

The degree of violence applied by police forces during unarmed protests depends a great deal on the kind of orders they get from their governments. This does not mean that in democracies, the police will always act with restraint

The sheer scale of India’s student protests may have come as a surprise to the Indian government, but it was no surprise to those observers in India,  as well as outsiders,  who monitored mounting student anger and frustration over high cost of education, cost of living and injustices in the education system over the years.

Indian social media is full of reports about students groaning under multiple economic burdens --  the cost of living in India’s sprawling cities due to high rents,  other expenses and a system unforgiving to those who make mistakes. One recent video shows a father and daughter pleading outside the closed gates of an examination centre to let them in. The gates were closed because they had arrived two minutes late, after travelling a long way on a motorcycle,  delayed due to rain and a flat tyre on the way.

Earlier, seeking jobs in UK was an alternative for many young Indians. But new and draconian immigration laws has led to many Indian youth working at UK jobs  in medical and IT sectors being deported. Last year, the Home Office passed a harsh new law prohibiting the employment of any foreign national whose arrival and UK visas were found to be  facilitated by  unregistered agencies. If unable to find alternative jobs within 30 days, they would be deported. This is harsh treatment of young hopefuls who spend minor fortunes looking for greener pastures, often getting into serious debt to pay agency sharks.

After two months of unrelenting protests, when tens of thousands of Gen Z protesters led by a spontaneous movement called the ‘Cockroach Janta Party’ clashed with police in New Delhi and other Indian cities, India’s autocratic Prime Minister Narendra Modi had to relent, and let his education minister and trusted Modi lieutenant Dharmendra Pradhan resign, whose removal was the protesters’ principal demand.

The vehemence with which Indian police cracked down on protesters shocked many. Indian police personnel wielding  sticks (or canes) is a frequent sight in India. There are videos showing policemen carrying them even outside cinema halls, trying to keep order as frantic fans try to get tickets. But the brutality shown towards these young students, both male and female, was highly disturbing. One video showed a senior officer slapping a young woman who was merely standing by, not doing anything aggressive.

But police brutality in riot situations is not unique to India. We have seen it often enough in Sri Lanka. A press photo showing a policeman using a baton on several female student protesters in Colombo who were crouching down, trying to protect themselves with their hands, caused an uproar. During the atter stages of the 2022 Aragalaya, the police used rubber bullets as well as live bullets, killing a protester in the town of Rambukkana.

Indian police have been accused of using pellet guns during the ‘Cockroach’ protests. A police officer was suspended in Bihar for using an assault rifle to disperse student protesters, and the chief justice of India called for an independent investigation after a petition was filed in the supreme court alleging police brutality at the Delhi protest, stating that the “right to peaceful, lawful protest is absolutely guaranteed” under the Indian constitution.

The degree of violence applied by police forces during unarmed protests depends a great deal on the kind of orders they get from their governments. This does not mean that in democracies, the police will always act with restraint. But there is clearly a difference between how China’s security forces reacted to peaceful protests in Tiananmen Square in June 1989 – according to independent estimates, over 2000 protesters were killed – and how India handled the protesters. At Tiananmen, it became a was a military rather than a police action.

In Iran, During massive civil unrest in January, police and security forces killed between 3000-40,000 people, and arrested protesters were executed on what human rights groups say were false charges.

These examples do not exonerate Indian police from what they did, but the kind of massacres reported from China and Iran would be unacceptable in India or Sri Lanka during a similar civil crisis. As Sri Lanka’s  youth-led Aragalaya gathered force in April-2022, many feared a strong backlash from ‘strongman’ President Gotabhaya Rajapakse who had full support of the military. But he hesitated to unleash heavy-handed police/military action on protesters possibly because he was unnerved by the sheer magnitude of public anger over his ruinous economic policies.

It is generally believed that police forces react with more restraint in Western countries, as they are better trained and equipped. But all one can say is – it depends. British police, often outnumbered, acted with restraint during Britain’s 2025 anti-immigration riots. But in the US, the police are often trigger-happy, and have an unsavoury history of shoot-first approach especially towards African-Americans.

Protests, riots, and demonstrations erupted against police brutality  in Minneapolis in the United States on May 26, 2020, after the murder of George Floyd, a 46-year-old unarmed African American, by city police during an arrest. Police officer Derek Chauvin was recorded as kneeling on Floyd’s neck for 9 minutes and 29 seconds; Floyd complained of not being able to breathe, and later died. Chauvin was convicted of murder.

But an already bad  situation in the US became darker after Donald Trump became president and began his war against immigrants. He unleashed US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) police on immigrants and protesters alike. ICE agents raid, operate and react outside legal limits and with excessive force, and have killed roughly 10 people in fatal operational shootings while  over 90 people have died in ICE detention centres since 2020. These are gross human rights violations in a country which preaches human rights to the rest of the world.

However, getting back to India, what happens now in the US or what happened in Tiananmen do not justify the way India’s young ‘Cockroach’ protesters were beaten by India’s  police. That has to be judged in its own context.
