Rs. 3.5 billion Indian chilli imports held up at port; exporters seek diplomatic intervention

Colombo, Nov. 19 (Daily Mirror) - As many as 120 containers of dry chillies valued at Rs. 3.5 billion, imported from India, have remained at the Colombo Port for nearly two months due to the consignments moisture content exceeding permissible levels under Sri Lankan standards, with local importers pressing for their immediate release and Indian exporters seeking diplomatic intervention to resolve the matter, an official said.

The situation has resulted in a surge in dry chilli prices in the local market, while chilli powder manufacturers are struggling to meet demand.

The Chillies Exporters Association of India has already informed its members, including chilli exporters, traders and other stakeholders, to suspend exports of red dry chillies to Sri Lanka until further notice.

The decision is intended to address the current concerns affecting the Indian red dry chilli trade and protect the interests of exporters, traders and other stakeholders in the industry. All members and associated exporters have been requested to take note of the decision and extend their cooperation. They have also been asked to refrain from entering into new export commitments to Sri Lanka during the suspension period, subject to their existing contractual obligations, according to a press release issued by the association last month.

Meanwhile, the organisation has written to Andhra Pradesh Chief Minister N. Chandrababu Naidu, seeking his intervention to resolve the matter with the Sri Lankan authorities through diplomatic channels.

“We, the Chillies Exporters Association of India, representing exporters of whole dried red chillies from Andhra Pradesh and other chilli-growing regions, seek your immediate intervention regarding the prolonged detention of Indian dry chilli consignments at Sri Lankan ports. Several consignments have been detained for over 60 days on moisture-related observations. Each shipment is valued at more than Rs. 40 lakh INR. The prolonged detention is causing serious financial hardship to exporters through the loss of working capital, mounting demurrage and container charges, the risk of quality deterioration and disruption of established trade,” it said.

“A large number of exporters and farmers from Andhra Pradesh are severely affected. We earnestly seek your strong support for urgent high-level diplomatic action to resolve this matter and ensure the smooth flow of agricultural trade between India and Sri Lanka,” it added.

Asked about the current status, Secretary of the Essential Food Commodity Importers and Traders Association P. Subramanium said his organisation had taken up the matter with the government, but without success.

He said samples were usually tested by the Sri Lanka Standards Institution and the Food Control Administration of the Health Ministry for moisture and aflatoxin levels.

According to him, whole dried chillies are naturally hygroscopic and can absorb moisture during transit and prolonged storage under humid conditions.

“In Sri Lanka, the humidity level is very high. It is challenging to meet the current specifications regarding moisture content under the weather conditions triggered by El Niño. The permissible level is set at 12 per cent. We are asking the authorities to increase it to at least 14 per cent,” he said.

Moisture content and aflatoxin are two distinct parameters and must be assessed separately, he stressed.

Explaining the nature of the product, he said dried chilli was made from green chilli and was not entirely devoid of moisture, despite being classified as a dried product.

Asked whether there were alternative markets from which Sri Lanka could source its imports, he said local consumers were accustomed to the taste of locally produced and Indian chillies.

“We can import from China or Pakistan. But Sri Lankan consumers are not used to the taste. If we import from China, it has to be done in deep-freezer containers. Then, the moisture level will rise further,” he said.

While acknowledging that high moisture content could lead to an increase in aflatoxin levels in the product, he argued that the existing Sri Lankan standard was unsuitable for a country with high humidity levels.

The situation has directly affected consumers, with dry chilli prices rising to around Rs. 1,800 per kilo in the retail market. Prices in supermarkets are even higher, exceeding Rs. 2,000 per kilo.
