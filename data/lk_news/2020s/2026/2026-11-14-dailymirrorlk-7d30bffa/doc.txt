Draconian Bills meet the test of democracy

The government, despite its fiery and brave political rhetoric, buoyed by its two-thirds majority, introduced two draconian pieces of legislation – the Prevention of Organised Crimes Bill and the Protection of the State from Terrorism Bill (PSTB).

The two proposed laws speak volumes about the government’s intentions – the suppression of dissent in a bid to consolidate a power base which is on the wane due to its failure to deliver. Alongside this, come what may, lawmakers should bear in mind that basic principles must be adhered to in the legislative process. A law should conform to the norms of the civilised world. Given the original contents of these two Bills, one wonders whether the government had an iota of concern for such principles in drafting them.

If that had been the case, the government would not have allowed connections to organised crime to be inferred from a person’s tattoos, manner of dress and associations under the Organised Crimes Bill.

Once the Bills were presented to Parliament and entered the public domain, aggrieved parties began challenging their constitutionality. Civil society activism peaked, with various groups lobbying for the scrapping or dilution of their provisions.

The Daily Mirror, as a newspaper that stands for the democratic rights of citizens, gave prominence to the issue in its coverage. Court proceedings were widely reported in a bid to mount pressure on the government to budge from its adamant stand.

Such media coverage and civil society activism have yielded results. The government has got cold feet and backtracked. It is now planning to introduce several amendments to the controversial Prevention of Organised Crimes Bill at the parliamentary committee stage. These include the removal of a provision allowing links to organised crime to be inferred from tattoos, dress and personal associations, as well as the removal of restrictions on media reporting of serious crimes. The Attorney General informed the Supreme Court of these proposed changes.

Solicitor General Viraj Dayaratne, appearing for the Attorney General, made this submission when the Supreme Court commenced hearing Special Determination petitions challenging the constitutionality of the proposed legislation. He said the amendments would be incorporated into the Bill at the parliamentary committee stage.

The matter was taken up before a three-judge bench comprising Chief Justice Preethi Padman Surasena and Justices Achala Wengappuli and Sampath Abayakoon.

The government also intends to remove the restriction on media reporting relating to serious crimes under Clause 14(3), which had raised concerns over its potential impact on press freedom and the public’s right to information. Under this provision, publishing such material without prior permission of the court was categorised as an offence punishable by up to two years’ imprisonment.

“Any person who prints or publishes any matter in relation to any proceeding in any court with respect to a serious crime under this Act, without the previous permission of such court, commits an offence and shall on conviction after summary trial before a Magistrate, be liable to a term of imprisonment of either description for a term not exceeding two years or to a fine or to both such imprisonment and fine.”

The Solicitor General further informed the Court that the definition of a ‘serious crime’ would be narrowed to cover offences listed in the First Schedule, punishable by more than four years’ imprisonment, when committed by a member of an organised criminal group.

He emphasised that the provisions of the proposed legislation would apply only to structured groups rather than individuals acting independently.

The government also proposed amendments to Clause 20(1) of the Bill, which empowers the police to detain a suspect for 72 hours, reducing the period to 48 hours.

One thing is clear: no powerful government can bulldoze its way past the checks and balances enshrined in the Constitution. Civil society activism, backed by responsible media, holds sway. Today, the government has relented, like a dog tucking its tail between its hind legs.

Civil society activism, coupled with responsible media scrutiny and judicial oversight, has once again demonstrated why democratic checks and balances matter. This is a lesson the government must not forget, particularly when it embarks on the exercise of drafting a new Constitution.
