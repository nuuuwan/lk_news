Let tax be collected, without making compliance a burden

By Charmaine Tillekeratne​ - Partner, Head of Tax, Deloitte Sri Lanka and Maldives

Colombo, Sep. 9 (Daily Mirror) - Sri Lanka’s tax administration recorded a strong revenue performance in 2025. The Inland Revenue Department collected Rs. 3,221.55 billion against an estimate of Rs. 3,093.45 billion, achieving 104.14 per cent of the target. Advance Income Tax, including withholding tax, yielded Rs. 175.79 billion, of which Rs. 71.62 billion came from interest. These figures show that collection at source is an effective and dependable part of the revenue system.

Yet,the success of collection should not be measured only by the amount raised. A sound tax system must also consider the cost, difficulty and anxiety imposed on people who are willing to pay what is properly due. For many middle-income taxpayers whose only income is employment income and interest, the tax itself is not the principal objection. Their frustration lies in the process that follows. obtaining withholding certificates and interest schedules from every bank and finance company, reconciling those records, completing an electronic return and, often, paying a professional merely to confirm tax that has already been deducted at source.

A taxpayer may maintain a salary account, several fixed deposits, a savings account and perhaps an account with a bank /finance company. Filing a complete return can therefore require separate information from each institution. The taxpayer must check gross interest, Advance Income Tax deducted, account details and the relevant period. Where certificates are delayed, incomplete or issued in different formats, even a simple tax profile becomes a document-gathering exercise.

This is especially difficult for senior citizens. The thought of contacting or visiting several financial institutions, requesting certificates, following up on missing information and then navigating an online return can itself be distressing. Some older taxpayers face mobility or health limitations. Even where a tax consultant is engaged, the consultant cannot prepare an accurate return without the underlying bank information. The taxpayer or a family member must still gather it, and the professional fee further reduces the savings income on which the person may depend.

Digital filing is useful, but digitising a complicated obligation does not necessarily simplify it. The IRD’s own 2025 report recognises low tax and digital literacy as barriers and records an individual income tax on-time filing rate of only 25 per cent for the year of assessment 2024/2025. The report also states that a simplified return is available for individuals with straightforward income profiles, including employment income and interest with AIT deducted. That is a welcome step, but a shorter return does not eliminate the need to obtain and reconcile the supporting information.

The approach proposed here is not without precedent in Sri Lankan income tax law. Section 106(1) of the Inland Revenue Act No. 10 of 2006, under the heading “Returns and information to be furnished”, contained a proviso under which the general return-filing requirement did not apply where an individual’s income consisted solely of one or a combination of specified categories. This included employment, provided income tax had been deducted by the employer, together with dividends taxed at source and interest on which income tax had been deducted under sections.

Where a resident individual has only employment income and interest income, and employment tax has been deducted correctly, the taxpayer should be permitted to make an annual declaration to the relevant financial institutions specifying the appropriate rate at which AIT should be withheld from interest. The rate should be determined by reference to the individual’s expected total taxable income and the applicable progressive personal income tax rates. If the declaration is accurate and the tax is deducted accordingly, the tax withheld on interest should be treated as final and no annual income tax return should be required solely because of that employment and interest income.

This proposal is different from the present relief declaration for people with no taxable income. Under the current framework, AIT on interest is generally deducted at 10 per cent from 1 April 2025. A resident individual whose assessable income from all sources does not exceed the Rs. 1.8 million personal relief may submit a self-declaration to seek relief from AIT on interest. The broader reform proposed here would allow a taxpayer with taxable employment and interest income to declare an appropriate withholding rate so that the final amount deducted more closely reflects the person’s progressive tax liability.

Such a system would not excuse tax. It would collect tax earlier, at source, while removing a return-filing obligation that adds little value for a taxpayer with only two transparent income streams. Banks and employers already operate withholding systems, and the IRD is strengthening its use of third-party information and risk-based data analysis. IRD can even use the data under APIT declarations to cross check on accuracy of self-declarations made. The policy question is therefore whether the administration should require every ordinary saver to reproduce information that regulated institutions can report directly.

A sensible reform should retain compulsory return filing for taxpayers whose circumstances warrant closer review. One option is a clearly legislated interest-income threshold. For example, a person earning interest of Rs. 400,000 or more a month, equivalent to Rs. 4.8 million a year, could be required to file a return regardless of tax deducted at source. The Rs. 400,000 figure is an illustrative policy threshold, not a threshold in the current law. Before adoption, it should be tested against prevailing interest rates, deposit values, personal relief, revenue impact and the IRD’s definition and monitoring of high-wealth individuals.

Returns should also remain mandatory where the taxpayer has business income, rent, capital gains, foreign-source income, multiple employments, deductible claims requiring verification, a refund claim, unexplained asset growth or any other prescribed risk factor. The final-tax treatment should be a targeted simplification for genuinely straightforward cases, not a route for complex or high-value income to escape scrutiny.

Any self-declaration regime must be backed by strict and credible penalties. A person who knowingly understates expected income, omits accounts, supplies a false withholding rate or fails to update a declaration after a material change should be liable for the tax shortfall, interest and a substantial additional penalty. Deliberate false declarations should also disqualify the person from using the simplified regime for a specified period and should permit audit or prosecution where the conduct amounts to evasion.

Safeguards should be matched by fair taxpayer protections. The declaration form and rate calculation must be simple and standardised. Taxpayers should be allowed to revise a declaration when income changes. Financial institutions should report gross interest and tax deducted directly to the IRD using the taxpayer’s TIN, and the taxpayer should receive a consolidated annual statement. There should also be a clear correction and refund process for genuine mistakes. Strong penalties are most defensible when the compliant route is easy to understand and use.

The IRD’s 2025 report shows both progress and the need for reform. It records 6,893 audits commenced, 6,350 finalised and Rs. 64.98 billion of additional tax collected through audit activity. It also reports risk-based audit selection using internal and external data and 100 per cent individual income tax return compliance across the 100 files assigned to the High Wealth Individuals Unit. These capabilities can be directed toward cases that present real revenue risk, rather than requiring large numbers of compliant employees, retirees and ordinary savers to undertake repetitive documentation exercises.

Sri Lanka needs revenue, and citizens must contribute according to law. But compliance should be designed around the reality of taxpayers’ lives. For an employee or pensioner with only salary or pension and interest, accurate deduction at source can achieve the State’s revenue objective without an annual ritual of collecting certificates, paying advisers and filing a return that repeats information already available within the financial system.

A carefully legislated final-tax option, combined with a rational filing threshold, direct third-party reporting and stringent penalties for false declarations, would strike a better balance. It would protect revenue, reduce avoidable costs and make voluntary compliance more credible. The best tax system is not the one that demands the most paperwork. It is the one that collects the right tax, from the right person, with the least unnecessary burden.
