Sri Lanka’s GDP growth can mask loss of natural wealth: Statistics Chief

Sri Lanka’s economy can grow even as the country depletes the forests, water resources and fertile soils that support key industries, the head of its statistics department said, outlining an initiative to track those losses alongside gross domestic product.

Upper watersheds and coastal and marine ecosystems will be the initial focus of the initiative, Department of Census and Statistics Director General U.V. Jayakody said at a workshop organised by the Centre for Poverty Analysis with support from the Asian Development Bank.

Jayakody cited timber harvesting as an example of what GDP can miss. The value of harvested timber counts towards economic output, while the depletion of the forest is only partly reflected in conventional statistics.

“Consequently, the country may experience economic growth while its natural wealth is gradually declining,” he said.

The proposed natural capital accounts would track changes in the availability and condition of resources and measure the services they provide. Watershed accounts could help assess the contribution of those ecosystems to water supplies, agriculture and hydropower generation. Coastal and marine accounts could do the same for fisheries, tourism and coastal protection.

The accounts would complement existing national accounts under a United Nations framework that combines environmental and economic data. Jayakody said physical measures of the condition of natural assets would be as important as monetary estimates. The data could inform development planning, investment decisions and environmental protection.

Sri Lanka’s 2026–2030 statistics strategy envisages turning the department into a National Statistics Office with a broader role in coordinating data across government. Jayakody said producing the accounts regularly would require agencies including the Forest Department, the Central Environmental Authority and the Survey Department to share and update data using common standards.

“We need to understand not only how fast our economy is growing today, but also whether we are preserving the natural resources that will sustain that growth in the future,” he said.
