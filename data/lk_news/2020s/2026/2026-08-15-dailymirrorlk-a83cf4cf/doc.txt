CHARTING THE NEXT WATCH

“Behold the true inheritance of military service: not steel, but character; not rank, but example; not power, but trust.”

“Victory demanded imagination, resilience, and officers and sailors prepared to think beyond established doctrine when doctrine proved insufficient.”

“Geography has blessed Sri Lanka, but geography guarantees nothing; strategic relevance belongs not merely to those well placed, but to those well prepared.”

Delivering the 2026 Gen. Denis Perera Memorial Oration, Navy Commander Vice Admiral Damian Fernando reflects on 75 years of naval inheritance, the lessons of asymmetric warfare, and the strategic imperative of preparing for tomorrow’s maritime challenges

If I were to distil this evening’s reflections into a single phrase, it would be ‘Our Watch’—a simple concept carrying the accumulated weight of 75 years of naval service.

Tonight, our thoughts turn first to General Deshamanya Denis Perera—a soldier and institution-builder whose legacy cannot be measured merely by appointments, honours, or ribbons. Such things adorn a career; they do not define it. General Denis Perera understood a fundamental truth: individuals are transient, institutions endure, and beneath both lies something even more enduring—character. Behold the true inheritance of military service: not steel, but character; not rank, but example; not power, but trust.

The General Sir John Kotelawala Defence University, Diyathalawa Military Academy, the Women’s Corps, and the Commando Regiment stand as enduring testimonials to that conviction—reminders that the greatest monuments are built in minds, traditions, and standards that successive generations refuse to compromise. His legacy presents every generation with an inescapable question: Are we merely preparing for today’s challenges, or are we building institutions capable of confronting tomorrow’s uncertainties? No institution preserves its relevance by remaining static.

For a sailor, the watch is never merely an entry upon a duty roster. When one watch ends, another begins, and responsibility passes from one pair of hands to the next. It is an inheritance. While others sleep, another must remain awake, read the horizon, anticipate danger, and hold the ship steadily upon her course.

That institution was tested by nearly three decades of conflict against the LTTE Sea Tigers, an adaptive maritime threat employing asymmetric tactics, unconventional thinking, and relentless innovation. There was no established doctrine or convenient precedent. Our sailors learned while fighting, adapted under fire, and evolved as the adversary changed. Courage alone could never have secured success; victory demanded imagination, resilience, and officers and sailors prepared to think beyond established doctrine.

Out of necessity emerged innovation. Small-craft operations were refined, maritime intelligence matured, special operations expanded, and long-range interdiction evolved beyond our immediate waters. The campaign against the LTTE’s floating logistics network became a defining maritime contribution to restoring peace. The permanent lesson was clear: institutions that learn more rapidly than their adversaries acquire an advantage which no treaty can confer and no budget can purchase.

When the guns fell silent in 2009, our responsibilities did not diminish; they expanded. Today, our maritime responsibilities extend far beyond conventional defence to encompass IUU fishing, illegal migration, narcotics smuggling, human trafficking, maritime terrorism, environmental crime, humanitarian assistance, and regional cooperation.

Our generation has inherited a different challenge. We must ensure that the Sri Lanka Navy remains strategically indispensable in an age where change arrives not by decades, but by months—and where complacency is the most dangerous adversary of any professional institution.

The Indian Ocean has once again become a principal arena where commerce, influence, technology, and strategic competition converge. Nearly two-thirds of the world’s seaborne petroleum passes through this ocean, while almost half of global container traffic traverses these waters. Regional naval investment is accelerating rapidly. Geography has blessed Sri Lanka, but geography guarantees nothing; strategic relevance belongs not merely to those well placed, but to those well prepared.

Our future cannot be secured merely by becoming a larger Navy; arithmetic alone has never produced strategic advantage. We must become a more intelligent Navy: one that anticipates before reacting, innovates without abandoning tradition, and masters emerging technologies without surrendering professional judgement.

Agility: The discipline to recognise change before change compels a response.

Credibility: Earned through professionalism, patrol by patrol, exercise by exercise, and decision by decision.

Strategic Relevance: History records navies that disappeared not because they were defeated, but because they ceased to matter.

Agility without credibility becomes recklessness; credibility without agility becomes stagnation. Together, they form true strategy: the disciplined habit of preparing today for challenges that have not yet announced their arrival. Today, artificial intelligence, autonomous systems, quantum technologies, cyber capabilities, and space-based surveillance are redefining maritime operations. Yet while the instruments evolve, our core purpose remains constant: safeguard sovereignty, protect maritime interests, preserve freedom of navigation, reassure friends, and prevent conflict through professionalism and credibility.

We must embrace innovation, invest in research, and cultivate technological mastery. Artificial intelligence must sharpen decision-making, autonomous systems must extend human capability, and data must enrich professional judgement. But technology must never become a substitute for judgement itself. Technology may determine how we operate; it must never determine why we serve.

I pay tribute not only to those who wear the uniform, but equally to their families. In particular, I acknowledge Madam Rajini Perera and family, whose sacrifice accompanied General Denis Perera throughout his lifetime of service. They, too, kept the watch.

We inherited this watch from generations of Sri Lankan sailors whose courage was tested by war, whose professionalism was refined by adversity, and whose devotion to duty never sought recognition. Our duty is greater than preservation; it is improvement. We must leave behind a Navy more agile, credible, innovative, respected, and strategically relevant than the one we inherited. General Deshamanya Denis Perera understood that enduring truth. His greatest legacy was not simply the institutions he established, but the spirit of stewardship he instilled within them. The true measure of leadership lies not in preserving an inheritance unchanged, but in strengthening it before faithfully passing the watch to those who follow.

During my watch, I remain resolutely committed to steering our Navy towards a force that is future-ready in thought, credible in conduct, and strategically relevant to our nation, our region, and the wider maritime fraternity. History seldom punishes nations for failing to predict the future; it judges them more severely for recognising change too late. We cannot afford our own ‘Sputnik Moment’. We must act while foresight remains a choice.

The watch is not ours to possess; it is ours to preserve, strengthen, and pass on. May those who come after us find that we enlarged the inheritance placed in our hands. May they discover that we left the seas safer than we found them, the Service stronger than we inherited it, and the standard of leadership higher than the one expected of us. If another generation can say that we kept the watch faithfully, no greater tribute could be paid, no finer memorial could be built, and no more enduring legacy could be left. This is ‘Our Watch’. May we be worthy of it.

The twelfth death anniversary of Deshamanya Gen. Denis Perera fell on 11 August. This is the Denis Perera Memorial Oration 2026 delivered by Vice Admiral Damian Fernando RSP, USP, rcds, MA in International Security & Strategy (UK), MSc (DS) Mgt, AFNI, JP (Whole Island), the 27th Commander of the Sri Lanka Navy.
