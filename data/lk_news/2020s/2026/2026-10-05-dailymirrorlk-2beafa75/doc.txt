Budget 2027 must put dollar earnings first: FTZMA

Asserts country needs a sustained effort to develop new sources of foreign currency alongside measures to strengthen government finances and remove obstacles facing existing businesses

Argues that managing available foreign currency could not substitute for expanding the country’s earning capacity

Sri Lanka has never had difficulty finding lenders. Finding new earnings has been the harder task, and it is the one Budget 2027 must now take up - FTZMA

Sri Lanka’s free trade zone manufacturers (FTZMA) have called for Budget 2027 to make earning and retaining foreign exchange a national priority, proposing a mechanism to turn viable business ideas into projects, a tax credit for foreign investor reinvestment and upgrades to export zone infrastructure.

The Free Trade Zone Manufacturers’ Association (FTZMA), in its budget submission, argued that the country needed a sustained effort to develop new sources of foreign currency alongside measures to strengthen government finances and remove obstacles facing existing businesses.

Pointing to the country’s external debt obligations, the association said, “The repayment schedule for the coming decade is already fixed by contract. The earnings that will meet it are not.”

Its proposals centre on establishing a small, empowered team to receive ideas from businesses and individuals, assess their foreign exchange potential and coordinate the government decisions needed to take credible opportunities forward.

FTZMA maintained that a stronger foreign exchange position was essential for manufacturers to plan, invest and employ people with confidence, while also making Sri Lanka a more dependable destination for investors.

The association acknowledged the Central Bank’s responsibility to protect foreign exchange liquidity, noting that its members complied with the rules arising from that duty. However, it contended that managing available foreign currency could not substitute for expanding the country’s earning capacity.

“Those rules decide how the dollars the country already has are shared out. They cannot create the next one,” it said.

Better revenue collection and tighter public spending were necessary, FTZMA acknowledged, while stressing that the fiscal and external financing challenges required different responses. Foreign currency had to come from what Sri Lanka earned, retained or attracted from overseas.

“Sri Lanka has never had difficulty finding lenders. Finding new earnings has been the harder task, and it is the one Budget 2027 must now take up,” the association said.

The proposed national mechanism would perform three functions: invite ideas, provide a clear entry point for assessment and carry viable proposals through to a decision.

FTZMA called for an open invitation to businesses, entrepreneurs, professionals, researchers, public officers and Sri Lankans overseas to identify opportunities to earn or save foreign exchange. Applicants could also highlight rules preventing such activities.

According to the association, opportunities could emerge from an exporter identifying new demand, a professional developing a service for overseas markets, a diaspora member recognising an opening abroad or a public officer identifying an obstructive regulation.

Access to the mechanism should not depend on an applicant being a large or well-connected company, it stressed.

A short initial submission would assess the foreign exchange logic and practical readiness of an idea. Applicants should not have to prepare a full business plan before receiving an indication of whether their proposal merited further work.

“Most good ideas die in the paperwork long before they die on merit,” FTZMA said.

Once a proposal passed the initial assessment, a named officer would take responsibility for the case, coordinate the institutions involved within fixed time limits and see it through to a final answer.

The association noted that this would address a recurring problem in which a proposal involved several agencies, yet no institution took responsibility for securing an outcome. It stressed that the mechanism would not provide automatic approvals. Some proposals would require safeguards or redesign, while others would be rejected.

“We are not asking for automatic approvals, and we are not asking for another large institution. A small team with the authority to get answers out of other agencies would achieve far more than a new department with none,” it said.

Rejected applicants should receive written reasons, FTZMA added, pointing out that these could also expose regulations warranting reconsideration.

“An opportunity delayed for three years cannot earn, cannot learn, cannot attract investment and cannot grow,” the association said.

Alongside the approval mechanism, FTZMA proposed a common national measure called “Net Foreign Currency Contribution” to assess how much foreign exchange an activity actually contributes after accounting for the overseas expenditure it generates.

The measure would deduct the foreign currency outflows an activity generates from the inflows associated with it.

“Gross export and investment figures can flatter. Foreign currency comes in while large amounts go straight back out again through imported inputs, equipment, overseas services, software, licence fees and finance costs. The number that matters to the country is what remains,” FTZMA said.

The assessment would also recognise foreign exchange savings, including those achieved when a local supplier replaces an imported input.

FTZMA argued that measuring net contributions would help distinguish activities delivering a genuine foreign exchange benefit from those whose gross inflows overstated their contribution. It called for results to be published and checked against actual performance.

The association also proposed a special tax credit to encourage non-resident shareholders to retain and reinvest profits in Sri Lanka.

The proposed credit would be equal to the 15 percent tax on dividends paid to non-resident shareholders. FTZMA outlined three qualifying methods: reinvesting profits directly in an existing business without paying dividends; declaring dividends and reinvesting them as equity in the same business; or reinvesting declared dividends in another business venture in Sri Lanka.

FTZMA urged the government to prioritise improvements within and around free trade zones, saying investors needed facilities and integrated logistics ready before installing machinery.

It identified expressway access, water and supporting amenities as urgent requirements for rural export processing zones, suggesting that some locations could be developed as industrial townships with a wider range of facilities.

The association wanted these requirements incorporated into planned zones rather than a repeat of the blueprint used for traditional developments.

Existing zones also required better external logistics and amenities, alongside the renovation and modernisation of ageing and dilapidated internal infrastructure, it added.

These improvements would support expansions, encourage reinvestment and help attract new investors to established zones.

FTZMA described its broader objective as creating a continuing process for identifying new earning opportunities, rather than relying solely on removing obstacles facing businesses already operating.

“Sri Lanka’s durable economic independence will be built by what it can earn, retain and reinvest, not by how much it can borrow again,” it said.
