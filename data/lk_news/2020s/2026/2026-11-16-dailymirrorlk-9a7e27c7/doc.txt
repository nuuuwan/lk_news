Interpreting Budget 2027: Focus on priorities in an increasingly volatile world

The Budget does have disparate housing and agricultural development programmes, particularly in fisheries

In a world prone to various shocks in recent years, a focus on the food system with an emphasis on self-sufficiency remains an urgent priority within or outside the provisions of the Budget

Budgets provide government priorities for the next year, but they also point to the economic trajectory over the next many years. How do we interpret Budget 2027 announced few days ago, and more importantly, how do we engage with it towards social and economic change?

In the concluding section of the Budget Speech, the President has the following to say: “Criticise the Government. Question our policies. Present a better alternative programme than ours. We fully accept your democratic right to do so. But bring alternative policies instead of curse verses, creative solutions instead of sorcery …”

I believe we should take this call for engagement seriously. Because budgets and for that matter economic policies, including even the best of them, will not materialise without people’s engagement, participation and mobilisation. Indeed, unless there is dialogue, debate and struggle both within the state and in the hinterlands, demanding that provisions of the budget are implemented, changed or alternatives created, there will be little progress in our economic situation.

In other words, budgets are the basis for ideological struggle, for concrete demands and alternatives that should not only be posed to the Government but the public at large. In this column I address what is lacking, the significant provisions and the alternatives to be forged in relation to Budget 2027.

The Budget lacks strong provisions for self-sufficiency in food. While there are disparate initiatives to increase agricultural production, including for exports, a larger conception of the food system integrating input and output markets in agriculture and linking it to the distribution and consumption of food is missing. In a world prone to various shocks in recent years, a focus on the food system with an emphasis on self-sufficiency remains an urgent priority within or outside the provisions of the Budget.

The major gap in the Budget is employment creation. Sri Lanka for decades has not had credible labour and employment policies. Labour market analysis itself is non-existent. We know that the unemployment data is for the most part meaningless given how it is calculated. In the context of the long economic crisis that the country has been mired in, the lack of budgetary provisions to increase employment and revitalise livelihoods is worrying.

There are many subsidised loan schemes in the budget for MSMEs in the hope of creating employment, but from past experience we know that such subsidies will not create the levels of employment necessary to address the protracted economic crisis. Firms will merely use the subsidies to reduce their losses or increase their profits.

What is necessary is to increase effective demand through public investment that clearly seeks to multiply employment and strengthen the food system. The Budget has a static balance sheet approach towards surplus from revenues in relation to expenditure, rather than a dynamic conception of multipliers to increase production, employment and livelihoods. In budget and policy making, it is important to understand that the whole is greater than the sum of the parts, and the state should engage in counter-cyclical policies. In other words, the government’s role including its deficits and debt has very different dynamics from that of firms and households.

The Budget does have disparate housing and agricultural development programmes, particularly in fisheries. These can lead to some backward and forward linkages and create multiplier effects depending on how they are implemented. The sourcing of housing material locally and the use of labour in construction increasing household incomes creating demand for locally produced consumption goods is one possible outcome. Similarly, investment in physical infrastructure for small-scale fisheries, locally produced fishnets and provisions for marketing seafood avoiding extractive traders is another example.

The Budget is perhaps strongest in the much needed approach to energy transformation. Indeed, the large investment in energy infrastructure, particularly renewable energy production, storage and transmission as well as facilities for storing and refining fuel are encouraging measures given the energy shocks we are likely to see. The plans for electric trains and the broader shift away from fossil fuels that will also reduce the large import bill for fuel – in times when fuel prices are likely to be volatile – is welcome.

Sri Lanka historically has very low investment in research and development, and we have done little to manage our large natural resource base. The Budget seeks to both invest in and create a number of state institutions to address this gap lasting decades. The initiatives to address our mineral resources and exploration of oil and gas depend on how they are taken forward. It is important to have sovereign control over our natural resources, and ensure that multi-national corporations and extractive powers are kept away.

The investment in research and development, both to develop the energy sector and value addition with a sustainable outlook for the country’s natural resource base, is important. These initiatives should not be seen as individual projects, but rather have the outlook of developing systems that strengthen the social and economic life of our people.

It is no secret that Budget 2027 is constrained by the policy decision of the NPP Government when it came to power to remain within the IMF programme. The Public Finance Management Act and the numerous other laws that came as conditionalities of the IMF programme and its austerity push have shackled the Government’s capacity to grow the economy,  and for that matter to meet the needs of the citizenry.

It is critical that the Government does not go into another IMF programme after it comes to an end in March 2027. The many laws and constraints that came with the IMF programme should be amended or repealed. Those are the preconditions for setting the economy on the path of growth while serving the needs of the working people.

Sri Lanka has adequate resources, but we lack the institutions and the capacity for economic rejuvenation, in many ways due to the approach of past regimes in power and the neoliberal policies spanning close to five decades. Amidst an unravelling global order and years ahead bound to be times of turmoil, Sri Lanka needs to leave behind its history of dependency on external powers and forge a different economic programme. Sri Lanka as a small state – facing reckless global giants with little concern for consequences and middle powers guarding their own interests – needs to think of both its domestic policies and external relations in ways to ensure the welfare of its people.

We have a long history of free education and healthcare, and for many decades a solid food system that served our people well. There is a need to draw on that legacy of self-sufficiency, expand our universal social welfare programmes and consider our future economic transformation; particularly in relation to the food and energy systems that are increasingly vulnerable to global shocks. Sri Lanka needs to work with other countries, and where possible other small or progressive countries with whom we can create relations of equality and freedom.

Budget 2027 should not be seen as set in stone, but as an opportunity for critical engagement both within and outside the state. We need to chart an alternative economic trajectory grounded in social justice that depends not just on the Government, but also the engagement of movements – of youth, co-operatives and trade unions – and the working people more broadly.
