Sri Lanka’s elephants are paying for our failures!

Sri Lanka calls itself a land of elephants. They feature in our history, religion, tourism campaigns and national celebrations. Yet behind this proud image is a shameful reality: elephants are dying year after year, often because of human actions, while the country struggles to protect them. Respect must be measured by whether they are safe.

The figures should alarm every citizen. Department of Wildlife Conservation data reported by the Daily Mirror shows that 397 elephants died in 2025. Seventy-one were shot, 56 died from electrocution, 46 were killed in train accidents and 20 died after eating explosives hidden in food, known as hakka patas. Two were poisoned. In 2024, 386 elephants died, including 81 from gunshots, 56 from electrocution and 51 from these explosives.

Nor is this only a story about elephants. People are dying too. The conflict between humans and elephants has become a national emergency, especially in farming communities where families live with fear of crop damage, property loss and dangerous encounters. Reuters reported that, since 2019, an average of 138 people and 402 elephants have died each year in Sri Lanka in human-elephant conflict. In 2023, elephant deaths reached a record 488.

A recent report also estimated that around 70% of Sri Lanka’s 7,451 wild elephants live outside protected areas, where the risks are harder to manage.

Villagers who use harmful traps or explosives must be stopped, but officials must also ask why the conflict keeps growing. When forests are cleared, corridors blocked and settlements spread into habitats, elephants move in search of food and water. People then face the consequences of decisions made far from their villages.

Farmers need practical protection, timely compensation for losses, reliable early-warning systems and support to protect crops without harming wildlife. Electric fences must be placed where they can work, maintained properly and monitored.

The same failure is visible on railway lines. In 2024, 11 elephants died in train accidents, according to figures reported by the Daily Mirror. Authorities must address known danger spots with better warnings, appropriate speed controls and coordination between railway and wildlife officials. Investigations after a herd is killed are not enough.

Then there is the uncomfortable issue of elephants kept for religious and public processions. Sri Lanka has been drawn into a diplomatic dispute with Thailand over two elephants gifted decades ago. Thailand has renewed efforts to bring them home amid concerns about their health and working conditions. In 2023, Sak Surin, a Thai elephant returned to Thailand, was flown home after allegations of neglect and serious leg injuries. In October 2026, Thai authorities were again under pressure from animal rights activists to recall two more elephants in Sri Lanka.

This is more than a diplomatic embarrassment. An elephant is a living animal, not an object of prestige. A gift does not remove the duty of care. If an elephant is too old, ill or exhausted to work, it should be allowed to rest. Independent veterinary assessments, transparent reports and clear welfare standards are not insults to religious institutions or national pride. They are essential.

The government needs a public, regularly updated record of elephant deaths, human deaths, causes, locations and action taken. If an animal is killed by an explosive bait, an illegal electrical fence or a train, the response cannot end with a statement of regret. Those responsible must be held accountable, and the conditions that made the death possible must be addressed.

Protecting elephant corridors, restoring habitats, planning development responsibly, supporting farmers and improving railway safety must work together. Plans must use reliable data and local knowledge, and listen to wildlife researchers and affected communities.

Sri Lanka cannot celebrate elephants in temples and tourist brochures while failing to protect them in forests, villages and on railway tracks. Nor can we demand that people living beside wildlife accept danger without meaningful support. Both human lives and elephant lives matter. The country must move beyond speeches and emergency committees to a consistent plan, with deadlines, funding and public accountability.

If we continue as we are, more elephants will die, more families will grieve and our international reputation will suffer. The question is not whether Sri Lanka loves elephants. The question is whether we are prepared to change the way we develop, govern and care for them. Until our actions match our pride, calling this an elephant-loving nation will ring hollow.
