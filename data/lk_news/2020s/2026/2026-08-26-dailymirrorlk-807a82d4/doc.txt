WTTC projections put Sri Lanka’s US$ 10bn tourism target a decade behind schedule

Long-term forecast points to a US$ 3.54bn gap between organic revenue growth and govt.’s 2030 target

WTTC projects Sri Lanka’s tourism employment will grow from 991,000 jobs in 2025 to 1.52 mn by 2036

S L’s total tourism GDP outpaces the Maldives and Cambodia despite trailing both on pure tourism earnings

Sri Lanka’s travel and tourism sector will generate US$ 6.46 billion in international visitor spending by 2036, according to the World Travel & Tourism Council’s (WTTC) latest long-term forecast which is a US$ 3.54 billion shortfall from government’s target of US$ 10 billion in tourism revenue from five million arrivals by 2030.

The WTTC’s independent projections show steady but moderate organic growth: visitor exports are estimated to rise from US$ 3.73 billion in 2025 to US$ 4.49 billion in 2026, before reaching US$ 6.46 billion in 2036. Extrapolating the WTTC’s implied 2025–2036 growth rate forward, Sri Lanka would not reach the US$ 10 billion mark until around 2045 — some 15 years behind the government’s original 2030 target. The widening gap between these baseline forecasts and the state’s targets points to a structural challenge for tourism industry.

Sri Lanka’s tourism earnings fell 11.5 percent year-on-year to US$ 1.79 billion in the first seven months of 2026, per Central Bank of Sri Lanka data, leaving the industry needing to average roughly US$ 480 million a month for the rest of the year to hit the revised US$ 4.2 billion target.

Bridging that gap, industry analysts say, will require a shift from volume-driven tourism to high-yield value creation. The numbers illustrate the point: WTTC forecasts Sri Lanka’s visitor spending to grow 73 percent between 2025 and 2036 which is more than double the Maldives’ 31 percent — yet the Maldives, with its ultra-luxury positioning, is still projected to out-earn Sri Lanka in absolute terms through 2036, at US$ 7.31 billion against Sri Lanka’s US$ 6.46 billion. Regionally, Cambodia offers a tougher comparison still: already ahead of Sri Lanka in 2025 at US$ 4.58 billion, it is forecast to reach US$ 9.39 billion by 2036, over 45 percent above Sri Lanka’s projection.

Despite the shortfall against state targets, tourism’s broader economic footprint remains significant. WTTC forecasts the sector’s total contribution to GDP will rise from US$ 9.92 billion in 2025 to US$ 11.12 billion in 2026, and to US$ 16.62 billion by 2036 — lifting tourism’s share of whole-economy GDP from 9.3 percent to 10.3 percent over the same period.

That total GDP figure tells a different story from visitor exports alone. Even though Sri Lanka trails both the Maldives and Cambodia in pure international visitor spending by 2036, its total tourism GDP contribution — which captures indirect effects such as investment and domestic supply chains, and induced effects such as spending by tourism-sector workers, not just foreign exchange earnings — is considerably larger than either: nearly double the Maldives’ US$ 5.22 billion in 2025 and about 39 percent bigger than Cambodia’s US$ 7.15 billion, a gap that widens by 2036 to more than double the Maldives’ US$ 7.07 billion and roughly 20 percent ahead of Cambodia’s US$ 13.9 billion. The gap suggests Sri Lanka’s tourism sector, while smaller in pure export terms, is better anchored in the domestic economy — plausibly because resort-heavy, import-reliant markets like the Maldives generate less downstream domestic activity per tourist dollar spent.

Globally, the picture is upbeat. WTTC President and CEO Gloria Guevara said travel and tourism did not just recover in 2025 but rebounded stronger and faster than expected, with the sector contributing US$ 11.6 trillion — 9.8 percent of world GDP — after growing 4.1 percent. WTTC forecasts global growth of 3.6 percent a year through 2036, taking the sector’s contribution to US$ 17.1 trillion, or 11 percent of the global economy.

Meeting Sri Lanka’s targets and keeping pace with that momentum, however, will hinge on clearing operational bottlenecks. Guevara said potential cannot be realised without close public-private collaboration, warning that labour shortages, skills gaps and limited visa access for workforce mobility are already constraining the sector in some countries. In Sri Lanka, total travel and tourism employment is projected to grow from 991,000 jobs in 2025 to 1.52 million by 2036.

Supporting that expansion — and justifying premium pricing — will require sustained investment in hospitality training, Guevara said, adding that countries treating tourism as a national strategic priority, with investment-friendly policies, stronger connectivity and reduced friction across the traveller journey, consistently gain a competitive edge. The public-private partnerships built today, she said, will define the next era of global travel and tourism.

The findings are drawn from WTTC’s Economic Impact Research: Global Trends Report, produced in collaboration with Oxford Economics and sponsored this year by Chase Travel as Lead Research Partner. The WTTC is the global body representing the private sector across travel and tourism, with its Council made up of the chairs, presidents and chief executives of the industry’s leading companies. Its annual Economic Impact Research tracks the direct, indirect and induced contribution of travel and tourism to GDP and employment across 184 countries and 28 regions worldwide.
