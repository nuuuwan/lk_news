Midterms deliver  wake-up call to America’s two major parties

Abdul El-Sayed embraces his wife, Dr Sarah Jukaku, onstage after declaring victory at a midterm election night party in Detroit, Michigan. Pic AFP

The midterm election results were more of a wake-up call for both Democrats and Republicans than merely a means of sending new members to the US legislature. The American people have spoken, and they have spoken through the ballot box more loudly than the voices of dissent that erupted in Paris and other major European cities weeks ago.

In Paris, the youth-led protests reflected public anger against politicians and turned unruly. In the United States, however, it was a silent and orderly revolt against the political establishment, one that neither party can afford to ignore and must instead take stock of and learn from.

For the first time since the Vietnam War, during which the current billionaire President Donald Trump allegedly evaded the draft five times, US foreign policy had become so intricately linked to domestic issues in an election. The post-mortems say so loud and clear.

Just as much as the Iran war and America’s blind support for Israel, rising living costs, corruption, and the moral decadence of the presidency helped decide the election.

Ruing their defeat, Republican leaders, including Trump and Vice President J.D. Vance, have admitted that the Iran war was a major cause of their humiliating defeat. Even in states regarded as Republican strongholds, such as Kansas, Ohio, Texas, North Carolina, and Maine, some Republicans abandoned the Grand Old Party and voted for the Democrats.

The result was a blue tsunami that the Democratic leadership was scarcely prepared to handle. Voters will find out in the coming months whether the party respects the mandate and delivers on its promises.

The wave turned blue, giving the Democrats total control of the House and the Senate, largely because of Trump’s reckless plunge into the Iran war at the behest of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, a war-crimes suspect wanted by the International Criminal Court. It was largely because of this war that working-class Americans are paying more for their food, groceries, fuel and transport than they did before February 28.

Trump and Netanyahu thought the Iranians would surrender and that regime change would occur within days, but the Iranians proved themselves a warrior nation determined to fight to the last. The war is now in its ninth month. Given Trump’s unpredictability and Netanyahu’s sinister designs, it is too early to say that the anti-war message of the election results will end the Iran war just as public opposition helped bring an end to the Vietnam War in 1975.

Days before the midterms, renowned podcaster Tucker Carlson claimed in a lengthy post that Trump was being prodded by Netanyahu to drop a nuclear weapon on Iran’s Pickaxe Mountain. The mountain is believed to house a secret nuclear facility built by the Islamic Republic to hide a stockpile of enriched uranium. The likely date for the attack was November 7.

In post-election statements, Trump claimed that the war was in the interest of the American people, even though they did not understand its long-term benefits. But the war served the billionaire class rather than ordinary Americans. In a display of indifference to the high grocery and fuel prices that people were paying, Trump bragged that the war had made American oil companies richer.

Lost in this rhetoric was the larger reality that ordinary Americans had to bear a heavier economic burden because of a war launched in the interests of Israel, a country that has received substantial financial support from the United States since its independence in 1948. Diverting US taxpayers’ money to support Israel’s actions in Gaza rather than using it for the welfare of the American people became a morally troubling issue during the election campaign, although not all Democratic Party candidates highlighted it because of the influence of AIPAC (the America-Israel Public Affairs Committee) on American politics.

Nevertheless, the issue energised student protests across the United States and prompted many people to question successive American administrations’ unwavering support for Israel despite allegations of war crimes. Perhaps it was in recognition of the importance of this issue that Trump, on the eve of the midterm elections, announced that his administration would stop sending aid to Israel. It was too little, too late.

Easier said than done. To say that the influence of the Zionist lobby is widespread would be a gross understatement. The lobby includes wealthy business figures, industry leaders and evangelical groups whose support for Israel is so strong that they view Netanyahu’s actions as serving a divine purpose. They fund election campaigns to help secure the victory of pro-Israel candidates who, in turn, are expected to advance Israeli interests rather than those of the American people who elected them.

The influence of the lobby remains a problem that US democracy has been unable to free itself from. The one who funds you controls you. Such is US politics.

AIPAC remains as influential as ever. In a message posted on X, the group congratulated “the hundreds of pro-Israel Democrats and Republicans who won the election.” It said that 234 candidates it supported had won. Among them were Democratic Party heavyweights Chuck Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries. The latter is expected to become Speaker of the House, the third-highest office in the line of succession after the President and Vice President.

The Republican Party, until Trump came onto the scene, had stood by the nation’s foundational values. It produced leaders whom the world respected, including Abraham Lincoln and Theodore Roosevelt. Trump, by contrast, is a joke that generates millions of memes. It is time the Republicans did some soul searching.

The progressives, whom Trump derided as communists, have now established themselves as a force to be reckoned with in US politics, not only within the Democratic Party but also across sections of the Republican Party. Years ago, it was nearly impossible for an American politician to openly adopt a pro-Palestinian stance. Today, however, pro-Palestinian views can help win elections.

Democrat James Talarico, who condemned Netanyahu over the Gaza massacre, would once have been a non-starter in a Texas primary. Instead, he went on to win the Senate race. “I’ve never taken money from AIPAC, and I’m not taking money from AIPAC in this campaign. Seeing what’s happening in Gaza makes me sick to my stomach as a teacher, as a Christian, as a human being. I screamed at God for the starving children until I realised the starving children were God screaming at me,” he said in a pre-election interview. Who would have imagined, even in their wildest dreams at the start of the election campaign, that Abdulrahman El Sayed, an American of Egyptian origin, would win the Michigan Senate race?

The obstacles he faced were formidable. Haley Stevens, the politician he defeated to secure the Democratic Party nomination in the primary, abandoned the party and backed El Sayed’s Republican rival, Kim Rogers. Hardly a day passed without Trump and Republican leaders accusing him of being a terrorist sympathiser linked to the Muslim Brotherhood, an organisation banned in Egypt and several Arab countries.

Yet he won comfortably despite his outspoken condemnation of Israel’s actions in Gaza. AIPAC spent US$42 million supporting rival candidates in an effort to defeat him. Much to the chagrin of AIPAC and the Zionist lobby, he made history by becoming the first Muslim elected to the US Senate.

The question now is whether the Democratic Party, which will take control of both the House and the Senate in January, is up to the task of cleaning the Augean stable that US politics has become.
