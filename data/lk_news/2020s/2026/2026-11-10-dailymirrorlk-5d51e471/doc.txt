Let’s learn from past disasters

As in all other issues, the National People’s Power (NPP) government is attempting to put the blame for  last Friday’s floods in Colombo city on the previous governments, especially certain mayors and ministers of  former governments.

Conversely, all Opposition parties and their supporters seem to be overjoyed by the floods, and are ridiculing the current government as if this was the first major flashflood in the city.

From our point of view, both parties are somewhat justifiable in their stance, while both share the blame. Despite Colombo having gone under water several times and several projects were announced to address the issue for the past two decades, all previous governments failed to find a lasting solution to the problem. They have to take the responsibility for it.

For instance, the Director General of Meteorology Ajith Wijemanna has pointed out that Colombo had experienced significantly higher rainfall during previous extreme weather events, including the years 1992, 2005, and 2017. He also stated that Colombo has experienced much heavier rainfall in the past, including 493mm in 1992 and 440mm in 2010.

During the heaviest rains in 18 years in Colombo, the Parliament complex and its access roads were severely inundated in November 2010. The lower floor of the building was submerged under more than a metre of water. The army deployed amphibious armored vehicles—commonly referred to locally as Unicorn(or Buffel)—to ferry MPs to parliament while Speaker Chamal Rajapaksa had to use a boat to get there.

The Opposition seems to believe they should not forewarn the government—even when they have prior information or scientific knowledge of disasters — and would rather let them happen to gain political mileage.

On the other hand, in spite of the NPP government being only two years in power,  they too,  failed to prevent  Colombo roads from going under water though the imminent disaster should have been foreseen months ago. This left tens of thousands of shop owners and city dwellers — especially those living in shanty areas and commuters waiting for buses after a hard day’s work—in extreme difficulty.

Cyclone Ditwah that played havoc exactly a year ago should have been an eye opener for the government and  officialdom. In fact, the government opened the eyes at least in Colombo District during the District Coordinating Committee (DCC) meeting held a month ago, on October 13, but  officials closed their eyes tightly thereafter. The follow-up supervision on the part of the political authority resulted in the disaster. These factors deprived the government as well as the Opposition of any moral right to blame each other and gain political mileage from the difficulties faced by the people.

During the DCC meeting, Prime Minister Dr. Harini Amarasuriya sternly advised the bureaucrats to clean the city’s drainage system, citing the approaching El Niño effects, while the latter attempted to make excuses, claiming there was a dearth of technical officers. However, after  Friday’s flash floods in the city, officials were seen busy mobilising employees to clean  obstructed canals and waterways.

Within a day after  Friday’s flashfloods, they announced that they had identified 60 locations across five electoral divisions in Colombo where drains and waterways are obstructing the flow of floodwater and steps were taken to clean them. Nobody was heard grumbling over shortage of technical officers.

Nevertheless, it is clear that even if the blocked drains and waterways had been cleaned as instructed by the Prime Minister last month, it would have only minimised the effects of  Friday’s heavy rains, as the city maintains an outdated and inadequate drainage system. Colombo Mayor Vraie Cally Balthazaar had stated that the city’s drainage system has been designed to accommodate approximately 30 millimeters of rainfall, whereas the city received 129 millimeters within two hours on Friday. This necessitates urgent upgrading of the drainage system.

One would wonder whether the authorities are bracing for future flash floods in Colombo alone as if all other cities and townships were safe. No government can foresee every impending disaster and crisis. Yet, every such eventuality should be a lesson, an eye-opener, and a guide.
