Dayasiri challenges mandatory remand powers in Namal’s case

Opposition MP Dayasiri Jayasekara has questioned the mandatory remand provisions under Section 149 of the Anti-Corruption Act, raising concerns over judicial independence and the possibility of using criminal investigations against political opponents.

Speaking in Parliament, Jayasekara, a lawyer by profession, questioned whether magistrates had sufficient discretion to examine the grounds for detention when a certificate issued by the Director-General of the Commission to Investigate Allegations of Bribery or Corruption (CIABOC) was presented under the provision.

Referring to proceedings involving Opposition MP Namal Rajapaksa, he said the Fort Magistrate had sought clarification in September on whether the Director-General had submitted such a certificate in the relevant proceedings.

Jayasekara said Section 149 of the Anti-Corruption Act, enacted in 2023, provides for the remand of suspects in certain bribery-related offences when a certificate issued by the CIABOC Director-General is presented, while allowing bail only in exceptional circumstances.

He questioned whether suspects had a meaningful opportunity to challenge the factual basis of such certificates, warning that prolonged remand could affect individuals and their families even if they were subsequently acquitted.

Citing Article 13(5) of the Constitution, he said the presumption of innocence must be protected and stressed that investigations should be guided by evidence rather than political considerations.

“An honest investigation follows the evidence, wherever it leads and whoever may be implicated,” he told Parliament.

The MP called on the Justice Minister to disclose the number of certificates issued under Section 149 annually, the period suspects had spent in remand under the provision and the number of cases that had proceeded to trial.

He said Parliament should examine the practical implications of the provision without interfering with ongoing court proceedings.

Jayasekara also raised concerns over the extension of the retirement ages of Supreme Court and Court of Appeal judges through the 22nd Amendment, arguing that the changes could have been applied to future appointments rather than judges already serving.

He further called for greater transparency in the appointment of judicial benches in politically sensitive cases, saying the publication of principles governing bench assignments would strengthen public confidence in the judiciary.

Referring to the 1937 Mark Bracegirdle case, he recalled how the judiciary had challenged the colonial administration's deportation order and secured Bracegirdle's release.

He also referred to the decision by the J.R. Jayewardene administration in 1980 to strip former Prime Minister Sirimavo Bandaranaike of her civic rights, preventing her from contesting the 1982 presidential election.

Warning the Government against using criminal law to restrict political competition, Jayasekara said such action would undermine the people's right to choose their representatives.

“If you use the criminal law to put an opponent out of the electoral contest, you are doing to that opponent what J.R. did to Sirimavo,” he said.

He urged the Government to face its political opponents at elections rather than use legal mechanisms to weaken them.

“Have the courage to face your opponents at the polls. Leave the choice to the people,” he said.

Recalling Bandaranaike's return to power as Prime Minister in 1994, Jayasekara concluded: “Do not forget, Honourable Speaker. Sirimavo had the last laugh.”
