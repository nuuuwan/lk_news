Education is the epicentre of Albania’s student protests

Student protester throws stone at Education Ministry building (photo credit Al Jezeera/AP)

The student unrest which engulfed South Asia and Western Europe since 2022 has now spread to Albania.

It’s a safe bet that many people haven’t heard of Albania, or have any idea where it’s located.

Albania is in the Balkans, sharing borders with Macedonia, Greece, Kosovo and Montenegro. With a population of just 2.74 Million people, nearly half of whom have fled abroad looking for work, it is also the last socialist state in Europe, and the poorest.

Though called an upper middle income country with a per capita GDP of $12, 490, the average gross monthly salary is just US$1,050 ($840 Net). Cost of living is very high. People say rents in Tirana, the capital, costs up to 75% of their incomes.

Student protesters ranging from high school to university broke into the Education Ministry on October 14, demanding better conditions, lower tuition fees and jobs. Despite a very strong government crackdown and police action, the protests continue unabated.

These protests grew out of the broader Flamingo protest movement which began almost five months ago. Alongside economic relief, protesters have been demanding that Albanian President Edi Rama and opposition leader Sali Berisha should both resign due to overwhelming corruption, a cry that has been taken up by the current wave of student protesters.

They are also demanding a stop to the proposed luxury holiday resort in an environmentally sensitive area funded by US president Donald Trump’s son in law Jared Kushner.

The irony is that, while Albania blessed by nature is fast becoming a top Balkans  tourist destination, most of the population is struggling to make ends meet. Cost of renting houses and apartments was a major focal point of the protests. Food prices too, are very high. Protesters say food is cheaper in Germany than in Albania.

Albania is a largely agricultural country, producing dairy products and olive oil. Industries are limited to footwear, handicrafts, minerals and apparels.

Prime Minister Rama, who has ruled for fourteen years, is accused of being increasingly authoritarian. Protest leaders turned down his request for negotiations, which he did by placing six chairs symbolically, as an invitation, in front of a ministry building.

Protesters broke up one of the chairs, refusing talks with the unpopular  PM. Just as it happened in Sri Lanka in April 2022, when ‘Gota Go Home’ became the deafening rallying cry, Albanian protesters are fed up with the system and want the entire government changed along with the head of state as well as opposition leader.

Unlike in Sri Lanka, this could turn out to be a long-drawn struggle. In Serbia, long-standing Prime Minister Alexander Vucic was ousted by student protesters after a two-year struggle, bitter but without bloodshed,  in October. But in Albania, PM Rama is grimly determined to hang on to power, and has introduced draconian new legislation to quell the protests.

While the police have so far refrained from firing live bullets, they have been breaking up protests with rubber bullets and water cannon. Bulldozers have been tearing up paved streets in Tirana, making it difficult for protesters to move. But they have been using the cobblestones to attack the police.

Rama has promised bonuses to the police, and his new anti-protest legislation makes parents of under-18 student protesters liable for any damage caused with fines of 5,500 Euros, a minor fortune in a country with such low wages. They will also be stripped of free education and scholarships.

Even bystanders can be charged under the new laws with fines up to 1500 Euros for ‘attending the protests.’ Protesters wearing masks, too, will be fined.

But none of this has discouraged the protesters. Albania’s experiment with democracy is relatively recent, holding its first multi-party elections in 1991 after half a century of Communist rule.

Little Albania was under Ottoman rule for more than four centuries. Next, it was occupied early in the 20th century by Italy. In 1944, communist partisans under Enver Hoxha liberated Albania from Italians.

But the country was liberated only to become a hermit kingdom with all borders closed to the outside world. Hoxha banned religion. After centuries of Turkish rule, about 50% of Albania’s population are Muslims, devided into a Sunni majority and a Betakshi Muslim minority, a liberal Sufi mystical branch of Islam whose global headquarters are located in Tirana. Various branches of Christianity including Roman Catholics, Eastern Orthodox Church,  Evangelicals and other sects bring up the rest. But Albania still remains a secular state.

Hoxha was paranoid, and cut off ties not just with the West but also with Soviet Russia and China. It’s tempting to call Albania Europe’s North Korea, but Hoxha’s contemporary Kim Il Sung remained close to both Moscow and Beijing,  while Hoxha made sure that Albania had no friends anywhere.

Albania under Hoxha didn’t have forced labour camps like in Soviet Russia or North Korea. But Hoxha was, if anything, even more paranoid than Kim Il Sung. Even though Albania had no hostile countries along its borders, he ordered the construction of thousands of concrete pillbox shaped bunkers along the borders and inside the country.

Reportedly, 750,000 of these domed bunkers were made in a country with an acute housing shortage. Up to 16 people could shelter in each. Never used in a war, many of these abandoned structures are being used now as beach bars.

Border guards were ordered to shoot anyone coming near the border’s bunkers, and they usually did. The total cost of construction is estimated to have exceeded that of France’s Maginot Line, constructed after World War I to stop a German invasion (In 1940, the German army simply avoided it and invaded through Belgium).

Radio and television were state-controlled. People listened to Italian radio using antennas, but TV was limited to two hours a night, mostly showing Hoxha cutting ribbons. He died in 1985, aged 76, and his communist party successors relaxed the rules a bit, but the borders still remained closed.

Finally, students stormed Tirana in 1990. Hoxha’s statue was torn down, and in 1992 the first-multi party elections were won by current opposition leader Sali Berisha, who finally opened the borders. Albania remained a socialist state but experimented with capitalism. Most Albanians invested their money in five finance companies.

All of them crashed in 1997, and people’s savings vanished overnight. Chaos followed, with looting everywhere. Over 2000 people died in the violence. Finally, a UN force of 7000 arrived in 2000 to maintain order and distribute food.

Normalcy returned, and Albania joined NATO in 2009, while talks opened to join the European Union in 2022. But most Albanians still remain poor as they always did, hence the current wave of student protests.

They are also disgusted with the bureaucracy and corruption. According to one story, the current female head of Albania’s secret service is under house arrest for ‘leaking secrets’ – not to foreign sources, but to Albania’s own ‘underground prime minister’ who, protesters say,  does all the ‘dirty work’ for PM Edi Rama.
