From discovery to the patient Role of a Pharmacist in the journey of a medicine

On World Pharmacists Day 2026, the SLCPP urges the public to recognise the broad expertise of pharmacists.

By Dr. Uthpali Mannapperuma, Dr. Abarna Nadeshkumar and Dr. Savini Senadheera

The Sri Lanka College of Pharmacy Professionals (SLCPP) brings together pharmacists with postgraduate qualifications working across different areas of the profession. As we celebrated the World Pharmacists Day recently, the SLCPP wishes to take readers along the journey of a medicine - from its early discovery and development to its eventual use by patients by highlighting the many roles pharmacists play along the way.

A pharmacist is much more than the person behind the pharmacy counter. Internationally, pharmacists are recognised as scientifically trained healthcare professionals with expertise that contributes to the safe and effective use of medicines.

Pharmacists work in many areas beyond the traditional roles that the public may be most familiar with. Some pharmacists build careers as pharmaceutical scientists, contributing to research, development, production and quality of medicines.

To a patient holding a tablet in the palm of their hand, it may simply be a small tablet taken once or twice a day. The journey of that tablet began years before it reached the patient. Along that journey, pharmacists contribute at many different stages, starting from discovering a potential medicine to developing and manufacturing it, checking its quality, contributing to its regulation, ensuring it reaches hospitals and pharmacies safely, and finally helping the patient to use it correctly.

The journey starts in a research laboratory. When pharmaceutical scientists identify a new chemical compound that may have the potential to treat or prevent disease, they determine whether it can reach the right place in the body, remain there long enough to work and do so without causing unacceptable harm. At this early stage, pharmaceutical expertise is vital, as it helps to answer an important question: “Can this substance eventually become a medicine that patients can use safely and effectively?” At this stage, pharmacists contribute their knowledge of medicines regarding what happens when a medicine enters the body including, how it is absorbed, where it travels, how it is broken down and how it is eventually removed from the body.

Even when research finds a new chemical compound that works, the next challenge is turning this promising compound into a form that can be used effectively and safely. The tablet that eventually reaches a patient’s hand is not simply a chemical compound pressed into a desirable shape. Pharmacists help to decide whether the new chemical compound should become a tablet, capsule, syrup, injection, inhaler, cream or another type of product. The decision is shaped by considerations such as how quickly the medicine should be released, which other ingredients are needed to make the product stable and usable, and what type of packaging will protect it from the environment around it.

Before a new medicine becomes widely available, it needs to be carefully studied by conducting clinical trials to understand how well it works and how safe it is. Once a medicine has been developed, an important next step is deciding how much a patient should receive and how often it should be given to ensure safe and effective treatment. The right dose can vary from one patient to another. For example, if a patient’s kidneys cannot remove a medicine efficiently, it may build up in the body. The dose may therefore need to be reduced or more time allowed between doses. Factors such as a patient’s age, organ function, type of illness, and other medicines they are taking can all affect how the body handles a medicine. Pharmacists with expertise in how medicines behave in the body help us understand these differences and determine doses that are appropriate for individual patients. Behind an apparently simple instruction such as “take one tablet twice daily” may therefore be years of work done by a team establishing why that dose is appropriate and when dosing changes are needed.

The next stage takes the medicine from development into large-scale manufacturing. Producing millions of tablets creates the challenge of being able to manufacture the same pharmaceutical product consistently to meet the required quality standards. Pharmacists working in the pharmaceutical industry who are involved in production, quality assurance, quality control testing and stability studies ensure that the medicine contains the medicinal active ingredient at the correct strength and continues to meet quality requirements throughout its shelf life.This is why “100 mg” on a medicine label is more than a printed number. It is a claim that must be supported by manufacturing controls and testing.

In Sri Lanka, the National Medicines Regulatory Authority (NMRA) provides regulatory oversight of medicines, including functions related to assessment, manufacturing oversight, and monitoring after medicines enter the market. Pharmacists working in regulatory settings apply their knowledge when technical information is evaluated, helping connect the scientific evidence provided about a medicine being assessed with the needs of the public in mind. Regulatory pharmacists safeguard medicines available to patients to ensure they meet required standards of quality, safety and effectiveness.

A medicine that leaves the manufacturer in perfect condition can become unsuitable if it is transported or stored incorrectly. Pharmacists contribute to selecting, purchasing, storing, managing and distributing medicines at national, hospital and pharmacy levels. Good planning helps maintain supplies while reducing shortages, unnecessary overstocking, expiry and waste. The aim at this stage is not simply to make sure that the medicine reaches the patient, but also to ensure that its quality is maintained throughout its journey. This helps ensure that patients have access to good-quality medicines at all times.

Eventually, the medicine reaches the pharmacy counter. Professional dispensing by a pharmacist involves more than selecting the correct medicine and handing it over. Pharmacists consider whether the medicines, doses and instructions in the prescriptions are appropriate for the individual patient based on factors such as age, allergies, other illnesses and other medicines being taken. Some patients may receive medicines from several clinics and sources. Pharmacists involved in clinical services can look at the patient’s medicines, helping to identify possible interactions, duplicated treatment, unsuitable doses and other medicine-related problems.

The way a medicine is used matters too. If correct instructions are not provided, the medicine might not work as intended. An inhaler will not work properly if the patient does not know how to use it. Certain tablets should not be crushed because it changes the way the medicine is released.Pharmacists in hospitals, community pharmacies, industry and regulatory agencies contribute to correct use of medicines and also recognise and report suspected adverse reactions and other medicine-related problems.

In Sri Lanka, pharmacies are required to have a registered pharmacist.Therefore, patients should make sure that the person dispensing their medicines is a qualified registered pharmacist. On World Pharmacists Day 2026, the SLCPP urges the public to recognise the broad expertise of pharmacists beyond the point of dispensing medicines and make full use of their knowledge and skills to help strengthen healthcare and promote the safe and effective use of medicines in Sri Lanka.

The writers are members of the College of Pharmacy Professionals (SLCPP).
