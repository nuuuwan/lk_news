A nation powered by nature: Sri Lanka’s first battery energy storage system commissioning turns new chapter in energy journey

According to the Sri Lanka Sustainable Energy Authority, the country’s renewable energy potential exceeds 13,000 MW

By investing early, Sri Lanka positions itself as a climate-resilient island nation, setting an example for other Asian countries

Sri Lanka’s dependence on imported fuels exposes the nation to international price volatility and foreign exchange pressures

Galilee BESS (Pvt) Ltd, a subsidiary of WKV Hydro Technics (Pvt) Ltd and wholly owned by Khen Energy Ltd, has successfully completed the commissioning process mandated by the National System Operator (NSO). This milestone culminated in the commissioning of Sri Lanka’s first-ever 10MW/40MWh Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) in Galle on 10th September 2026.

The achievement was made possible not only through corporate commitment and technical expertise, but also through the Government’s decisive leadership and expedited facilitation of the BESS initiative within a remarkably short period of time.

BESS is an integrated system that stabilises power grids, supports renewable energy, and strengthens economic resilience. For Sri Lanka, the commissioning of its first BESS in Galle is both a technological milestone and a strategic policy achievement a catalyst for industrial ecosystems and a foundation for regional leadership in the global sustainable energy transition.

This landmark achievement inaugurates a new chapter in Sri Lanka’s energy journey, one where storage technology transforms the country’s abundant renewable resources into dependable, dispatchable power. This partnership between the public and private sectors marks a historic breakthrough, advancing resilience, sustainability, and national progress.

Sri Lanka has always been blessed with sun, wind, and water. What was missing was the storage, the technology, the investment, and the leadership to convert these natural gifts into reliable energy for the nation and beyond. The Galilee BESS project demonstrates that this vision is no longer a distant aspiration but a proven reality standing as proof of concept delivered, affirming Sri Lanka’s capacity to lead in renewable energy innovation and to inspire progress across South Asia and the wider Asian region.

Sri Lanka’s energy transition is about much more than batteries, solar panels, or renewable energy targets. It is about making intelligent use of the exceptional natural resources our country has been endowed with, and converting them into lasting economic and national value.

Our abundant sunshine, together with our hydropower and wind potential, gives us a strong foundation for a cleaner and more self-reliant future. According to the Sri Lanka Sustainable Energy Authority, the country’s renewable energy potential exceeds 13,000 MW including 6,700 MW of solar, 4,000 MW of offshore wind, 1,860 MW of mini hydro, and 300 MW of biomass (Sri Lanka Sustainable Energy Authority, 2025) more than double our current installed capacity.

Battery Energy Storage Systems are central to unlocking this potential. They provide the flexibility to store renewable electricity when it is abundant and release it when it is most needed, helping manage generation fluctuations, support grid stability, reduce renewable energy wastage, and strengthen the reliability of electricity supply.

The National System Operator, established under the Sri Lanka Electricity Act No. 36 of 2024, oversaw the pre-commissioning of this pioneering 10MW/40MWh facility.  The Act unbundled the former Ceylon Electricity Board into separate successor companies, establishing the NSO as a state-controlled entity responsible for real-time power system operations including generation dispatch, power scheduling, and grid management overseeing bulk transactions between generation and distribution companies and reporting deviations from the approved power plan to maintain optimal, least-cost electricity provision.

This pioneering project marks a significant milestone in our country’s energy sector and represents a major step towards strengthening Sri Lanka’s energy security, enhancing grid resilience, and accelerating the nation’s transition to a sustainable, low-carbon energy future.

The project delivers a range of strategic national benefits, including:

a) Enhancing grid stability and reliability, particularly in Galle District, thereby reducing power interruptions to domestic consumers, businesses, and essential public services.

b) Facilitating higher integration of renewable energy into the national grid, reducing dependence on fossil fuels and strengthening national energy resilience.

c) Creating employment opportunities during the construction and operational phases and continuing to add value to the area through its CSR projects.

d) Promoting the transfer of advanced battery energy storage technology and expertise to the Sri Lankan engineering fraternity.

e) Supporting Sri Lanka’s national commitment to achieving 70% renewable electricity generation by 2030 and carbon neutrality in the electricity sector by 2050.

f) Significantly minimising the disruptive effects of El Niño on Sri Lanka’s energy sector.

Further, the regulatory authorities’ unwavering commitment to stakeholders is a fundamental to the success of Sri Lanka’s BESS initiatives, particularly given the urgent national imperative to strengthen grid stability and mitigate the impact of hydropower variability, including anticipated El Niño-related shortfalls. A transparent, predictable, and efficient approval framework for BESS projects not only instils confidence among investors and project developers but also demonstrates the government’s resolve to translate energy policy into tangible, on-ground capacity.  By hastening and streamlining the BESS approval process through the elimination of redundant procedures, clearer coordination between the Ministry of Energy, the National System Operator, and other relevant agencies, and firm adherence to stipulated timelines regulators can significantly reduce project gestation periods, thereby lowering costs and accelerating the deployment of critical grid-balancing infrastructure.  Such commitment sends an unambiguous signal to stakeholders that their investments in Sri Lanka’s energy transition are valued and safeguarded within a framework of good governance, ultimately positioning the country to build on milestones for BESS.

Sri Lanka, like many nations in the Indian Ocean, is highly vulnerable to climate variability. Monsoon shifts, droughts, and cyclones can disrupt electricity generation and place immense strain on national infrastructure. In such circumstances, BESS is not merely a technical solution it is a resilience strategy.

Stored energy can keep hospitals functioning during blackouts, ensure schools remain open, and power emergency services when conventional generation falters. It can provide backup capacity during droughts that reduce hydropower output, or during storms that damage transmission lines.

By investing early, Sri Lanka positions itself as a climate-resilient island nation, setting an example for other Asian countries facing similar vulnerabilities. From Bangladesh’s floodplains to the typhoon-prone Philippines, the lesson is clear: storage is not optional; it is essential. The catastrophic Himalayan flash floods that struck Nepal and Tibet in August 2026 triggered by the collapse of a glacier above the Lhende river on the Tibetan side, which dammed the waterway before the surge tore through border settlements in Nepal’s Rasuwa district and into Tibet’s Gyirong Port killed hundreds and left thousands more missing across both sides of the border, with scientists linking the collapse to warming-driven destabilisation of mountain permafrost and Nepal’s own climate officials and foreign minister explicitly attributing the disaster to climate change and calling for a coordinated global response.

It is a stark reminder that no nation, however remote or well-prepared its early warning systems, is insulated from the accelerating consequences of a warming planet. Sri Lanka’s leadership can demonstrate how proactive planning and investment in BESS can mitigate risks and protect societies against these unpredictable and intensifying forces of climate change.

Energy security is inseparable from economic security. Sri Lanka’s dependence on imported fuels exposes the nation to international price volatility and foreign exchange pressures.  In 2025, fuel imports accounted for 20–22% of total merchandise imports, costing the country USD 4 billion annually (World Bank, 2025).  Today, this vulnerability is even more pronounced.  Global crude oil prices remain elevated, with Brent trading near $108 per barrel and WTI at $102.78 per barrel (September 14, 2026). WTI stands for West Texas Intermediate a grade of crude oil that serves as a major global benchmark for oil pricing, and is often used as a reference point in energy sector analysis, including discussions on fuel costs, power generation economics, and broader energy security. This sharp rise underscores the urgency of reducing reliance on imported fuels and highlights the strategic importance of the newly commissioned Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) in Galle.

Private sector participation is equally vital. Transparent procurement frameworks and clear investment pathways can encourage domestic and international investors to contribute to Sri Lanka’s energy future, while small and medium enterprises can find opportunities in installation, maintenance, and ancillary services.

By reducing fuel imports, Sri Lanka also strengthens its balance of payments and shields its economy from external shocks creating a more attractive environment for foreign investment and reinforcing the virtuous cycle of growth and innovation.

Sri Lanka’s energy transition carries profound geopolitical significance. In the Indian Ocean, energy security is inseparable from maritime trade and strategic competition. By reducing dependence on imported fuels, Sri Lanka strengthens its sovereignty and reduces exposure to external shocks.

The vision must extend beyond national borders. South Asia is one of the fastest-growing energy markets in the world, with energy demand projected to grow at 6% annually, while fossil fuels still account for 70% of the region’s primary energy supply (International Energy Agency, 2025). This imbalance underscores the urgency of scalable renewable solutions.

By positioning Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) as a cornerstone of its energy transition, Sri Lanka can demonstrate how renewable energy can be stabilised and scaled, offering a model for neighbouring countries. Cross-border electricity trade, regional disaster-response frameworks, and shared technology platforms can all benefit from Sri Lanka’s leadership.

Today, roughly 8–10 Asian countries have meaningful grid-scale BESS deployment: China, India, Japan, South Korea, Australia, Philippines, Vietnam, Taiwan, and now Sri Lanka, with newer entrants emerging. The broader ASEAN bloc is still building out regulatory frameworks to catch up.

Indeed, Sri Lanka can become a role model for Asia. Our nation, though small in size, can show how intelligent planning, early investment in storage, and the harnessing of natural assets deliver resilience, prosperity, and leadership. Larger economies across Asia many of which face similar challenges of fuel dependence, climate vulnerability, and rising demand can look to Sri Lanka’s example as proof that clean energy can be reliable, affordable, and scalable.

Energy transition is not only a matter for governments and institutions it is a matter for every citizen.  People must understand that when we invest in BESS, we are investing in cleaner air, more reliable electricity, reduced fuel imports, and a stronger economy.

This is a generational opportunity. For our youth, it means new jobs, new industries, and new skills. For our communities, it means resilience against climate shocks and greater reliability in daily life. For our nation, it means independence, prosperity, and leadership.

Public engagement must therefore be central to the National BESS Programme. Education campaigns, community-level renewable projects, and transparent communication can ensure that citizens see themselves as stakeholders in the energy future. When people understand the benefits, they will support the policies, participate in the projects, and contribute to the transformation.

The sun that warms our homes, the wind that sweeps across our fields, and the rivers that flow through our land are not abstractions they are gifts of nature entrusted to our stewardship. With storage, technology, investment, and leadership, these gifts can be transformed into dependable power, stronger economic performance, and enduring prosperity.

This is a moment that calls for national vision and coordinated regional action. What Sri Lanka has long awaited the storage, the technology, the investment, and the leadership are now within our grasp, ready to be harnessed not only for our nation, but for South Asia and Asia at large.  That future has already begun.  The commissioning of Sri Lanka’s first Battery Energy Storage System in Galle stands as living proof that this vision is no longer aspirational it is already unfolding.

The energy challenge of tomorrow calls for action today. Let us build the capacity that empowers Sri Lanka to inspire Asia and brighten a cleaner, more resilient future for generations to come.
