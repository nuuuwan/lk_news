Stop worshipping growth, start measuring poverty, economists tell government

Poverty rate more than doubled to nearly 30 percent during the crisis, but the state has published no official measure since

Economists say policy is captured by a small circle of vested interests - “100 families”- blocking competition and enterprise

High real interest rates quietly transferring wealth from wage earners to capital holders, panel warns

Sri Lanka’s obsession with headline GDP growth is masking a deepening poverty crisis that the state has failed to even measure, leading economists warned, calling for an urgent shift towards a national development plan that confronts the country’s entrenched vested interests.

Speaking at a panel discussion hosted by the Sri Lanka–Korea Business Council titled “Sri Lanka’s Future – Forecast, Scenarios and Challenges,” Verité Research Executive Director Dr. Nishan de Mel said growth that fails to improve ordinary lives will eventually destabilise the country, including for those who benefit from it.

“We mustn’t confuse means and ends. The point of growth is for us to live better lives, and for the flourishing of humans and the planet. Growth doesn’t become the master, it must become the servant. And as economists, we often forget that. We start worshipping growth, but not the outcomes on human lives,” de Mel said.

The blind spot, he argued, is statistical as much as political. Sri Lanka’s poverty rate more than doubled from 14.3 percent to nearly 30 percent during the crisis—a steeper deterioration than in other debt-distressed nations—yet the state has not published an official poverty measure since. “What you fail to measure, you fail to focus on,” de Mel said, noting that roughly 92 percent of formal private sector employees earn less than Rs. 100,000 a month.

Advocata Institute Chairman Murtaza Jafferjee traced the failure back to policy capture. “The main determinant of productivity is competition,” he said, arguing that the country is effectively “governed for 100 families” - a figurative shorthand, he clarified, for how policy is framed and implemented to serve concentrated interests rather than open competition. Citing a recent address by the Prime Minister, Jafferjee said dismantling these protectionist walls to level the playing field would unlock the “animal spirits” needed for genuine enterprise.

De Mel linked that same concentration of advantage to Sri Lanka’s high real interest rates, which he said quietly transfer wealth from wage earners to capital holders. “When you have high interest rates and no inflation, the only people that benefit from it are people like us who have large savings, can leverage and borrow, and we make money for nothing,” he said.

“While all the people who are living at subsistence, are just managing on wages, are paying higher taxes - 18 percent VAT - so the government can collect that money and pay us the high real interest rates. This is a racket in which people like us benefit enormously on the backs of those who see very little benefit from the economy.”

Both economists urged corporate leaders and trade chambers to move beyond narrow lobbying and push instead for a genuine national development strategy - one that also addresses the brain drain hollowing out businesses.

“One of the reasons you can’t hire competent people at reasonable prices is everyone who is capable wants to leave the country, and then you’re left in trouble,” de Mel said. “Narrow interests that are constantly pursued and promoted are harming those narrow interests itself.”
