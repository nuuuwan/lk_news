Another IMF program in 2028?

Sri Lanka faces a defining economic choice. As its current four-year IMF program approaches its scheduled conclusion in March 2027, debate is growing over whether the country should seek another program in 2028 or attempt to sustain its recovery without one.

The question is not simply whether Sri Lanka needs the IMF. It is whether the country can convert economic stabilisation into lasting prosperity, reduce poverty and create opportunities for its people. The IMF program has helped restore stability, credibility, strengthen reserves and advance debt restructuring. Yet significant debt vulnerabilities remain, and sustainable growth requires reforms that go beyond fiscal targets .Sri Lanka must move beyond managing its crisis to building an economy that can prevent the next one.

Fiscal discipline cannot be abandoned. Sri Lanka must maintain credible public finances, manage debt-service obligations and rebuild buffers against external shocks. Walking away from the IMF without a credible alternative financing strategy could weaken investor confidence and expose the economy to renewed pressure.

However, fiscal consolidation cannot become an end in itself. A government can improve its accounts while households struggle, businesses postpone investment and skilled young people leave. If economic recovery does not translate into better living standards and reduced poverty, its sustainability will remain in question.

The World Bank’s October 2026 Sri Lanka Development Update estimated that 16.9% of the population lived below its $ 4.20-a-day poverty threshold in 2025, compared with 11.5% in 2019. It projects poverty to decline gradually, but remain above pre-crisis levels through 2028. These estimates underline a fundamental weakness in measuring success through macroeconomic indicators alone: the economy can recover before household welfare does. The tax burden deserves particular attention. Individuals pay income tax on earnings and then face consumption taxes when spending their after-tax income. Consumption taxes can disproportionately affect lower-income households, which spend a greater share of their earnings on essentials. Businesses also face multiple taxes and levies, although corporate income tax is generally applied to taxable profits.

The objective should be a broader, fairer and more efficient tax base, not simply higher collections. Public expenditure must deliver better services, waste and inefficient spending must be addressed, and social protection must reach vulnerable households. Citizens are more likely to support fiscal discipline when they can see tangible results.

Sri Lanka cannot achieve sustainable debt reduction through taxation and expenditure cuts alone. It needs faster economic growth, stronger exports and substantial investment in productive capacity.

Foreign direct investment (FDI) can bring capital, technology, management expertise, access to international markets and higher-value employment. Yet investors need predictable policies, efficient approvals, competitive energy costs, reliable infrastructure and confidence that rules will not change arbitrarily.

Sri Lanka should target investment in export manufacturing, IT and business services, logistics, tourism, renewable energy and agribusiness. Investment promotion must translate into implementation: fewer bureaucratic delays, transparent incentives and accountability for delivering projects.

Crucially, FDI should generate productive jobs, raise incomes and expand opportunities beyond a narrow segment of society. Growth matters, but who benefits from that growth matters equally.

Perhaps Sri Lanka’s greatest economic risk is losing the people needed to drive its recovery. When skilled professionals, entrepreneurs and ambitious young people leave, the country loses productive capacity, innovation and part of its future tax base. Remittances support foreign-exchange earnings, but cannot fully replace the economic value of a skilled workforce building businesses at home.

Retaining talent requires more than appeals to patriotism. Young people need competitive salaries, merit-based career progression, opportunities to innovate and confidence that enterprise will be rewarded. They must be able to see a future in Sri Lanka.

This is where FDI, education, labour reform, employment and poverty reduction intersect. Investors need skilled workers; skilled workers need productive employers; and growing businesses create jobs that can lift families into more secure livelihoods. Better technical education, vocational training and stronger links between education and industry are essential.

The ultimate test of recovery is not merely how much the economy grows, but how many people escape poverty, find productive employment and gain a genuine stake in the country’s future.

Sri Lanka should neither rule out another IMF program nor assume one is inevitable. The decision must depend on financing needs, debt-service capacity, reserve adequacy, market access and the credibility of the domestic policy framework.

Before the current program ends, the Government should establish measurable targets for investment, exports, productivity, quality employment, poverty reduction and tax fairness alongside fiscal and debt objectives. Progress should be publicly monitored.

The real measure of success is not whether Sri Lanka signs another IMF agreement in 2028. It is whether the country has built an economy in which investors want to invest, businesses can grow, poverty declines and young people choose to stay. The IMF can provide a framework for stability, but it cannot substitute for strong institutions, creativity, innovation, sound economic management and private-sector dynamism. Sri Lanka’s ultimate objective must be to make external assistance a choice rather than a recurring necessity, ensuring that economic recovery delivers dignity, opportunity and shared prosperity for all. With its human talent, entrepreneurial spirit and strategic advantages, Sri Lanka has every potential to achieve this—and to do so with confidence and dignity.
