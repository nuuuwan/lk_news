Budget 2027:  Political Promises Vs People’s  Everyday Realities

However, beyond these concessions, the fundamental question remains: Has the 2027 Budget adequately addressed the economic difficulties confronting ordinary Sri Lankans?

The Government deserves recognition for introducing several positive tax concessions in its 2027 Budget. The increase in the monthly personal income tax-free threshold from Rs. 150,000 to Rs. 162,500, reductions in certain income tax rates, and the removal of VAT on printed books and exercise books are welcome measures. The proposed removal of the 10% Ports and Airports Development Levy on imported newsprint from 1 January 2027 is also commendable, particularly for the newspaper and printing industries struggling with rising production costs.

Similarly, reductions in the Special Commodity Levy on imported sprats, Maldive fish and dried chillies are positive steps towards making essential food items more affordable. These measures indicate some recognition of the financial difficulties faced by ordinary people and businesses.

However, beyond these concessions, the fundamental question remains: Has the 2027 Budget adequately addressed the economic difficulties confronting ordinary Sri Lankans? The answer must be examined against people’s actual living conditions rather than the Government’s financial projections, development promises, and announcements.

The continuing high prices of fuel, LP gas and electricity have created a chain reaction throughout the economy. Increased energy and transport costs inevitably push up the prices of food, consumer goods, construction materials, medicines and almost every other essential commodity. When businesses face higher operating expenses, they have little choice but to increase prices.

Consumers, meanwhile, are left with less disposable income. Families are increasingly compelled to prioritise food, education, healthcare and utility bills while abandoning other spending.

The Government must recognise that economic recovery means very little to a family that cannot afford its monthly groceries, electricity bills and children’s educational expenses. The difficulties faced by small businesses and self-employed individuals deserve far greater attention in the Budget. Artists, entertainers, musicians and others in the creative industries are particularly vulnerable. Their earnings depend heavily on people’s ability to spend on entertainment and leisure. When household budgets are squeezed by rising essential costs, entertainment is one of the first areas where people cut spending.

A self-employed artist today needs at least Rs. 200,000 per month to support a family, pay utility bills, purchase groceries and consumer goods, and meet children’s educational expenses. This is not necessarily a demand for a luxurious lifestyle. It reflects the actual financial requirements of maintaining a family under present economic conditions.

The Government should introduce a dedicated low-interest bank loan scheme for self-employed artists and small creative businesses. Such assistance would enable them to purchase equipment, improve production facilities, expand their services and develop sustainable businesses.

Although concessionary financing for small and medium enterprises has been included in the Budget, the crucial issue is whether self-employed individuals and micro-enterprises can actually access these facilities without excessive collateral requirements and complicated procedures.

The rising cost of transportation is another major concern. Parents are struggling with increasing school transport expenses, while private transport operators face escalating costs for fuel, tyres, spare parts and vehicle maintenance. The transport sector points out that taxation on imported spare parts has contributed significantly to the increase in vehicle maintenance expenses. Tyre prices have reportedly increased by approximately 200%, placing an enormous burden on transport operators.  Trade union demands cannot be ignored. The concerns raised by trade unions and professional organisations demonstrate the widespread nature of economic dissatisfaction. The Ceylon Mercantile Union has proposed establishing a national wage commission to determine private-sector salaries in accordance with the cost of living and increasing expenses. University academics and other professionals have resorted to strikes and protests, demanding better remuneration, tax revisions and measures to prevent the migration of skilled professionals. FUTA has also raised concerns about the deterioration of the education system and the resulting threat to the country’s future supply of qualified professionals.

The demands raised by the Government Medical Officers’ Association also require serious consideration. These include extra-duty and holiday allowances, increases in disturbance, availability and transport allowances, revisions to Advance Personal Income Tax, and solutions to staffing and other professional benefit issues. Instead of allowing industrial disputes to continue, the Government should engage in meaningful negotiations with healthcare professionals. Equally important is the need to strengthen local pharmaceutical manufacturing. Sri Lanka must reduce its dependence on imported medicines and medical equipment by encouraging domestic production.

Another concern raised by sections of the public is that the Government appears to devote considerable attention to legislation, regulations and constitutional amendments, while the economic difficulties affecting ordinary people remain unresolved. The public does not expect political leaders to intervene personally in every investigation into corruption or unlawful activities.

Fighting corruption is essential, but it must be pursued through independent institutions and transparent legal procedures. It should not become a substitute for addressing the country’s economic and social problems. The Attorney General’s Department, the police, CIABOC and independent legal institutions have the responsibility to investigate wrongdoing and enforce the law.

There were no allocated provisions for long-delayed Provincial Council elections.

“Previous governments had a policy on their policy statement: They would mention the Provincial Council elections,” ITAK’s MP Shanakiyan Rasamanickam told parliament during the budget debate. “Not a single word about the Provincial Council election! About the Provincial elections, you haven’t mentioned a word, but what have you mentioned? You are taking away the powers of the Provincial Council.”

The Government’s primary responsibility should be to create an environment in which people can afford food, education, healthcare, transport and electricity. The Budget must focus on poverty reduction, affordable essential services and sustainable employment rather than political considerations.

Sri Lanka cannot achieve long-term prosperity merely by increasing taxation, borrowing money or depending on external assistance. The country must become a production-oriented economy capable of generating employment, exports and foreign exchange. Agriculture, fisheries, manufacturing, tourism, pharmaceuticals, technology and creative industries should receive greater attention. Local entrepreneurs must be encouraged to manufacture goods that are currently imported. Small businesses should receive affordable credit, technological assistance and access to markets.

The easing of the West Asian conflict following recent developments may reduce some external economic uncertainty. Nevertheless, Sri Lanka cannot depend on international developments to resolve its domestic economic difficulties. It must strengthen local production and build economic resilience.

The Government must also demonstrate that the substantial public investment announced in the Budget will produce measurable economic benefits rather than remain a collection of financial allocations and promises.

The people expect solutions, not promises. The 2027 Budget must ultimately be judged by its impact on ordinary people. Tax concessions and development announcements are welcome, but they cannot conceal the continuing financial difficulties faced by households, workers, professionals and small businesses. It must also honour the commitments made in its election manifesto and demonstrate that economic recovery is benefiting the population. The people are not asking for privileges or luxuries. They are asking for affordable food, reasonable electricity and fuel prices, accessible education and healthcare, fair wages and opportunities to earn a decent living.

A Budget should not merely satisfy financial institutions, external stakeholders or government revenue targets. Its ultimate responsibility is to improve the living standards of the people and create a country in which families can live with dignity and economic security. The real success of the 2027 Budget will not be determined by how impressive it appears in Parliament, but by whether an ordinary Sri Lankan can feel a genuine improvement in everyday life.
