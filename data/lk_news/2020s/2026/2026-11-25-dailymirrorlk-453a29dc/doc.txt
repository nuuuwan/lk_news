‘Destructors’ take different forms in different countries

We studied a short story called ‘The Destructors’ by British author Graham Greene as an Advance Level text. Our English teacher told us while discussing the text that there is no such word in English, and Greene in fact invented it.

But an internet search shows me that the word entered the English language in the 17th century, derived from Latin. Over time, it came to signify physical devices such as refuse incinerators or an explosive self-destruct mechanism in rocketry or defense.

What my English teacher meant perhaps was that Greene (1904-1991) brought back its original meaning, which meant a person or force that destroys. But this column is not about the origin or meaning of ‘destructor.’ It’s rather about the people  described in his story as destructors. They are all British teenagers, members of a gang.

I remember my teacher laughing as he reached the end of the story. He was about the same age as Mr. Thomas, the  pensioner whose life is pretty much destroyed by the young hooligans. But he found it funny. It is very bleak humour.

The setting is a poverty-stricken neighbourhood known as Wormsley Common, still bearing scars of German bombing during World War II. The street gang has a new leader called Trevor, from a family fallen from middle class status to poverty. Trevor resents Mr. Thomas’ beautiful 18th century house which miraculously escaped the bombing.

Mr. Thomas,  called ‘Old Misery’ by the teenage gang, lives alone. He  is mocked and hated by them. When he goes away during a weekend, the gang begin to strip the house of all its fittings. They find Mr. Thomas’ life savings (no more than seventy pounds) and burn the bank notes simply for fun.

When the pensioner returns, the gang locks him inside the outdoor toilet. They then tie the remaining load-bearing struts to the house to a lorry. When the driver pulls away, the entire house collapses into a heap of rubble.

This is the passage which made my teacher laugh. There is macabre humour in it. But the pensioner, his house destroyed and all his belongings and savings gone, is ruined.

Greene was commenting on the sense of nihilism and moral emptiness in post-war England. The gang revel in the violence because purely for the perverse pleasure of it. Their motive is not hate or theft, though envy of someone better off than them is certainly part of it.  In 1972, Stanley Kubrik’s film ‘A Clockwork Orange’ painted a grimmer picture by portraying a gang which indulges in physical violence and rape.

Europe recovered quickly from the destruction of World War II, but youth malaise and unrest continued. It took the form of political protest against heavy-handed governance (French students against France’s Charles de Galle, or Czech students against Soviet invasion forces), and non-political hooliganism of the kind Graham Greene described in ‘Destructors’ took second place.

This student unrest is now reflected in the recent wave of youth-led revolts from Sri Lanka and South Asia to currently Serbia and Albania. But there is plenty of non-political youth violence and vandalism in the world.

A You Tube video on youth violence in the American state of New Mexico prompted me to write this column,

A couple were badly beaten by five youths  outside a cafeteria --  three boys and two girls, all of them teenagers except the oldest male, 21,  with a criminal record. The youngest girl was 12 and the other was 14.

Like the gang members in Graham Greene’s story, they didn’t steal anything. Instead of vandalising  property, they beat up the couple. The woman was pregnant. The culprits were quickly apprehended by the police.

But, in this real life case as in Greene’s fictitious one (perhaps based on a real life story known to him), police can’t establish a motive. It’s nothing personal, and the impersonal quality of the act is truly chilling.

This is not an isolated case. Such cases are reported frequently in the US. President Donald Trump’s hardcore followers would point a finger at immigrants – three of the accused were partly of fully Hispanic, while one was of Vietnamese descent. But such racist critics forget that roughly 52% to 57% of the perpetrators who committed indiscriminate public rampage shootings in the US over the past decade are white. Both shooters who attempted to kill Donald Trump, too, are white.

Street gangs are a significant problem in the United Kingdom, and they are more violent than the one in ‘Destructors.’ Orrganised gang activity and violence are heavily concentrated in major cities such as London, Birmingham, Manchester, Liverpool, and Glasgow—as well as certain regional areas and smaller towns.

These gangs are associated with serious youth violence, knife and firearm offenses, “county lines” drug supply networks,  retail theft and counterfeiting. But according to research,  the violence mainly takes the form of confrontations  between rival groups; it poses less of a threat to the general public.

In Asia, Hong Kong was known for violent street gangs – it was said that Bruce Lee studied martial arts to escape street violence. But the  city no longer has a major street gang problem, though organised criminal syndicates known as triads still exist. These triad groups have shifted toward cybercrime, online scams, money laundering, and white-collar schemes.

In India, criminal activity by young men and teenagers takes a different hue – there are no street gangs like in the US and UK, but adolescent and older males have weaponised rape and use it  to intimidate girls and women. Sometimes, the victims are killed to silence them. One such victim was reported from Gujarat State’s Surat City, known for  its diamond polishing industry. The girl was 9-11 years old, and her  battered body bore 86 injury marks.

According to a BBC report, the police believe she was held captive, tortured and ravaged. More than 10 days after they found her body, the police checked the list of some 8,000 missing children and couldn’t identify her. “There was no sign of struggle at the spot where the body was found,” the local police chief said.

This menace was exploited as film material by Bollywood in Mardani I and Mardani II starring Rani Mukherjee.

Analysts say  rape is increasingly used as an instrument to assert power and intimidate the powerless in India. They say this is not surprising in a hierarchical, patriarchal and increasingly polarised society, where hate is being used to divide people and harvest votes.

An awful sex ratio imbalance - largely because of illegal sex-selection abortions - means India has 112 boys born for every 100 girls, which is against the natural sex ratio of 105 boys for every 100 girls. The northern state of Haryana, which records the highest number of gang rapes in India, has the worst sex ratio in the country. In January alone, a 50-year-old man was held for mutilating a 10-year-old girl, a 15-year-old boy allegedly raped a three-and-a-half-year-old girl and a minor’s girl’s brutalised body was found in the fields. Eight Hindu men have been charged with the Kashmir gang-rape and murder of a Muslim girl.

One Indian social media video recently showed police and citizens nabbing four men who kidnapped a girl in broad daylight and sped away in a car. They were caught after a tyre burst and they were forced to stop.

In Sri Lanka, sexual violence, while not as acute as in India, remains a serious issue. But gang violence in the Western or the traditional Hong Kong sense is largely absent. Even in the roughest parts of Colombo, citizens are not threatened and shops are not looted by street gangs, and female workers  and  security guards can be seen walking home after work even late at night. Rape seems to be more a rural  problem than urban in Sri Lanka.
