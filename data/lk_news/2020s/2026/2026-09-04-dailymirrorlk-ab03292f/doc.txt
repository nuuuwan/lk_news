South Asia’s waste problem is becoming a methane crisis

South Asian countries have been urged to move beyond studies and commitments and take immediate, practical action to reduce methane emissions from the waste sector, as unmanaged waste continues to create serious climate, environmental, health and economic risks across the region.

The call came at the Regional Kick-off and Capacity Building Workshop on “Accelerating Waste Methane Mitigation in South Asia for Climate, Health, and Development Co-Benefits,” which commenced on July 15, 2026, at the South Asia Co-operative Environment Programme (SACEP) Headquarters in Colombo.

The four-day workshop, funded by the Global Methane Hub and implemented by the Institute for Global Environmental Strategies (IGES) in collaboration with SACEP, HELP-O and the Climate and Clean Air Coalition (CCAC), brought together government officials, experts, regional organisations, international agencies and other stakeholders to discuss how South Asia can reduce methane emissions from waste.

Methane is a major concern because of its strong climate impact. The workshop report notes that methane has a climate impact 86 times stronger than carbon dioxide over a 20-year period. Globally, the waste sector contributes approximately 20% of human-caused methane emissions.

The challenge is particularly serious in South Asia, where rapid urbanisation and increasing waste generation are putting greater pressure on already limited waste-management systems. The region generates about 70 million tonnes of municipal solid waste each year, while only around 44% is collected. Much of the remaining waste is disposed of through open dumping, burning and littering, creating conditions for methane emissions and contributing to the wider crisis of climate change, biodiversity loss and pollution.

Governments can form policy, but implementation requires the private sector

Dr. Premakumara Jagath Dickella Gamaralalage, Director of the Institute for Global Environmental Strategies (IGES), who moderated the workshop’s high-level panel discussion, placed strong emphasis on the need for coordinated action involving governments, businesses, scientists and communities.

He thanked the Government of Sri Lanka for hosting and inaugurating the first kick-off meeting and recognised SACEP as a long-standing partner since 2019. He also highlighted cooperation with the SAARC Chamber of Commerce and Industry and the Sri Lanka Chamber of Commerce on the role of businesses in advancing circular economy principles and reducing waste.

Dr. Premakumara said that addressing short-lived climate pollutants requires a “whole-of-society” approach involving governments, private industry, academia and scientific communities.

While governments can formulate policies, he pointed out that implementation requires the involvement of the private sector. At the same time, scientists and researchers are needed to produce the data and scientific evidence required to measure progress.

The immediate objective of the project is to develop a regional roadmap with specific targets for South Asian countries to collectively reduce methane emissions from the waste sector. The project will also develop scientific knowledge products and conduct gap analyses using data already collected from participating countries.

Importantly, the proposed roadmap is not intended to create completely new targets. Instead, it will seek to align regional action with existing commitments, including the goal of a 30% reduction in methane emissions under the Global Methane Pledge.

Dr. Premakumara also highlighted a major weakness in waste-sector projects: technologies may be available, but projects can fail once external funding ends. He cited composting initiatives that stopped after project completion as an example.

The workshop therefore focused not only on technology but also on financing, implementation, long-term sustainability and lessons that can be shared between South Asian countries.

Mr. Donovan Storey, Technology and Economic Assessment Panel Manager of the CCAC, warned that pollution is already slowing overall economic progress and becoming increasingly expensive for the region to manage.

He highlighted the wide-ranging effects of greenhouse gases and pollutants on human health, ecosystems, agricultural productivity, monsoon patterns and energy systems.

While acknowledging the importance of better data, Storey said there is already sufficient information to begin taking action. His message was that “actions create data”, stressing the need to move from prolonged analysis to practical implementation of proven solutions.

He also pointed out that pollutants such as methane and black carbon do not stop at national borders. Their impacts cross countries, making isolated national approaches insufficient.

Regional organisations such as SACEP therefore have an important role in sharing knowledge and developing “champions of change”. Storey also stressed that policies must involve communities directly, including farmers in relation to agricultural emissions and informal-sector workers involved in waste management.

A central issue raised during the workshop was the lack of finance for waste methane mitigation.

The panel argued that waste should not be treated only as a costly public service. Organic waste, in particular, can become a resource and a feedstock for businesses involved in composting, waste-to-energy projects and Black Soldier Fly farming.

However, direct grants alone are not enough to meet the region’s needs. The panel called for blended finance, de-risking mechanisms, first-loss guarantees and viability-gap funding to encourage private-sector investment.

Another challenge is the lack of proven and easily replicated business models. Standardised legal, contractual and operational frameworks could help municipalities and private investors develop projects that are financially viable and easier to replicate.

The panel also identified institutional silos as a major problem. Waste-management departments, municipal authorities and national climate ministries often operate separately, making coordinated action difficult.

The workshop stressed the need for stronger bureaucratic leadership, flexible procurement systems, public-private partnership frameworks and regional standards.

The panel called for harmonised Measurement, Reporting and Verification (MRV) systems across South Asia. Without standardised data, countries face difficulties in measuring progress, comparing results and accessing performance-based international climate finance.

Participants also highlighted the need for better coordination between countries because both climate impacts and waste-related pollution cross national boundaries.

The workshop’s open discussions identified data scarcity, the integration of informal waste workers, political commitment and sustained government support as key regional challenges. Participants stressed that policies must be supported by adequate resources and enforcement.

Sri Lanka’s own waste sector presents significant opportunities for methane reduction.

The country generates more than 10,000 tonnes of municipal solid waste per day, with an estimated 62% organic fraction. Around 55% of municipal solid waste is disposed of through open dumps, while approximately 20% is treated through composting or anaerobic digestion. Sri Lanka’s waste-sector methane emissions were estimated at approximately 0.66 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent in 2021. Municipal solid-waste disposal accounted for the largest share, followed by incineration and open burning, biological treatment and wastewater treatment.

Sri Lanka has signed the Global Methane Pledge and has set a waste-sector greenhouse-gas reduction target of 20.8% by 2035, comprising unconditional and conditional components.

However, the country faces several barriers, including limited sanitary landfill capacity and gas recovery, inadequate wastewater treatment, weak source segregation, high capital and operating costs, fragmented institutional responsibilities and incomplete data.

The workshop identified the need to strengthen methane monitoring, improve inventory methodologies, develop methane-related regulations, increase access to carbon markets and establish better data platforms.

Priority interventions include sanitary landfills with gas capture, anaerobic digestion and biogas systems, blended climate finance and stronger MRV systems.

On the fourth day, delegates visited the Kaduwela Municipal Waste Facility, which includes a biogas unit, Material Recovery Facility and compost plant. The facility combines biological treatment and resource recovery to divert waste from landfills and reduce methane emissions.

The delegation also visited the Karadiyana semi-aerobic landfill and the Moratuwa Municipal Council Material Recovery Facility.

At Moratuwa, delegates observed waste segregation, recycling and composting operations. Dry waste is sorted into recyclable materials such as plastics, paper, metals and glass, while organic waste is processed into compost.

The visits provided participants from the eight SACEP member countries with practical examples of waste-management approaches being implemented in Sri Lanka. Delegates from Maldives and Bhutan, facing their own challenges linked to land scarcity and waste generation, expressed interest in adapting aspects of the Moratuwa model to their own circumstances.

The workshop is expected to lead to the development of a regional methane mitigation roadmap. IGES and SACEP will prepare an initial outline based on discussions, followed by stakeholder and ministry feedback, an online review process and a second technical meeting before the final document is submitted to the SACEP Secretariat for consideration at a ministerial meeting.

The central message emerging from the Colombo workshop was that South Asia cannot afford to treat waste methane as a future problem.

The technology exists. The need for action is clear. But without finance, reliable data, strong institutions, private-sector participation and long-term implementation, methane reduction commitments may remain largely on paper.

For Dr. Premakumara and other participants, the priority is therefore to turn regional commitments into practical projects — and to ensure that those projects continue long after individual programmes and funding cycles end.
