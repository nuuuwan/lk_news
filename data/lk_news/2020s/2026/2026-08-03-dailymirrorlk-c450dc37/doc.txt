Inflation hits three-year high as CB’s target breach stretches to fourth month

Sri Lanka’s headline inflation accelerated to 7.3 percent in July 2026, up from 6.8 percent in June — the highest reading since June 2023, when inflation was still unwinding from the 2022 economic crisis — marking the fourth consecutive month the Colombo Consumer Price Index (CCPI) has run above the Central Bank’s 5 percent target, as food prices surged and wage growth failed to keep pace with the cost of living.

Data released by the Central Bank of Sri Lanka (CBSL) showed the CCPI, on a year-on-year basis, has climbed steadily since crossing the target threshold in April this year - from 5.4 percent in April to 5.5 percent in May, 6.8 percent in June and now 7.3 percent in July. Food inflation nearly doubled month-on-month in year-on-year terms, jumping to 6.3 percent in July from 3.6 percent in June, which the Central Bank attributed largely to a base effect. Non-food inflation eased slightly to 7.8 percent from 8.4 percent, though it remained the bigger driver of the latest monthly increase, with an adjustment to housing rents contributing 0.23 percentage points of the CCPI’s 0.24 percent month-on-month rise.

Core inflation, which strips out volatile food and energy items, also accelerated, rising to 4.4 percent in July from 4.0 percent in June, pointing to broader, more entrenched price pressures across the economy.

The pain is uneven across household budgets. Transport costs remain the standout burden, up 17.2 percent year-on-year in July, even after easing from 18.7 percent in June. Restaurants and hotels rose 10.6 percent, education climbed to 7.2 percent from 6.5 percent, and miscellaneous goods and services rose to 6.2 percent from 5.8 percent - all categories that eat directly into discretionary middle-class spending.

Wage growth has not kept up. Central Bank data show nominal wages rose about 9 percent for informal private sector employees and about 5 percent for public sector employees in the year to June - both trailing headline inflation, leaving public sector workers worse off in real terms even before July’s acceleration is factored in.

The sustained breach of the 5 percent target — agreed under the Monetary Policy Framework Agreement (MPFA) in October 2023 — comes just as the Central Bank is reassessing whether that target is still the right one. In June, the Bank opened a public consultation on the inflation target and related parameters ahead of a new MPFA expected around October 2026, when the current agreement lapses under the three-year review requirement in the new Central Bank Act; some critics have argued the 5 percent target is too high and should be lowered to as low as 2 percent.

The Bank’s own quarterly fan chart, published alongside the July monetary policy round, shows inflation crossing the target line around the second quarter of 2026 and continuing to climb, with the central forecast path appearing to peak in the high single digits around the third or fourth quarter before easing through 2027 and converging back near target only by early 2028 — with the confidence bands around that path widening sharply the further out the projection runs, underscoring how uncertain even the Bank considers its own medium-term outlook to be.

The Bank has been explicit that the chart is a projection rather than a commitment, and has flagged elevated uncertainty stemming from the fluid Middle East conflict and its spillovers into global shipping, energy prices and domestic economic activity as key risks to that outlook.
