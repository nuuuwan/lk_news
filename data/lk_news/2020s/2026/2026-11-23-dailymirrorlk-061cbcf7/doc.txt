Belihuloya: Hill-country gateway  Sri Lanka keeps driving past

For generations, Belihuloya has been a place Sri Lankans pass through rather than linger in. The travellers on the A4 between Colombo and the hills would pull in at the old rest house, eat rice and curry within an earshot of the Belihul Oya tumbling over its boulders and press on towards Haputale, Bandarawela or Ella, before the afternoon mist settled over the ridges.

EKHO Hotels and Resorts, the hotel brand of Ceylon Hotels Corporation PLC (CHC), is betting that the pause can become a stay. The rest house has been relaunched as EKHO Belihuloya and the team behind it is working to turn the surrounding valley into a destination in its own right rather than a lunch stop.

“Most of the tourists who come here are passing through from the Bandarawela side,” EKHO naturalist Gayathri Tissera observed.

“This location has not yet become a tourism pinpoint. It has not developed enough as a destination.”

CHC Marketing and Communications Manager Sherin Hamid conceded that Belihuloya rarely features on the itineraries of foreign holidaymakers.

“At the moment, a lot of foreign travellers don’t know Belihuloya as a destination; so, most of the time it doesn’t come under the itinerary,” she noted.

Hamid sees part of the opening in Ella’s own success, which has turned the hill town into one of the island’s most sought-after stops.

“Ella is now sort of too crowded; so, we want to promote Belihuloya in terms of experiences,” she explained.

“There are a lot of operators who are focusing on Belihuloya at the moment; so, I think it could be a great start for the destination.”

“The travel agents who spoke to me said that if we promote the activities here properly, this can become a destination,” she remarked.

She agreed that the valley holds the kind of promise Ella once did, provided it is not overrun in the same way.

The valley packs a remarkable range of terrain into a short radius. Belihuloya sits at a little over 600 metres, where the wet and dry zones meet and the hill country begins its descent towards the southern plains; yet, within a short drive, the land climbs to the edge of Horton Plains.

The waterfalls come first. A short drive up the lane beside the rest house leads to Pahanthuduwa Ella, named for the lamp-like shape of its base. Bambarakanda Ella, at 263 metres, the island’s tallest waterfall, plunges down a forested cliff about 18 kilometres away.

Smaller cascades such as Surathali Ella and Papulagala Ella are scattered across the surrounding hills.

“As far as I have learned, there is a line of waterfalls running all along this area and there is rock climbing too,” Tissera pointed out, adding that the operators with climbing equipment are based in nearby Bandarawela.

Above the valley rise the ridgelines. Hawagala, a summit of roughly 1,330 metres a few kilometres from town, rewards a rough three-kilometre climb with views across Samanala Wewa towards Horton Plains.

Higher still, the road through Nonpareil Estate winds for 11 kilometres, climbing more than 900 metres, to Baker’s Bend. This horseshoe-shaped viewpoint sits at about 1,530 metres and according to local lore, takes its name from a colonial-era estate manager whose horse slipped to its death on the bend. On clear mornings, the Samanala and Udawalawe reservoirs glint on the horizon.

Then there is the Devil’s Staircase, an old estate road of hairpin bends climbing from the Bambarakanda area to the road between Ohiya and Horton Plains. The 22-stage Pekoe Trail also runs nearby, with its Horton Plains stages crossing the plateau above.

Yet, several of these routes have quietly shifted from footpaths to jeep tracks, a gap that a hotel built around experiences is well placed to fill.

“There are places that used to be hikes and now they have been converted into drives. The Devil’s Staircase is the same, they drive up that road now,” EKHO Belihuloya Resident Manager Ruwindra Dias acknowledged.

“We have a couple of excursions, which I have to explore myself before I take guests out.”

Tissera, a self-taught wildlife photographer, who previously worked at Aitken Spence, was handed a clear brief.

“When my CEO came here, he told me to put together all the activities that can be done here if we are to make this a tourism destination,” she recalled.

“I don’t usually like to do things the way they are written in a book. I like to try them practically because what is in a book is someone else’s experience,” Tissera reflected.

That philosophy extends to the property’s reptilian residents. Tissera handles snakes herself and has asked the garden staff not to kill any they find but to call her, so that she can move them.

“Nobody taught me. I just watched and from that day I have been catching animals,” she said.

“More than calling it eco, we must develop it on the sustainable side,” Tissera stressed.

“But I see many places now making sustainability a marketing point. It has to be done genuinely.”

The property itself straddles past and present. EKHO Belihuloya now has 16 rooms, all in the deluxe category, after the old staff quarters upstairs were converted into guest rooms. The larger rooms on the far side interconnect for families, Dias noted. The grounds stretch across more than five acres.

Its most beguiling feature, however, lies at the foot of the garden. The Belihul Oya flows along the edge of the property, pooling between boulders in its shallows. It is one of the principal tributaries of the Walawe River, which rises on the slopes of Sri Pada and runs 138 kilometres to the sea at Ambalantota.

The guests who dip their feet in the water are soon surrounded by shoals of small fish nibbling at the skin, a free, natural version of the fish pedicure that spas elsewhere charge for.

The island’s rocky streams are home to the Ceylon stone sucker, Garra ceylonensis, known locally as ‘gal paandiya’, an endemic fish whose habit of nibbling dead skin has made it a sought-after “doctor fish” in the spa trade. A sucker-like disc lets it cling to rocks and graze on algae in fast-flowing water.

Scientists have found that populations in different river basins, though identical in appearance, belong to six genetically distinct lineages and the species’ popularity in the spa and aquarium trade has seen it listed nationally as near threatened.

“The staff we have right now are from the old staff. They have been working here for a long time and they know those signature dishes,” Dias explained.

He was referring to the fried fish and fried ‘handalla’ that even the first-time guests still ask about when they call. The rice and curry buffet now runs at weekends, when the crowds return.

“It’s not much but they love to have that next to the river,” he added.

“Earlier it was more of a rice and curry spot that everyone stopped at. Now we have added more international dishes and more dishes that reflect the ingredients of the area that we could source,” Hamid observed.

Beyond the table, EKHO has partnered with operator Belihuloya Safari on customisable packages to Nonpareil Estate and Baker’s Bend, each taking in two or three points of interest.

Stargazing, signature dining and in time, glamping on the grounds are also in the pipeline.

The hotel is currently offering an introductory rate of Rs.30,000 for a deluxe double room, with a complimentary upgrade to half board until the end-November.

The cycling groups riding from Bandarawela down to Avissawella have already begun using the hotel as a rest stop, with more groups due this month, according to Hamid.

EKHO is also working closely with the Sri Lanka Tourism Development Authority, which has its own plan to uplift Belihuloya. The property is being grouped with EKHO’s Ella and Kithulgala hotels under an EKHO Adventure banner, pitched at specialist adventure travel agents.

If Belihuloya is to become the next Ella, those shaping it would rather it did not become another Ella. For now, the hotel’s ambition is to be the reason the travellers stop driving past.

“For us, it’s more of promoting the destination and making this place a hub for people to come and stay when visiting Belihuloya,” Hamid said.
