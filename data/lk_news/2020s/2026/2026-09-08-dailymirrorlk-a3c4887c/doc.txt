Where have our fish gone?

Colombo, Sep. 8 (Daily Mirror) - Sri Lanka is witnessing a significant decline in its stocks of small pelagic fish, including sardines, sardinellas and anchovies, with the standing stock of pelagic species now estimated at only around 20,000 tonnes in the surveyed waters, according to Norwegian fisheries scientist Jens-Otto Krakstad.

Krakstad, a senior scientist at Norway’s Institute of Marine Research and a lead researcher of the recent study on Sri Lanka’s marine fish stocks, said the decline was particularly evident among clupeoids – a fish family that includes several species commonly consumed in Sri Lanka.

These include Halmessa, Sudaya, Hendalla, Hurulla and Salaya, which are important sources of affordable protein for Sri Lankans.

“We are talking now about standing stock of fish in the survey area of around 20,000 tonnes of pelagic species which are very popular in the market,” he said in an interview with Daily Mirror.

He said the decline observed in the 2018 survey appeared to have continued, although preliminary data from a new survey conducted this year had yet to be analysed.

The findings are contained in a study published in Frontiers in Marine Science, which compares fisheries survey data collected around Sri Lanka by the Norwegian research vessel Dr. Fridtjof Nansen between 1978 and 1980 with the findings of a more comprehensive survey conducted in 2018.

The earlier surveys recorded total fish biomass of between 280,000 and 395,000 tonnes, while the 2018 fisheries-acoustic survey recorded only 121,000 tonnes. A separate bottom-trawl swept-area method estimated an additional 50,000 tonnes.

According to the study, pelagic fish biomass recorded through acoustic surveys fell from 150,000 tonnes in 1978 to just 21,000 tonnes in 2018. The distribution of pelagic fish also changed considerably, with the east coast almost devoid of pelagic species during the 2018 survey. Most of the remaining clupeoid biomass was concentrated in an area off Negombo.

The species recorded in the Negombo area included Sardinella gibbosa (Salaya), Sardinella albella (Sudaya), Amblygaster sirm (Hurulla), Amblygaster clupeoides (Gal Hurulla) and Amblygaster leiogaster (Keeramin).

The researchers also found that the average length of several commercially important fish species had declined substantially over the four decades. For several important pelagic and demersal species, average length declined by about 50%, a pattern the researchers said could indicate the effects of overexploitation.
