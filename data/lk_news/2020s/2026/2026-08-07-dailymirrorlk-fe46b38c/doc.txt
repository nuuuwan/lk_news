Judiciary not to be adjusted according to the convenience of the day

The government is apparently determined to proceed with the constitutional amendment to extend the retirement age of Supreme Court and Court of Appeal judges despite objections from relevant quarters including the legal fraternity. It is a test of the government’s politeness, judicial independence and its understanding of the delicate balance of power that underpins a democratic state.

Whether the extension is by two years or more is not the central issue. The real concern is whether the Executive and Legislature should alter the rules governing the tenure of sitting judges at a time when the judiciary is expected to function as the final guardian of the Constitution. Even if the government’s intentions are entirely benign, the perception created by such a move is one that Sri Lanka can ill afford.

The judiciary is one institution that should remain beyond political calculations. Unlike other sectors of public administration where retirement ages can be revised according to labour market needs or demographic trends, the superior judiciary occupies a unique constitutional position. Judges are not ordinary public servants. Their independence depends not only on security of tenure but also on public confidence that neither rewards nor extensions are used to influence judicial conduct.

Governments across the world have learned that attempts to change judicial tenure invariably attract suspicion. It is not because every such reform is unconstitutional,  but because judicial independence depends as much on perception as on legal safeguards. Justice must not only be done; it must also be seen to be done. Likewise, judicial independence must not only exist but also be seen to exist.

The government’s proposal has already encountered resistance from the Bar Association of Sri Lanka, constitutional experts and opposition parties. Such opposition should not be dismissed as mere politics. Once governments begin altering the rules governing judges during their tenure, the line separating institutional reform from political convenience becomes blurred.

International experience provides ample lessons. In Hong Kong, former Chief Justice Geoffrey Ma chose to retire upon reaching the prescribed retirement age despite legislative changes that allowed extensions under certain circumstances. His decision reinforced an important principle—that institutions are often strengthened not by exercising available powers but by exercising restraint.

Sri Lanka has struggled for decades to rebuild confidence in its democratic institutions.

The European Union, Commonwealth institutions and international legal organisations have consistently emphasised judicial independence as a cornerstone of democratic governance. While they may not directly intervene in domestic constitutional matters, developments that appear to weaken institutional safeguards often find their way into governance assessments, human rights reports and discussions on trade preferences. At a time when Sri Lanka is attempting to project itself as a stable democracy committed to reform, it would be unwise to invite unnecessary questions about its constitutional direction.

The government argues that experienced judges should be retained to reduce case backlogs and preserve institutional memory. However, extending the retirement age is a temporary administrative solution to what is fundamentally a structural problem. Judicial delays stem from procedural inefficiencies, inadequate staffing, outdated court administration, insufficient digitalisation and staff shortages.

Moreover, constitutional amendments should never be tailored to meet immediate administrative needs or accommodate particular individuals. Constitutions are designed for generations, not governments. If there is genuine consensus that retirement ages should be reviewed, such reforms should be considered prospectively, after broad consultation with the judiciary, the legal profession, constitutional scholars and civil society, and should apply only to future appointments. That would eliminate any perception that changes are intended to benefit  specific judges.

The government’s overwhelming parliamentary majority provides it with the numbers to amend the Constitution. But constitutional legitimacy requires more than numerical strength. The judiciary has neither the purse nor the sword. Its authority rests entirely on public confidence in its impartiality. Once that confidence is weakened, rebuilding it becomes exceedingly difficult. Governments come and go through elections. Judges retire. Institutions, however, must endure.

This is why the government should reconsider its course. There are moments in governance when exercising power is less important than choosing not to exercise it. The judiciary is not an institution to be adjusted according to the convenience of the day.
