Israeli poll deals blow to world peace

The Israelis have spoken democratically to reaffirm that they glorify hardliners. They had little choice. There were hardly any candidates with peace credentials. The two main extremist-led blocs that contested the election produced a result in which neither emerged as the overall winner.

Although Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud-led right-wing bloc is leading with 53 seats, it remains well short of the 61 seats required to control the 120-seat Knesset and form a government. His rival, Gadi Eisenkot, and his Change Bloc won 48 seats. The bloc representing the Palestinians, who are deliberately referred to as ‘Israeli Arabs’ in a sinister bid to erase their Palestinian identity, won 19 seats, much to the chagrin of both Zionist blocs.

Tuesday’s general election was the first since the Zionist regime unleashed its genocidal campaign against Gaza’s civilians, ostensibly to punish Hamas, which was responsible for the October 7 attacks on Israeli border villages that once belonged to the Palestinians.

There is no denying that Netanyahu and Eisenkot were two sides of the same evil coin. Both spoke the language of genocide regarding Gaza. Both subscribe to the dangerous and expansionist Greater Israel ideology and show scant respect for international law. Both support the Gaza genocide, the war on Iran, and the military occupation of Lebanon and Syria. The election campaign debate was not about the war’s illegality but about who could better wipe out Iran and punish the Palestinians.

Obviously, neither the illegality of the Gaza genocide nor that of the war on Iran was an election issue in the nation that US politicians and the legacy media often hail as the Middle East’s only democracy to mask its crimes against humanity.

Some say Eisenkot, the chief of staff of the Israeli Defence Forces (IDF) until 2024, could be worse than Netanyahu. For it was he who authored the dreaded Dahiya doctrine, named after a Lebanese village during a previous Israeli aggression. The doctrine simply states that deterrence must be created by inflicting disproportionate pain on civilians. He is the architect of the aggressive Gaza genocide, which Netanyahu adopted with some fine-tuning. When it comes to Israeli settlement-building activities, he criticises Netanyahu, not because he sees settlements as illegal but because he proposes a silent and cautious settlement-building policy that will not draw Western sanctions.

The political deadlock works in favour of Netanyahu. The longer it continues, the longer he will remain caretaker prime minister. Netanyahu is unlikely to strike a deal with the Arabs to form the next government.

The Arab bloc was every bit as jubilant as the two main blocs when the results were announced. Voter turnout in Arab areas was unprecedented, at more than 77 per cent. They think they will be kingmakers and are willing to extend their support to the opposition bloc to form a government. They think they will be able to check the next government’s aggressive actions in Gaza, the West Bank and other Arab nations.

But Eisenkot faces a dilemma. His coalition comprises both hardliners and Democrats, the residue from the left-leaning Labour Party, which has virtually disappeared from Israeli politics that has become hardliners’ domain. Labour was the least adamant regarding Palestinians’ right to statehood. If Eisenkot embraces the Arab bloc, it will make Netanyahu’s task of splitting the opposition easier. Netanyahu is banking on the defection of former prime minister Naftali Bennett, former minister Avigdor Lieberman and their supporters to cobble together a majority. Both Bennett and Lieberman detest Netanyahu and would be happy to see him behind bars on corruption charges ranging from accepting expensive gifts to offering regulatory benefits to media groups in return for favourable coverage. Bennett’s party is likely to win ten seats and Lieberman’s seven. Lieberman once posted a message on X: “There are no innocents in Gaza.” Bennett supports Greater Israel, which includes not only the Gaza Strip and the West Bank but also parts of Egypt, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, Iraq and Saudi Arabia.

But they detest an alliance with Arabs even more, as their political survival depends on their anti-Arab racist credentials. So Netanyahu is sitting pretty. The only segment within the Eisenkot bloc willing to sit with the Arab bloc is Yair Golan’s Democrat Party, which emerged from the now-defunct Labour Party. But it is a minority.

Another factor working in Netanyahu’s favour is US President Donald Trump’s endorsement. Trump openly backed Netanyahu, and they are partners in crime in many an international conflict, especially the Iran war.

The new session of the Knesset will begin on November 10, a full week after the US midterms. The interim period gives both Trump and Netanyahu an opportunity to resume the war on Iran. Some analysts had predicted that Netanyahu, being a seasoned politician, would attack Iran days before the Israeli election in a bid to postpone it and hang on to power to avoid facing trial on corruption charges. It did not happen. But there is a possibility that he, together with Trump, will resume the war once the midterms are over, thus creating a security crisis. The war would put the political crisis on the back burner and suspend the government-formation process until the dust settles.

According to political analyst Ben Ephraim, Netanyahu has told Trump that he was willing to form a unity government without hardcore genocide backers such as Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezaled Smotrich.

If talks on a unity government fail, Netanyahu could exploit the engineered security and political crises, thereby paving the way for a fresh election, as has happened before. Given Netanyahu’s unscrupulousness and political shrewdness, which make Eisenkot’s challenge look amateurish, he could resort to some form of gerrymandering to ensure lower Arab voter turnout at the next election, paving the way for an outright victory for his Likud-led bloc.

While Netanyahu is determined to remain in power and is leaving no stone unturned to prompt dozens of opposition-bloc members to defect, the West Asian region is being pushed to the brink of a devastating new phase of the US-Israel war against Iran. Ahead of this possible flare-up, diplomacy was at its peak this week, with Mecca Defence Pact leadersSaudi Arabia’s Defence Minister Khalid bin Salman, Pakistan’s military chief Asim Munir and Türkiye’s intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalinvisiting Tehran for talks with Iranian leaders aimed at launching a peace process.

But very little information has been revealed to the media, prompting wild speculation. Some said the visitors warned Iran of pending American and Israeli attacks. Others said the Mecca Pact trio cautioned Iran that if Saudi Arabia were attacked, they would have no option but to join a Saudi counterattack. Yet others said the talks were aimed at seeking Iran’s intervention to bring about a ceasefire between Saudi Arabia and Yemen’s Houthis.

There is very little good news for the Palestinians, the West Asian region and the rest of the world in the Israeli election results. With Trump in charge of the United States, the world’s foremost military power, though certainly not moral power, and Netanyahu in charge of Israel, world peace and justice for the Palestinians, who are being subjected to genocide or ethnic cleansing, are as elusive as the lost city of Atlantis.
