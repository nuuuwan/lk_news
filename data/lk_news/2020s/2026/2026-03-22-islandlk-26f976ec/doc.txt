Sri Lanka’s biodiversity: A living laboratory from backyard to ocean depths

Sri Lanka’s extraordinary biodiversity — spanning everything from ants in home gardens to blue whales in its surrounding oceans — took centre stage at Thursday’s public lecture that highlighted the island’s status as one of the world’s most remarkable natural treasure troves.

The lecture, titled “The Amazing Biodiversity of Sri Lanka: An Overview of the Plants and Animals on this Magical Island,” was organised by the Wildlife and Nature Protection Society, in collaboration with Nations Trust Bank, and delivered by renowned naturalist Gehan de Silva Wijeyeratne, on Thursday, at the Bandaranaike Memorial International Conference Hall (BMICH).

In a wide-ranging and engaging presentation, Wijeyeratne took the audience on a journey across the tree of life, demonstrating how Sri Lanka functions as a living laboratory of evolution, with high levels of endemism and an astonishing diversity of species — many still inadequately studied.

“Biodiversity is not just about the big, charismatic animals people pay money to come and see. It is much wider — and often right there in your backyard,” he stressed, setting the tone for a lecture that challenged conventional perceptions of wildlife.

Beginning with insects, Wijeyeratne revealed a hidden world of complex interactions and extraordinary adaptations. Far from being insignificant, insects represent some of the most sophisticated ecological relationships found in nature.

Certain butterfly species, for instance, engage in mutually beneficial relationships with ants. The caterpillars produce sugary secretions that attract ants, which in turn provide protection from predators. However, this relationship comes at a cost.

“You give us honey, and in return the ants get to eat some of your babies. It’s a bizarre trade-off, but that’s how the animal world operates,” he explained.

“If it’s hungry, it will eat its siblings. It’s cannibalistic — that’s how the animal world operates,” he added, referring to certain “blue” butterflies found in and around Colombo.

Dragonflies, another group highlighted in the lecture, illustrate Sri Lanka’s ecological significance. Of the 133 species recorded on the island, approximately 66 percent are endemic. Within this group lies the genus Ceylonosticta, or shadow damselflies, comprising 23 species found nowhere else on Earth.

“These are incredibly special insects,” he noted, emphasising their uniqueness.

Wijeyeratne also described one of nature’s most astonishing phenomena — the migration of the globe skimmer dragonfly.

“They start from India, come to Sri Lanka and the Maldives, lay eggs, and the next generation continues to Africa. It’s an intergenerational migration — the longest known in insects,” he said.

A central theme of the lecture was accessibility. Sri Lanka’s biodiversity is not confined to remote jungles but exists all around us — even within urban environments.

“You don’t have to go far from Colombo to see amazing endemic biodiversity — you just need to know where to look,” Wijeyeratne said.

He recounted how dragonflies are sometimes deceived by modern urban landscapes.

“The wandering skimmer will try to lay its eggs on car roofs because it mistakes the reflection for a water body,” he said, drawing laughter from the audience.

Even a simple rock pool, he explained, can reveal multiple branches of life.

“If you really want to see biodiversity across major animal groups, just look into a rock pool,” he added.

Moving beyond insects, Wijeyeratne highlighted Sri Lanka’s remarkable diversity of molluscs, particularly land snails. With nearly 89 percent of species being endemic, the island ranks among the world’s most significant hotspots for these often-overlooked creatures.

“It’s hard to imagine, but land snails, marine snails and squid all belong to the same phylum,” he explained, illustrating the surprising connections within the tree of life.

He also discussed echinoderms — starfish and sea urchins — which can be found in coastal rock pools and represent another major branch of animal evolution.

“These simple habitats can show you an incredible cross-section of life,” he noted.

“What we call fish is actually a complicated grouping — it’s not a simple evolutionary category,” he explained.

“I am more closely related to a humphead wrasse than that fish is to a shark,” highlighting the complexities of evolutionary relationships.

“When the dominant male dies, the female can change sex and take over. It’s an amazing adaptation that ensures continuity,” he said.

Freshwater species, such as killifish, demonstrate equally fascinating resilience. Their eggs can survive in dry conditions and hatch when water returns.

“You might even find fish appearing in a puddle in your garden after rain, because the eggs were lying dormant,” he said.

Sri Lanka’s amphibians represent one of its greatest scientific success stories. Of the 107 recorded species, an extraordinary 88 percent are endemic.

Wijeyeratne described recent decades as a “renaissance” in biodiversity research, with scientists uncovering a wealth of previously unknown species.

“This work has transformed our understanding of Sri Lanka’s biodiversity,” he said.

The lecture also challenged common assumptions about reptiles and birds.

“The crocodile is more closely related to the bird in your garden than to a lizard,” Wijeyeratne explained, illustrating evolutionary divergence.

Sri Lanka is home to 34 endemic bird species, many of which display complex behaviours, such as cooperative breeding, where older offspring assist in raising younger siblings.

“These behaviours are not just fascinating — they tell us a lot about evolution and survival strategies,” he said.

Turning to marine ecosystems, Wijeyeratne highlighted Sri Lanka’s global significance for whale watching, particularly for blue whales — the largest animals ever to have lived.

“They evolved from land mammals — something like a small deer that went back into the sea,” he explained.

Sperm whales, he noted, represent extreme adaptations, capable of diving to great depths in total darkness.

“They rely on echolocation, much like bats, because vision doesn’t work in those conditions,” he said.

“People confuse poor visibility with poor biodiversity. We don’t lack marine life — we are very rich,” he emphasised.

Wijeyeratne acknowledged the growing importance of wildlife tourism, noting that Sri Lanka has seen a rapid increase in safari operators.

“We’ve gone from not recognising we are a top wildlife destination to perhaps realising it too well. Now we must manage it,” he cautioned.

While tourism presents economic opportunities, it also poses challenges that require careful regulation and sustainable practices.

On conservation, Wijeyeratne was candid about the challenges faced by communities living near protected areas.

“At the end of the day, it comes down to money,” he said, noting that economic pressures often drive environmental degradation.

He stressed the need for a multi-level approach — from individual responsibility to national policy and private sector involvement — to ensure effective conservation.

Wijeyeratne also warned of the growing impact of climate change, which is already altering species distribution patterns.

Bird species, once confined to dry zones, are increasingly appearing in wetter regions, while those in mountainous areas face the risk of extinction.

“Things in the mountains can’t go any higher — so they will disappear,” he cautioned.

Concluding his lecture, Wijeyeratne reminded Sri Lankans of the privilege of living in such a biologically rich country.

“We are incredibly privileged to have this biodiversity, sometimes right in our own backyards,” he said.

His message was clear: Sri Lanka’s biodiversity is not only a scientific marvel but also a vital national asset — one that, if properly understood and managed, could play a crucial role in the country’s environmental sustainability and economic future.

The lecture served as both a celebration of Sri Lanka’s natural heritage and a call to action — urging greater awareness, stronger conservation efforts and a deeper appreciation of the island’s unparalleled place in the natural world.
