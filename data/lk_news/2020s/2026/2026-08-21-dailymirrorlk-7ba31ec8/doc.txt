Give war a chance:  Iran faces Trump’s  economic  D‑Day

Iranian people endure more economic hardships as the war drags on

Trade between the UAE and Iran is worth more than US$6.2 billion, with the surplus heavily in favour of Abu Dhabi, Iran’s second‑most important economic partner after China

“Give War a Chance” is the title of an article American political scientist Edward N. Luttwak wrote for the prestigious Foreign Affairs magazine in 1999. He argued that premature peacemaking does not lead to permanent peace.

“An unpleasant truth often overlooked is that although war is a great evil, it does have a great virtue: it can resolve political conflicts and lead to peace. This can happen when all belligerents become exhausted or when one wins decisively. Either way the key is that the fighting must continue until a resolution is reached. War brings peace only after passing a culminating phase of violence. Hopes of military success must fade for accommodation to become more attractive than further combat,” says Luttwark in the opening paragraph.

He argues that premature ceasefires tend to arrest war‑induced exhaustion and allow belligerents to reconstitute and rearm their forces. This intensifies and prolongs the struggle once the ceasefire ends — and it usually does end.

Luttwak’s argument, flawed or not, could not be more relevant to the ongoing United States war of aggression against Iran.

As the US‑Iran war continues and the 60‑day Memorandum of Understanding that saw more clashes than talks expired on Monday amid a widening trust deficit between the parties, the two countries seem more determined to exhaust each other in a war of attrition than to strike a deal to end the conflict.

This week, the United States and United Arab Emirates intensified their economic war on Iran. On Wednesday, the UAE announced, “All trade, commercial exchanges, and financial transactions with Iran have been halted until further notice,” accusing Iran of firing ballistic missiles into its territorial waters — an act analysts say will deal a severe blow to Iran’s economy. Hours later, the US announced backbreaking economic sanctions and warned nations against dealing with Iran.

In a Truth Social post, US President Donald Trump described his new salvo — with capital letters for emphasis — as the “MOST CRUSHING ECONOMIC OPERATION EVER TAKEN AGAINST ANY COUNTRY”.

“This will be Economic Warfare and Isolation on an unprecedented scale. Their navy is gone, their air force is destroyed, their military factories are now rubble, their currency is worthless, and their country is hanging by a thread,” he said.

Trump warned that any country supporting Tehran in any way will face “TREMENDOUS Economic Consequences,” but did not mention a specific country or what actions Washington is expected to take. Analysts, however, said the warning is directed at China, which buys more than 80 percent of Iran’s oil.

“This will be an ECONOMIC D‑DAY, and we need all of our Allies to stand with the United States of America to isolate and defeat the Iran threat,” Trump said. “These maniacs are on the ropes, and these HISTORIC MEASURES will cripple them and their ability to project terror worldwide. IRAN WILL NEVER HAVE A NUCLEAR WEAPON.”

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi yesterday dismissed Trump’s newly announced sanctions as a diversion from domestic American issues, warning that the “so-called ‘Economic D-Day’” would only bring further defeat to Washington.

It appears that the US acted in coordination with the UAE. The Gulf state did not resort to economic warfare even as more than 1,000 Iranian missile and drone attacks hit the country in the early stages of the war, though it could have.

Iran yesterday denied firing missiles at the UAE, claiming it was a ‘false flag operation’ by Israel and the US.

Trade between the UAE and Iran is worth more than US$6.2 billion, with the surplus heavily in favour of Abu Dhabi, Iran’s second‑most important economic partner after China. Iran uses the UAE financial platforms to circumvent sanctions, conduct money transfers, and channel its export revenue. The US was not unaware of Iran’s use of UAE loopholes.

Biting indeed, therefore, are these economic sanctions for Iran, which has seen its GDP per capita fall from US$8,000 in 2012 to less than US$5,000 last year, inflation soar to 90 per cent, and the US dollar sell at nearly 1.9 million riyals on the black market. Although Iran is not unfamiliar with dealing with sanctions and can endure the new sanctions, its resolve to fight the US‑led economic warfare has limits.

Since a strong economy is vital not only to bolster the war effort but also to gain public support for it, Iran’s strategy, aimed at exhausting US resolve to continue the fight at a huge economic cost, may backfire. High oil prices may disadvantage the Republican Party in the midterm elections. US military action may be constrained by dwindling weapon stockpiles. US morale may be low, as seen in reports from the USS Abraham Lincoln, deployed in the region for more than nine months. Yet none of these developments offer Iran permanent leverage. Trump will remain in power until the end of 2028. Together with Israel and America’s Gulf allies, he will continue the war by military or other means.

What options is Iran left with? To retaliate. This is the stance of hardliners, especially the Revolutionary Guard leadership, determined to punish the US for starting the war and killing the highly respected supreme leader. If Iran is not allowed to export oil, none in the region will. If Iran suffers economic harm, US allies in the region should also endure similar pain. Such a stance threatens to widen the war.

The hardliners’ position may have been reinforced by Iran’s yet‑to‑be‑exhausted arsenal of missiles and drones. They claim to produce more weapons than they fire at the enemy. Iran may even possess secret weapons not yet battle‑tested. Moreover, they believe they can further tighten their hold on the Strait of Hormuz—which Trump this week declared a US territory—to choke the US and world economy.

Moderates in Iran, however, insist on continuing the talks based on the MoU mediated by Pakistan and Qatar. Former Iranian President Mohammad Khatami this week questioned the hardliners’ stance that Iran’s ‘life and survival lie in war’.

“Some say, ‘First, we will teach America a lesson here, then we will go to the heart of America and fight it there.’ But what reality is this based on?” he asked.

“My feelings were also wounded when they killed our commander, and one’s eyes could almost weep blood. But governing a society cannot be based on raw emotion,” he added. “We must consider what serves the ultimate interests of the country, the system, and Islam.”

Iran’s President Masoud Pezeshkian is also pushing for talks and peace. Last week, he called for an end to what he described as a state of ‘neither war nor peace’ with the US. He said Iran was now in a position of strength, and hence it was the right time to reach an agreement with Washington.

Moderates argue Iran should not overstretch its advantage over the Strait of Hormuz, noting that networks of pipelines can diminish the waterway’s importance.

These differences of opinion do not amount to serious cracks in Iran’s body politic, but the moderates’ view reflects public opinion. The hardliners will find it difficult to ignore it, especially if economic wounds deepen.

Both the US and Iran should realise that a give-war-a-chance approach aimed at exhausting the other also has its limits.
