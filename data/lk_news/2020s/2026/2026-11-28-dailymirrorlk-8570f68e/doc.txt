Imran Khan debate: Sri Lanka should stay out of domestic affairs of other countries

Last week, there was a rather disingenuous attempt in Parliament by the political opposition seeking a debate over the imprisonment of former Pakistani Cricket legend and deposed prime minister, Imran Khan. Another involved a call for a discussion on transboundary pollution originating from India.

As a matter of fact, these concerns deserve attention; however, Parliament is not the forum to discuss the internal affairs of another country, or, as with the second well-founded concern, transboundary pollution, to take a swipe at a friendly nation to score cheap political points and Facebook views. Both motions, if they had their day, would have harmed the country’s bilateral relations with two close allies.  Since opposition MPs or the opposition leader himself were not oblivious to this, it makes them even more self-seeking and opportunistic.

Last week, Samagi Jana Balawegaya MP Ajith Perera submitted an urgent adjournment motion calling for action over Imran Khan’s detention on humanitarian grounds, with recent reports citing his deteriorating health. On the same day, Opposition leader Sajith Premadasa, while criticising the government’s decision to block an adjournment motion on Mr. Khan’s detention, demanded that the government explain deteriorating air quality across the country, the obvious reason being the transboundary air pollution due to agricultural smoke originating from India.

The government rejected the motion, claiming that it would affect bilateral relations.  Non-interference in the internal affairs of other states is a cornerstone of Sri Lanka’s traditionally non-aligned foreign policy. It has helped the country maintain cordial international relations during the throes of a prolonged military conflict at home and growing geopolitical competition in its neighbourhood, though a non-interventionist policy alone could achieve only the bare minimum without a corresponding approach to cultivate economic and trade ties through proactive economic liberalisation, which has sadly been lacking in Sri Lanka’s grand national strategy.

However, the founding pillars of foreign policy should not be uprooted simply because the opposition had nothing else to discuss or because it expects  the NPP-led government as usual to  fall for its trap.

Imran Khan has been in a Rawalpindi jail since August 2023 following a series of corruption convictions after his ouster in a no-confidence motion in 2022. His wife Bushra Bibi has been in jail since January 2025.

Khan led Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI) to victory in the general election in 2018, and served as prime minister until 2022. His troubles began when he fell out with Gen. Asim Munir, then Director General of Inter-Services Intelligence, the all-powerful intelligence agency, and sacked Munir in 2019 after the latter allegedly presented him with allegations of corruption involving Khan’s wife, Bibi.

Munir is now the army chief, elevated to Field Marshal and the man behind Pakistan’s real power. He has taken Pakistan back to the goodwill of the United States, serving as a mediator in the Iran conflict. The PTI remains widely popular and after the party was stripped of its election symbol, Cricket bat, independent candidates backed by PTI won the most number of seats in the general election in 2024, but was kept out of forming a government through a deal reached by Pakistan Muslim League  (Nawaz) and late Benazir Bhutto’s Pakistan Peoples Party (PPP). The PTI also lost its stronghold of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP) after the federal government declared a state of emergency and suspended the PTI-led-provincial government following mass protests demanding Khan’s release.

Khan is a World Cup-winning Pakistani captain and well received in Sri Lanka for his cricketing record. He set up the Imran Khan High Performance  Sports Centre in Colombo during his state visit in 2021 as Prime Minister. He was also credited with nudging the Gotabaya Rajapaksa administration to revoke the COVID-era ban on the burial of Covid-19 victims.

However, real power in Pakistan lies with its military, and Sri Lanka has maintained cordial bilateral relations under every political dispensation in Islamabad. During the war, Pakistan was often described as the friend in need. During the LTTE offensive to retake Jaffna, following the collapse of the Elephant Pass garrison, the first multi-barrel rocket launchers arrived from Pakistan in emergency cargo flights, plucked from field guns, and helped turn the tide at the door of Jaffna.

Without that timely assistance, which came at a time the Chandrika Kumaratunga administration was pondering over emergency troop evacuation from Jaffna, the history of this country could have been very different. Therefore, personal allegiances aside, giving the middle finger to the country that once helped save Jaffna is not the most intelligent foreign policy.

The government was right to reject an adjournment motion, which would have offended both political and military powers in Pakistan.  However, none of that should dissuade the government or the political opposition from conveying their concerns over Khan’s detention to the Pakistani authorities through appropriate channels.

The other aborted debate involved transboundary pollution. Seasonal wind shifts carrying industrial and agricultural smoke have resulted in air quality in much of Sri Lanka deteriorating to the level of ‘unhealthy’ and briefly ‘very unhealthy.’ These are concerns worth redress, but a parliamentary debate where usual charlatans scoring political brownie points could only provoke India’s social media brigades, not the most enlightened types, and ruffle feathers with the Modi administration.

Sajith Premadasa cannot solve the problem of Indian farmers en masse burning their post-harvest stubble, and the government cannot raise a wall against trans boundary pollution. India’s successive efforts to rein in its farmers, even with hefty fines and tracking violators through satellite imagery, have delivered little progress.  Sri Lanka already participates in regional cooperation initiatives such as the Male Declaration on control and prevention of Air pollution and its likely transboundary effects for South Asia, though enforcement mechanisms remain weak.

Another recent concern involves the plans to purchase four Israeli-made surveillance drones for the Sri Lankan Air Force. Rauf Hakeem has challenged the purchase and questioned why it had bypassed the open tender process.

The only Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) the Sri Lankan Air Force ever used were Israeli-made, and during the height of war in 1995, when drone technology was still a domain of a few high-tech nations, Israel sold the first batch of drones and shared technology. The Israeli-made UAVs were instrumental in surveillance and reconnaissance in uncleared areas throughout the war.  However, unlike some industrious states that cultivated a domestic military industry, such as Ukraine, the powerhouse of drone technology, neither Sri Lankan policymakers nor military officials could claim a long-term grand strategy.  And Hakeem’s claims are only petty politics to rile the Muslim voters and cheer up professional social justice warriors.

Sri Lanka’s foreign policy has largely been a continuation, founded on the same premise of non-alignment. The problem is, however, that its foreign policy czars and policymakers, including in this government, lacked a vision beyond statements of solidarity. Sri Lanka should strive to nurture goodwill it earned through its foreign policy into enhanced economic, investment, trade, technology and military cooperation.  Just to say, if its planners have a basic vision, the Sri Lankan military, which spends nearly 1.8 per cent of GDP on defence expenses, all on rations and salaries, could cultivate a domestic arms industry with the help of countries like Israel and Turkey.
