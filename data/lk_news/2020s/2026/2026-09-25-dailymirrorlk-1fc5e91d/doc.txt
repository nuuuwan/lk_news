Development banks should return to equity stakes and board seats, says DFCC CEO

Sri Lanka’s gap lies in access to finance rather than inclusion

Lenders urged to move from collateral to cash flow-based lending

Any new development bank must be independent and commercially viable

Sri Lanka’s development banks should return to taking equity stakes and board seats in the businesses they finance, as they once did, while lenders at large must shift from collateral to cash flow-based lending to reach start-ups, DFCC Bank Director/Chief Executive Officer Thimal Perera stressed.

Perera, who is also an ADFIAP Board Member, was speaking on a panel at the 49th Annual Meetings of the Association of Development Financing Institutions in Asia and the Pacific (ADFIAP), hosted by his bank in Colombo this week.

He drew a sharp distinction between two problems that are often treated as one.

“If you look at Sri Lanka and we talk about financial inclusivity, I don’t think we have an inclusivity issue, because everybody has access to a bank account. The issue we have is access to finance,” he observed.

Small entrepreneurs, small businesses and start-ups, he pointed out, find it impossible to secure bank facilities, leaving them to rely on other means or venture capital. Banks, in his view, must find ways to assess such borrowers and lend to them at a reasonable risk and a reasonable price.

“Without supporting that segment and getting new entrepreneurs and new businesses off the ground, this economy cannot really achieve the objectives that it set for itself,” he warned.

He saw opportunities well beyond fintech, in areas such as agritech and pharmatech. He cited countries such as Germany, where start-up accelerators and financial support form part of a wider ecosystem, and contended that banks have a key role to play in building a similar one in Sri Lanka.

However, he insisted that technology and new risk models would count for little unless lenders changed their approach at the most basic level.  “Yes, we need tech and new ways of evaluating risk, but first and foremost the mindset has to change. We can’t always do collateralised lending, we need to rely on cash flow-based lending,” he stressed.

Going further, Perera identified the retreat of development banks from ownership and governance roles as the biggest gap in the system.

“In the good old days, development banks used to participate in equity and had board seats, so they had an involvement in the running of the company,” he recalled.

The International Finance Corporation (IFC), the World Bank Group’s private sector arm, still held US$ 12,157 million in equity investments at the end of 2025. That is some 17 percent of its US$ 73,224 million disbursed portfolio, invested both directly in companies and through private equity funds. In Germany, which Perera cited as a model, KfW Capital, the venture arm of the state-owned development bank KfW, committed EUR 748 million in 2025. It has now invested around EUR 2.9 billion in 153 venture capital funds, reaching some 2,900 start-ups through fund managers rather than direct stakes of its own. That suggests equity participation remains part of the development finance toolkit, albeit in a changed form.

Meanwhile, Perera held that the case for development banking in Sri Lanka had already been proven.

He pointed to its record in major infrastructure, renewable energy and SME support, and to the needs of the country’s growth plans. DFCC, which he described as the first of the country’s two development banks, was established in 1955 through a partnership between the government and the World Bank. By its own account, the bank financed Sri Lanka’s first private mini-hydro project in 1996, followed by the first private sector wind farm, the first grid-scale solar plant and, in 2021, the country’s first waste-to-energy facility. Funding, he acknowledged, has always been the constraint on long-term, risk-taking development finance. It was the reason DFCC, after 60 years as a development bank, pivoted to commercial banking in 2015 by merging with its subsidiary DFCC Vardhana Bank. He added that the bank’s DNA remains in development banking and that project finance still makes up a large share of its book. Customers approaching a development bank tend to expect subsidised interest rates, he explained. At the same time, the bank itself had to raise deposits in the market and faced difficulties in securing low-cost funding from overseas.

“Then you are no different, and you are in fact at a disadvantage when compared to other commercial banks, because we had restrictions in terms of opening zero-cost CASA accounts and things like that,” he reflected.

DFCC’s CASA ratio stood at 24.49 percent at the end of 2025, against an average of 32.9 percent for ten banks accounting for about 95 percent of the industry, as per an industry review by Boston Consulting Group. That places DFCC at roughly three-quarters of the industry level, a gap that still reflects its development banking legacy. The ratio edged up to 24.99 percent by the end of June 2026, or 29.42 percent when medium to long-term concessionary credit lines are included.

DFCC completed its acquisition of Standard Chartered’s wealth and retail banking business in Sri Lanka on 1 August 2026, taking on around 50,000 customers and 260 employees for a final premium of Rs.3,655 million. Standard Chartered Bank Sri Lanka Chief Executive Officer Bingumal Thewarathanthri shared the panel with Perera.

Acknowledging the renewed debate on development banking, Perera cautioned that any new institution must be insulated from politics and able to pay its own way. His stance contrasts with Thewarathanthri’s call for fiscal incentives to draw capital into the sector.

“These need to be run independently without political interference, they need to be commercially viable, they need to be able to stand on their own feet, because otherwise it becomes a burden on the taxpayer at the end of the day, if the development banks are loss-making,” he maintained.

Fellow panellist and John Keells IT Director/Chief Executive Officer Ramesh Shanmuganathan argued that data and AI-driven models could help close the risk-assessment gap Perera described. He noted that many SME owners lack any credit history for lenders to assess.

“There are better ways of doing it rather than going to a CRIB, where even an SME person may not have a record to even analyse the creditworthiness,” he remarked.

“I think the argument for a proper, well-managed, profitable, independent development bank is there for everybody to see,” Perera asserted.  (NF)
