Sri Lanka opens doors to 1.4 b Chinese, but Sri Lankan traders still face a visa wall to China

China’s Ambassador to Sri Lanka, Wei Huaxiang, has been active in Sri Lankan media and public diplomacy, emphasising China’s development opportunities and the prospects for bilateral cooperation. Beijing has consistently encouraged Sri Lankan businesses to use platforms such as the China International Import Expo and the Canton Fair to access the Chinese market. Yet if Sri Lankan traders cannot obtain a business visa within a reasonable and predictable timeframe, those trade promotion pledges ring hollow

As Sri Lanka rolls out the red carpet for Chinese travellers with free electronic travel authorisations (ETAs), a stark asymmetry is increasingly troubling the island’s business community: Sri Lankan citizens—particularly entrepreneurs who shuttle between Colombo and Guangzhou or Shanghai—continue to navigate a cumbersome, time-consuming and uncertain visa process for China.

This imbalance feels particularly jarring at a time when bilateral relations under the Belt and Road Initiative are supposedly at their warmest. Sri Lankan business circles are now urging the new Chinese Ambassador in Colombo to confront this bottleneck directly and recommend practical solutions to Beijing.

According to Sri Lanka’s Department of Immigration and Emigration, nationals of 40 countries—including China—are eligible for a free tourist ETA valid for 30 days, effective from May 2026. The scheme permits double entry within the validity period. The message to Chinese tourists and business travellers is unambiguous: Sri Lanka is open, and it is free.

The experience for a Sri Lankan travelling in the opposite direction is markedly different.

Since June 2025, the Chinese Embassy in Colombo has operated an online visa processing system. The official guidance is explicit about the timeline: after submitting an application online, applicants must wait 1 to 3 working days for preliminary review. Once approved, they must appear in person at the Embassy to submit their passport and biometric data. The visa processing itself then takes another 3 to 4 working days.

The Embassy does not accept postal applications. For a businessman based in Kandy or Galle, this means two separate trips to Colombo—one to submit documents, another to collect the passport—or the cost of engaging an agent.

In September 2025, the Embassy issued a notice acknowledging that applications had surged following the launch of the online system, and that some applicants had experienced longer processing times. The Embassy advised applicants to submit their materials 15 to 30 days in advance to avoid disrupting travel plans.

For a Sri Lankan entrepreneur with a long history of legitimate travel to China—someone who has attended the Canton Fair or the China International Import Expo multiple times—this process offers little recognition of that track record. Every trip begins anew with the same queue, the same waiting periods and the same uncertainty.

A persistent observation in Colombo’s diplomatic and business circles is that Sri Lanka’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs has not made Chinese visa facilitation a priority lobbying item. The reason, according to several business sources who spoke on condition of anonymity, is structural: senior officials and most Government leaders hold diplomatic or official passports.

Under the 2012 bilateral agreement, holders of diplomatic and official passports between China and Sri Lanka are exempt from visa requirements. Those who shape visa policy do not personally experience the queue, the fingerprinting or the fortnight-long wait that ordinary passport holders endure.

“The people who need to go to China to sell Ceylon tea, gemstones, or to seek manufacturing partnerships are precisely the private sector—the ones with ordinary passports,” one Colombo-based exporter told this correspondent. “We respect China’s sovereignty and security considerations. But when Chinese leaders speak of a ‘community with a shared future’ and the Belt and Road, facilitation of people-to-people flows should not be a one-way street.”

China’s Ambassador to Sri Lanka, Wei Huaxiang has been active in Sri Lankan media and public diplomacy, emphasising China’s development opportunities and the prospects for bilateral cooperation. Beijing has consistently encouraged Sri Lankan businesses to use platforms such as the China International Import Expo and the Canton Fair to access the Chinese market.

Yet if Sri Lankan traders cannot obtain a business visa within a reasonable and predictable timeframe, those trade promotion pledges ring hollow.

China has, in recent years, extended unilateral visa-free entry to ordinary passport holders from dozens of countries—including Japan, Australia, and several European nations—for business, tourism and transit purposes, for stays of up to 30 days. Sri Lanka, while not a wealthy nation, is a key node on the Maritime Silk Road and a long-standing partner. Analysts suggest several possible solutions: multi-year, multiple-entry visas for Sri Lankan business travellers with a clean travel history; a streamlined channel for those attending officially recognised trade fairs or business events; or a reciprocal business travel facilitation arrangement negotiated between the two governments.

The Chinese Embassy has stated its commitment to “facilitating visa applications for Sri Lankan friends travelling to China” and says it will “continue to introduce new measures to facilitate China-Sri Lanka personnel exchanges.” Business leaders say they would welcome concrete steps.

Sri Lanka has extended its hand to Chinese visitors with a free ETA. That hand is open at the airport counter in Katunayake. But when a Sri Lankan trader reaches out to grasp it in return, they find their own hand bound by forms, two mandatory Embassy visits, and a wait that can stretch beyond two weeks.

For a new Ambassador who has already shown a willingness to engage publicly, resolving this “one-way facilitation” may be a more tangible demonstration of friendship than any grand narrative. After all, the Belt and Road is not only about port cranes and railway tracks. It is also about a small businessman in Colombo being able to fly to Guangzhou with reasonable ease—to sell a shipment of tea, or to buy a machine.
