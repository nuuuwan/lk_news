Accountability has been a buzz word for many lawmakers that Sri Lanka produced. It had been a promise that was rarely fulfilled on the political stage. This is why people rejected corrupt leaders and their associates. People rejected political nepotism, dynastic politics and demanded transparency and accountability.

Sri Lanka has surpassed turbulent periods in history. From insurrections to a prolonged armed conflict,  and unfortunate events such as the Easter Sunday attacks and the economic crisis, Sri Lankans have survived the best and worst of times. In each one of these incidents, there is the truth, the rumours, the fake narratives and a long list of questions and doubts that remain unanswered.

No party has claimed authorship for carrying out the 1971, 1987-1989 insurrections despite popular secrets. Claims of the Batalanda torture chambers were seen by some to be unfounded. Tamil communities in the North and East including the diaspora claim that the armed forces engaged in war crimes during the final stages of the conflict. But in his speech at the United Nations General Assembly in 2010, the then President Mahinda Rajapaksa defended his military campaign as a humanitarian operation. To date, many frontline army officials reject war crime allegations during the final stages of the war.

A similar accountability crisis is being observed when it comes to the Easter Sunday attacks. It is true that the main suspects have been served long jail terms with rigorous punishments. But that doesn’t serve justice to victims. There are many children and adults who have endured the trauma. Some parents still recall how they had to bury the remains of their children who weren’t terrorists but innocent churchgoers on an Easter Sunday.

Who is responsible for this crime and what was their purpose, they question.

But apart from such major incidents, journalists were being abducted, attacked and even murdered in a previous era.

Questions regarding accountability have once again come to the fore with former President Gotabaya Rajapaksa’s responses before the Colombo Magistrate’s Court on Tuesday. “I can’t remember”, was his response to many of the questions raised by lawyer Nuwan Bopege. This was his response when Bopege questioned him about the abduction and attack on journalist Keith Noyahr. He had said that he doesn’t remember Noyahr sustaining injuries following the attack.

Rajapaksa further said that he had not been involved in any operation on the ground,  and that his duty as Defence Secretary was to convey policies adopted by the Government to those institutions. When asked whether he was aware of abductions and disappearances that happened in the country,  he said that these were “matters presented with the intention of gaining political advantage and that there is no truth in them.” He said that these were said with the intention of damaging the reputation of the country.

People including victims have been longing for answers. But when former political leaders respond in such a manner, the quest for accountability becomes a series of dead-ends.

Without meaningful accountability, democratic governance decays into corruption, lack of public trust, and authoritarian rule. Political accountability acts as a core control on government power, ensuring that leaders remain responsive to society rather than acting without oversight. This applies to any administration.

But when accountability becomes a lost memory, it does more harm than good to a society and the principle of democracy.
