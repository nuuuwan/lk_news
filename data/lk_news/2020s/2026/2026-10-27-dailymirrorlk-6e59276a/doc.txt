Trump backs Netanyahu: Israel’s Likud party trails Eisenkot’s Yashar in  pre-poll surveys

Gadi Eisenkot is a firm Zionist but he opposes formal annexation of the West Bank

Elections to the Israeli parliament, the Knesset, to be held on  October 27, are expected to produce a photo finish between Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud party and Gadi Eisenkot’s Yashar party, a new outfit created only a year ago.

According to the opinion poll published by the daily Haaretz, the coalition led by GediEisenkot’s Yashar party would get 56 out of the 120 seats,  and the bloc led by Prime Minister Benjamin Natanyahu’s Likud party will get 50 and the others 14.

Individually, Yashar will get 23, Likud 21, Together 15, The Democrats 10, Joint List 9, Shas 8, Otzma Yahudit 8, Yisrael Beiteinu 8, Religious Zionism 6 and the rest the others.

Seats in the Israeli parliament are allocated on the basis of proportional representation. The whole country is one electoral district. Voters choose a party list, not an individual candidate.

In a desperate move, Netanyahu is predicting an Iranian nuclear blast in Israel which he calls the “second holocaust”. Very importantly, he is backed by US President Trump. “When you go to the polls please remember that Bibi [Netanyahu] has been an outstanding Warrior Prime Minister!” Trump wrote.

His current government, in power since 2022, is widely regarded as the most right-wing in Israeli history. But he now faces significant questions. His campaign against Hamas has not produced a decisive outcome, nor have his efforts directed at Iran. He faces an International Criminal Court arrest warrant.

Gadi Eisenkot’s Yashar (Upright) party was founded in 2025 and currently holds no seats, yet it has emerged as Netanyahu’s principal rival. A former chief of staff of the Israel Defence Forces (2015–2019), Eisenkot, 66, has served in the Knesset since 2022 but has limited ministerial experience. Of Moroccan origin (a Mizrahi Jew), he operates in a political culture long dominated by figures of European (Ashkenazi) background.

But Eisenkot is a firm Zionist. During Israel’s 2006 Lebanon war he articulated the “Dahiya Doctrine,” which called for the use of disproportionate force against civilian infrastructure linked to militant groups. However, he opposes formal annexation of the West Bank on the grounds that it would exceed Israel’s capacity to manage the territory.

Eisenkot has rejected reliance on Palestinian parties to form a government and, because of his insistence on compulsory military service, is unlikely to partner with ultra-Orthodox parties that seek to preserve exemptions for Haredi men.

Itamar Ben-Gvir’s Otzma Yehudit (“Jewish Power”) holds six seats in the Knesset. Once regarded as too extreme for mainstream politics, the party has pulled Netanyahu’s government further to the right since 2022 and is expected to gain seats, particularly among West Bank settlers. It supports annexation of the West Bank.

A Knesset member since 2021, Ben-Gvir became National Security minister in 2022 despite earlier criminal convictions for incitement to racism and support for a terrorist organisation. He has been sanctioned by the UK, France and other states over his rhetoric and policies regarding Gaza. He asserts Jewish sovereignty over the Al-Aqsa compound in Jerusalem.

Otzma Yehudit advocates “voluntary emigration” of Palestinians from Gaza; its rallies have featured chants like “Death to the Arabs.”

Bezalel Smotrich’s Religious Zionism Party holds seven seats and has grown through its partnership in the ruling coalition. Smotrich, 46, the son of an Orthodox rabbi, draws support from a more religious base than Ben-Gvir and favours the application of Jewish religious law (Halacha).

He has served as finance minister since 2022. He calls for the abrogation of the Oslo Accords, the dismantling of the Palestinian Authority, and Israeli settlement of both Gaza and the West Bank. Prosecutors at the International Criminal Court have sought his arrest on allegations related to the West Bank.

Former prime ministers Naftali Bennett (2021–22) and Yair Lapid (July–December 2022) announced a joint ticket in April, later named B’yachad (“Together”). Bennett, 54, is a long-time opponent of Palestinian statehood and a former leader of the Yesha Council representing West Bank settlers. He served as Netanyahu’s chief of staff from 2006 to 2008 before the two fell out.

Yair Lapid, a writer, founded Yesh Atid (“There is a Future”) in 2012. Though a secular centrist, he opposes Palestinian statehood and supports the settlement enterprise in principle while distancing himself from the more extreme positions of Ben-Gvir and Smotrich.

Yair Golan’s “The Democrats” hold four seats. Formed in 2024 from the Labour Party and the smaller Meretz, the party describes itself as a liberal-democratic Zionist force. Golan, 64, was among the few Israeli politicians to criticise the scale of Palestinian civilian casualties in Gaza, describing it at one point as a “national psychosis.”

A former deputy chief of staff (2014–2017), he re-enlisted after 7 October 2023 and is credited with helping to rescue Israelis during the Hamas attacks, earning him a reputation as a war hero among many Israelis. The Democrats oppose annexation of the West Bank, support a two-state solution, have fielded an Arab candidate, and are the only major party openly willing to consider coalition arrangements with Arab parties.

Sixty Eight year old Avigdor Lieberman’s Yisrael Beiteinu (Israel Our Home) holds six  seats and draws support primarily from Israel’s 1.5 million Russian-speaking citizens. The party has previously served in coalition governments and Lieberman has held senior posts including deputy prime minister.

It supports universal conscription, settlement expansion and tougher policies toward Iran, Hamas and Hezbollah. Lieberman has faced corruption investigations.

Shas (short for Sephardic Guardians) and United Torah Judaism, are ultra-Orthodox parties which draw strong support from Haredi (very orthodox) Jews, who constitute about 14 per cent of Israel’s population. Both prioritise religious authority in public life. Shas, with 11 seats, has participated in seven of the last ten Israeli governments and remains part of the current coalition.

In recent years it has moved further right, aligning with Ben-Gvir. Its Knesset leader is Aryeh Deri, 67, who has held multiple ministerial posts and served a prison sentence for bribery in the early 2000s.

United Torah Judaism (UTJ), with seven seats, represents Ashkenazi ultra-Orthodox streams. Ultimate authority in it rests with senior rabbis. Like Shas, its priorities centre on preserving ultra-Orthodox life.

Although many of its voters live in settlements, the party is officially non-Zionist, holding that a fully Jewish state should await the arrival of the Messiah. Some of its rabbis have criticised the Gaza war because of Israeli military casualties. The conscription issue has strained its relationship with Netanyahu.

Approximately two million Palestinian citizens of Israel possess the franchise. A high participation rate of around 70 per cent is projected this time given the gravity of current issues. Three of the main Arab-led parties—Hadash, Ta’al and Balad—are contesting together as the “Joint List” under Hadash leader Yousef Jabareen, 54.

The list holds now five seats. The parties, which are secular, demand greater civil rights for Palestinian citizens of Israel, criticise Israeli policy in Gaza and West Bank settlements, and support a two-state solution. Hadash includes communist and left-wing elements and fields both Arab and Jewish candidates. Ta’al is a moderate nationalist party. Balad has also proposed a Jewish candidate. But no Arab party has yet been invited into a governing coalition.

Mansour Abbas’s Ra’am (United Arab List) holds five seats. Led by Abbas, 52, it is the only Arab-led party to have served in government, during the short-lived Bennett–Lapid coalition of 2021–22. For the first time, Ra’am has fielded a Jewish candidate.

The outcome of the October 27 vote will hinge on the turnout, surplus-vote agreements and the ability of the major blocs to construct a workable majority of at least 61 seats. A day before polling, Eisenkot’s alliance is ahead with 54 seats, four seats ahead of the Netanyahu bloc.
