Ranil’s 2023 Law Exposed Political Wealth

Asset declaration began under the government of Prime Minister Sirimavo Bandaranaike and was expanded under Presidents J. R. Jayawardene and R. Premadasa

Minister Bimal Rathnayake explained in Parliament that the Government had decided to revise the Gazette notification issued last month, which had suspended the publication of public representatives’ asset declarations and triggered widespread debate. Under the revised approach, declarations of politicians and other elected public representatives will remain available for public scrutiny.

The publication of politicians’ asset declarations is, in reality, the latest chapter in a story that began more than half a century ago. A law that has steadily evolved from a little-known administrative requirement into a major instrument of public accountability.

Rathnayake described the Sections 86 and 88 of Anti-Corruption Act No. 09 of 2023, which requires senior officials and political leaders to disclose their assets and liabilities. Initially, around 300 persons, from the President downwards, were required to submit declarations in printed form. Subsequent amendments enabled the declarations to be submitted online.

But the significance of today’s controversy cannot be understood merely by looking at the latest Gazette or parliamentary statement. It has to be viewed against the long history of Sri Lanka’s asset declaration system.

The story began in 1975, when Parliament enacted the Declaration of Assets and Liabilities Law No. 1 of 1975. Introduced under the government of Prime Minister Sirimavo Bandaranaike, the legislation established the principle that persons occupying positions of public responsibility should periodically declare their assets and liabilities, whether held within Sri Lanka or abroad.

The purpose was clear. Those entrusted with public power should be subject to a mechanism through which unexplained wealth and financial irregularities could be investigated.  The penalty for failing to make a declaration, making a false declaration or failing to provide additional information when required remained remarkably low. A person found guilty could face a fine of not more than Rs. 1,000, or imprisonment for up to one year, or both. The first major expansion came with the Declaration of Assets and Liabilities Amendment Act No. 74 of 1988, under JRJ-Premadasa. The amendment widened the categories of persons required to declare their wealth. Judges, senior public officers, trade union executives, political office-bearers and election candidates were among those brought within the expanded framework.

The significance of the 1988 amendment lay in its recognition that corruption risks were not confined to a narrow group of public officials. Individuals occupying different positions of influence could also potentially exercise power over public resources or decisions, making financial disclosure an important safeguard.

Yet the basic character of the system remained largely unchanged. Declarations were principally documents submitted to authorities rather than information routinely examined by the public. The law required disclosure, but it did not create the level of public transparency that exists today.

The weaknesses became particularly obvious when the Rs. 1,000 penalty remained unchanged for decades. A penalty that may have had some deterrent value in 1975 had become almost symbolic with the passage of time. Proposals were therefore made to increase the fine substantially, with one proposal seeking to raise it to Rs. 100,000. An important attempt to modernise the law came in 2019. A proposed amendment sought to make annual declarations compulsory for the President, Speaker, Ministers, Deputy Ministers and Sri Lankan envoys. It also proposed bringing members of commissions, Provincial Councils and Local Authorities, as well as employees of Sri Lankan foreign missions, within the system. The proposed reform went further. Chairpersons, directors and employees of companies in which the State held more than 25 percent of the shares were also to come under the disclosure regime. The proposal also reinforced annual disclosure requirements relating to spouses and dependent children.

Although that particular bill did not secure sufficient parliamentary support, its underlying ideas were significant. It demonstrated the growing recognition that an effective asset declaration system required wider coverage, stronger penalties and greater accountability.

The major transformation, however, came with the Anti-Corruption Act No. 9 of 2023, under Ranil Wickremasinghe.

The 2023 legislation substantially strengthened Sri Lanka’s anti-corruption framework and expanded the importance of asset declarations. Sections 80, 86 and 88 established stronger disclosure obligations covering high-ranking public officials and political figures, extending from the President downwards.

The role of the Commission to Investigate Allegations of Bribery or Corruption, commonly known as CIABOC, was also strengthened within the modern disclosure framework.

What had once involved a relatively small number of senior office-holders submitting printed documents was, through subsequent reforms, transformed into a centralised electronic declaration system. The number of people required to submit declarations expanded dramatically, reaching approximately 150,000 to 160,000 public officials, professionals and other persons covered by the law. That transformation produced an equally important change: public access. Asset declarations were no longer merely documents stored within government offices. They became information that citizens could examine. For politicians, in particular, this created a new level of democratic accountability.

For years, some politicians had cultivated images of themselves as men and women of modest personal means, people who depended on supporters, party loyalists and well-wishers for their political existence. The introduction of public access to asset declarations changed that overnight. Politicians who had submitted their declarations without apparently anticipating the extent of subsequent public scrutiny were taken unaware when details of their declared assets became public knowledge. What had previously remained within official files was suddenly available for examination by journalists, civil society groups and ordinary citizens. The declarations therefore brought into the public domain financial information that could be compared with the modest lifestyles and public narratives that some politicians had projected for years.

For perhaps the first time, voters were allowed to compare a politician’s public image with an official financial declaration. The law provided a documentary basis for questions that previously depended largely on rumour, political claims or speculation. That is precisely why public access to politicians’ declarations matters.

A functioning asset declaration system can help identify conflicts of interest, unexplained increases in wealth and possible discrepancies requiring investigation. It can also allow voters to make more informed judgments about the people seeking their support. In that sense, the declarations represent more than financial forms. They are instruments of democratic accountability. When public access was extended through the centralised electronic system to a very large number of declarants, the information made available was not limited to politicians. Ordinary public officials and other non-political persons also became subject to disclosure requirements. Their declarations could contain sensitive information about family members, children, property and financial circumstances.

Sri Lanka’s asset declaration system has therefore travelled a long road, from the 1975 law, through the 1988 expansion, the reform attempts of 2019, and finally the comprehensive Anti-Corruption Act of 2023 and its subsequent digital transformation.

What began as a confidential statutory obligation has gradually become an instrument through which citizens can examine the financial affairs of their elected representatives. And after fifty years, Sri Lanka’s asset declaration law may finally be doing what such a law was intended to do from the beginning: making those who exercise public power answerable not only to the law, but also to the people.
