Govt. finally breaking the drug mafia

For decades, Sri Lanka sat at an agonisingly strategic crossroads in the Indian Ocean, acting as a vital maritime trade route and a fertile transit hub for international drug cartels.

The roots of this national crisis run remarkably deep into our history. During the long years of the LTTE conflict, northern coastal stretches like Velvettithurai, Point Pedro and Mannar were routinely exploited by contraband smuggling networks moving across the narrow waters of the Palk Strait. Over the decades that followed, those early illicit supply lines mutated into a highly organised, multi-million-dollar narcotics trade.

Major cartel figures operated with near-total impunity, directing street-level distribution in Colombo while lounging in luxury high-rises in Dubai, Bangkok, or Karachi. For years, ordinary Sri Lankans watched a frustrating pattern repeat itself. Local police would parade small street dealers for the news cameras, while the true architects running the syndicates stayed safe overseas.

That narrative is finally undergoing a sharp and visible shift. Under President Anura Kumara Dissanayake, the government has shown an unprecedented level of political will to dismantle organised crime and its global connections.

Where previous administrations treated international legal steps as slow, bureaucratic hurdles, the current leadership treats them as direct law enforcement operations. By working closely with Interpol, modernising mutual legal assistance treaties, and sharing real-time intelligence with foreign police forces, Sri Lankan authorities are achieving results once thought impossible. They are actively tracking down high-profile drug lords in foreign nations, arresting them, and extraditing them back to Sri Lankan soil.

Consider the recent string of high-profile extraditions arranged through Interpol Red Notices. For years, cities like Dubai served as untouchable safe houses for top cartel figures who ordered gang shootings, ran protection rackets, and directed massive heroin shipments into the island. The arrest and swift return of these key operatives sent a clear, unambiguous message to the underworld. Owning a foreign passport or a luxury apartment in the Gulf no longer protects anyone from Sri Lankan justice.

This firm strategy deserves recognition because it directly attacks the head of the snake rather than its tail. Arresting a small seller in Maligawatte or Grandpass might clean up a neighborhood street corner for a few days, but extraditing the financier who pays for the entire multi-ton shipment cuts off the supply chain at its true source.

While this momentum is commendable, the government must build a comprehensive institutional framework that lasts far beyond a single political term. Winning a permanent war on drugs requires a deeper, long-term policy blueprint.

First, law enforcement must focus heavily on financial intelligence and asset recovery. Extraditing kingpins is only the initial step. Stripping them of their luxury properties, bank accounts, shell companies, and vehicle fleets is the second, equally vital step. Money is the lifeblood of organised crime. Until money laundering inside local financial channels is completely eradicated, new criminal figures will always step forward to fill the void left by extradited bosses.

Second, our northern sea border requires permanent, modern surveillance infrastructure. The historical legacy of the northern smuggling corridor cannot be erased overnight. Providing the Sri Lanka Navy and Coast Guard with advanced radar technology, aerial drones, and joint patrol agreements with Indian maritime authorities is essential.

Third, domestic court procedures must match the efficiency of foreign arrests. Extradited suspects must face fast, fair, and legally airtight prosecution. Weak police files, intimidated witnesses, or endless delays in court will only undermine the hard work spent netting these criminals.

Finally, community awareness and public trust must be rebuilt across vulnerable neighborhoods. Local youth in low-income urban areas are often lured into becoming mules or dealers out of economic desperation. State intelligence agencies must engage deeply with community leaders, schools, and local organisations to spot early warning signs and protect young people from predatory gangs. At the same time, supply-side eradication must be paired with compassionate, accessible rehabilitation facilities for the thousands of young Sri Lankans jailed due to  drug addiction. President Dissanayake has proven that strong political leadership combined with decisive police work can overcome international borders. Maintaining this uncompromising standard will determine whether this moment is remembered as a short-lived crackdown or a permanent victory for the nation.
