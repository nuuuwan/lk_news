Crisis Monitoring Is Not a Back Office Function

Sri Lankan organisations still call their communications agencies in the middle of a crisis they could have seen coming. The tools have never been better. The gap is in how the monitoring work is valued, staffed and connected to the decisions it is meant to inform.

Trust is thinner, and the information environment more crowded, than most organisations assume. Four recent sources make the case.

The Edelman Trust Barometer 2026 Asia Pacific report finds that 67% of respondents in the region hold an insular trust mindset, and only 33% are open to trusting someone who is different from them. Across 28 markets, social media is the least trusted sector at 50%, and 65% worry that other countries deliberately contaminate their media with falsehoods, up 11 points since 2021. In Malaysia and Indonesia, the figure is 73% and 72%.

The World Economic Forum’s Global Risks Report 2026 ranks misinformation and disinformation second over the two year horizon, its highest position since 2023, and fourth over ten years. It warns that the problem “corrodes public discourse, weakens crisis responses and is propagated by technological advancements, such as in AI.”

At home, DataReportal’s Digital 2026 Sri Lanka counts 13.9 million internet users, 59.7% of the population, and 9.0 million social media user identities, 38.7% of the population. These are advertising reach estimates, not active users, but they show how far a conversation can travel before anyone at the top has read it.

Finally, the State of Social Intelligence 2026, a survey of 330 social intelligence professionals, finds that 80% are often or always involved in interpreting meaning from social and internet data. Yet 64% name tool limitations or integration problems as the main thing holding back their impact, and only 12% say strategic planning is their most frequent use of the work.  The first signal is usually small

Every crisis has a first signal, and it is rarely dramatic: A late night post with a few hundred shares, or a remark in a closed group nobody in the boardroom has heard. The people who catch these signals are seldom in the room when the decision is made.

Early warning does not come only from screens, and print still matters. A critical piece in a feature section weighs differently from the same words on the front page.

A story with modest volume, taken up by a credible tier one publication, can carry more risk than a viral post with no institutional weight. Reach, credibility and engagement have to be read together.

Experienced practitioners can often feel the change in the room before the numbers confirm it. Instinct is not a substitute for data. It tells you which data to examine first.

Not every signal warrants a response. Some low volume items are better tracked quietly, because a statement would turn a quiet item into a public one.  The hours that matter

Crises often break in the morning or late at night, and this is not a job with a clear off switch. A crisis desk that works only office hours is working on yesterday’s conversation.

When a client needs an answer within the hour, the person reading the data has to stay calm. That is a discipline, not a personality trait. Panic in the monitoring room reaches the client quickly.

The honest answer is usually the gist: where things stand, what is still being checked, and when a fuller picture will follow. Reassurance and time are legitimate tools. Making a call before the data is ready is not.

Information should reach the strategy team and the account lead before the client. Sending a raw item before anyone has assessed it is an error many practitioners learn from early.

A chief executive reading a crisis report at midnight needs three to five clear indicators, with sentiment at the centre and the positive and neutral lines showing whether a situation is recovering.

A good result often looks like a falling negative line, and not every situation needs a statement or a spokesperson.

The report is also a meeting. The monitoring lead has to sit with the strategy team and tell the story, not just display the data.  Where the work breaks down

Three problems recur, and none of them is really about software.

The first is information. Monitoring teams often learn about a client’s activity only when it appears in print, with no word on whether it could be contentious.

The second is interpretation. A dashboard can show a spike in mentions, but only someone who knows the outlet, its audience and history can say whether it matters.

The third is people. Monitoring desks absorb theworst of public conversation every day, including abuse, grief and threats, largely out of sight.  The cost on the people doing the work

The first rule is not to absorb everything. Short breaks, time away from screens and conversations that are not about work all help. When colleagues say it is too much, the workload is the problem, not the person.

Many clients call for monitoring only in a crisis, but good times need tracking,  too. A strong position makes a brand the first target when a regulation or labelling change arrives, so a market leader should worry more, not less.

Proactive monitoring is far cheaper than a crisis that arrives unseen, and a playbook written in advance saves time when it matters most.  What machines cannot read

Dashboards promise instant summaries, but summaries are not the job. A tool can produce a headline. It cannot always tell whether a mention is relevant, or whether a story matters to a particular audience.

The analyst draws the picture for the client, with judgement built over years of reading. That remains the step that matters most.  What agencies and clients should do differently

Four changes would make a measurable difference, and none requires new software. One, Brief monitoring before the announcement, not after it, with the context, the likely reaction and the people who could be affected.

Two, write the crisis playbook before the crisis, naming who decides, who speaks, who monitors and what triggers escalation Three,  Protect the first hour. Let the monitoring lead give an incomplete picture honestly. Four, Staff for the work and recognise it, in performance reviews as well as the fee line.

Clients have a part to play too. Agencies that do this work well should be brought in early, not called after the damage is done.  Advice for the next practitioner

Read the data before you respond, especially when you are new, and understand the platforms and publications, not just the headlines. Ask your seniors, or whoever handles the client, when you are unsure. Early mistakes are common and recoverable.

When something goes wrong, take a breather. The world is not falling apart.

(Anjalie Munasinghe is Manager, PR and Social Intelligence at Mark and Comm, a boutique strategic communications agency in Colombo. She is Secretary of CIPR International, the international chapter of the Chartered Institute of Public Relations, and a Member of CIPR).
