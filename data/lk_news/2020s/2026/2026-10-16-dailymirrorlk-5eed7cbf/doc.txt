Another IMF Programme: What Is Missing in Sri Lanka’s Growth Story?

There is an uncomfortable question Sri Lanka must confront as the Government considers its economic future beyond the current International Monetary Fund (IMF) programme. If the country has restored macroeconomic stability after its worst economic crisis in decades, why does it still need to look to another programme to sustain its recovery?

The question is not whether Sri Lanka should continue working with the IMF. Given its debt obligations, limited room for fiscal manoeuvre and exposure to external shocks, continued engagement with the Fund is prudent. Nor can the Government be expected to dismantle overnight  economic problems accumulated over decades. The issue is whether the country has moved sufficiently far from crisis management to establish an economic direction of its own.

Sri Lanka’s economic recovery has reached an important juncture. The immediate dangers of the 2022 collapse have receded. The country has restructured much of its debt, improved revenue mobilisation and rebuilt its foreign reserves. These achievements should not be discounted. Yet, they represent the restoration of basic economic functioning rather than proof that the foundations of a new growth model have been established.

A successor programme  should compel the Government to explain what it intends to do differently in the next phase of the recovery.

The danger is that Sri Lanka may become proficient at satisfying the requirements of successive stabilisation programmes without addressing the deeper weaknesses that repeatedly bring it back to the negotiating table. Fiscal discipline is essential, but it cannot be the country’s entire economic philosophy. Raising revenue, containing expenditure and maintaining monetary discipline can help prevent another collapse. They do not automatically generate new industries, expand exports or create sufficiently remunerative employment.

The present Government inherited an economy in distress, but it also inherited an opportunity. Its electoral mandate was built partly on the promise of a different political and economic culture. That promise now needs to be translated into a development strategy that goes beyond the preservation of the existing IMF framework.

Where, for instance, is the decisive shift towards an export-oriented economy? What tangible progress is being made in attracting investment into manufacturing, technology, logistics and higher-value services? How will Sri Lanka reduce the structural costs that make local production uncompetitive?

These are not questions that can be answered by pointing to an improved primary balance or a successful IMF review. They require measurable outcomes in investment, productivity, exports and real incomes. Without such progress, the recovery risks remaining disproportionately visible in macroeconomic indicators rather than in the economic circumstances of ordinary people. Nevertheless, the Government’s performance must be judged in the context of events beyond its control. The escalating Middle East crisis is an important consideration. For an economy heavily dependent on imported fuel, an extended conflict can quickly translate into higher import costs and pressure on foreign exchange while weaker demand or instability in the region could affect tourism and remittances.

Sri Lanka cannot insulate itself completely from such developments. Nor would it be reasonable to expect the Government to pursue its economic agenda as though international energy markets and geopolitical conditions were stable. The fiscal pressures arising from reconstruction after Cyclone Ditwah add another complication. Maintaining stability under these circumstances is itself a considerable undertaking.

Yet, the Middle East crisis also shows  why the country’s economic strategy cannot remain confined to managing immediate pressures. External shocks expose the vulnerabilities of an economy that has insufficient buffers, limited export diversification and a heavy dependence on imported energy. The response must go beyond temporary adjustments to fuel prices or expenditure. It must involve a sustained effort to strengthen energy security, diversify export markets, broaden the production base and build resilience into public finances.

A successor programme should not, therefore, be regarded as either a triumph or a failure in itself. Its significance will depend on what Sri Lanka expects to achieve through it. Continued IMF support may be necessary, but necessity should not become a substitute for ambition. Sri Lanka’s next economic chapter must be about more than avoiding another crisis. It must be about creating an economy capable of growing without repeatedly depending on emergency measures to keep it afloat.
