Lisan ud-Dawat:  The Language of the  Bohras  and how they  keep it alive

To any untrained ear, the language does sound like an Indian language. However, their conversation does not have the same musical melody of Hindi or Urdu, nor does it have the rhythm of Sri Lankan Muslim Tamil, and it does not have the pauses of Arabic either. Instead, Lisan ud-Dawat possesses a very distinctive acoustic vitality

Everyone in Colombo recognizes the Dawoodi Bohras. Standing out in their distinctive attire, they are a familiar sight whether in the bustling bazaar of Pettah or drinking coffee in a hotel lobby. And if you happen to catch a fragment of their conversation, your ears will encounter a fascinating linguistic phenomenon: they speak a unique language - “Lisan ud-Dawat” (literally meaning “language of the community”).

To any untrained ear, it does sound like an Indian language. However, their conversation does not have the same musical melody of Hindi or Urdu, nor does it have the rhythm of Sri Lankan Muslim Tamil, and it does not have the pauses of Arabic either. Instead, Lisan ud-Dawat possesses a very distinctive acoustic vitality.

Rashida Tajbhoy, a Mumbai-native member of the community now living in Sri Lanka, notes that while most linguistic sources debate whether it is an entirely different language or a dialect of Gujarati from northern India, it remains mutually intelligible. She explains that most standard Gujarati speakers can easily understand their dialogue, and vice versa.

However, Lisan ud-Dawat possesses distinct elements influenced by Persian (Farsi), Urdu, and Arabic.

Despite these influences, Lisan ud-Dawat has retained its distinct identity, largely because it continues to be spoken within Bohra households and passed down through generations.

“Families keep it alive because parents speak it at home with their kids,” says Mufaddal Zainudeen Adamjee, who leads community outreach. “All of our relatives speak it - it’s just a normal part of everyday life for us - passed down from generations.”

The language holds several “linguistic fossils”—ancient features that faded from modern languages but remain used by Bohras. This includes the Persian ezafe used to link words (such as “Tarz-e-Jadeed”, meaning novel approach) or suffixing Gujarati plural markers directly onto classical Arabic roots (such as adding the plural suffix “-o” to form “kitabo” for books). Another word that Bohras frequently use is “ohbat” meaning preparation, which is not used in Gujarati.

A passerby in the marketplace might be familiar hearing Bohras greeting each other with “Aap kem cho? Khuda nu shukr che.” (How are you? Doing well by the grace of God), showcasing loanwords like “Khuda” or God (Persian) and “shukr” or thankful (Arabic).

Words of Arabic origin, such as “dawā” (medicine), have become so embedded in Gujarati speech that they are used naturally in expressions such as “dawā ni dukān” (medicine shop). These words reflect the different cultures and languages that have shaped Lisan ud-Dawat over centuries.

The community’s deep historical roots link back to Arabic spoken in Egypt and Yemen, while Lisan ud-Dawat developed when Fatimid emissaries from Cairo first went to Gujarat, India around a thousand years ago.

Today, this global community spans nearly one million people across the world, most being in India with over 2,000 members residing in Sri Lanka.

When the global leader of the community, Syedna Mufaddal Saifuddin, visits Colombo, he is often seen on the local news. While onlookers frequently assume he is speaking a larger Middle Eastern or Indian language, he is actually speaking formal Lisan ud-Dawat.

The community’s religious congregations, known as Ashara Mubaraka, rotate globally. Colombo hosted the gathering in 2019, drawing more than 21,000 global visitors. A few years later, in 2024 and 2025, Colombo served as an international satellite convention hub. During this time, over 15,000 visitors came to Colombo’s convention centers and the Husaini Masjid in Bambalapitiya to participate via live broadcast. Among the crowds in Colombo, one could hear Lisan ud-Dawat spoken in distinct Indian, Pakistani, East African, and even British or American accents— a nuance that’s more noticeable when they converse with locals in English.

A family gathering might feature everyday terms unique to them, like “thaal”, the large, circular metal platter around which a group of eight sit to share a meal. Another word is “waaz” – the religious discourses delivered by clergy.

While religious sermons are delivered using formal terms, everyday speech incorporates the local vernacular. Tajbhoy notes that the local Bohras use Lankan words extensively, “I’ve heard all the specific Sri Lankan colloquial terms within the Colombo community when they chit-chat, like naturally dropping an “aiyo” or an “aney” when they are surprised or expressing sympathy”. Naturally, modern vocabulary like words for “computer” or “internet” are borrowed directly from English. “We are surrounded by so many other communities, and this naturally affects how we speak,” she adds.

Switching languages seems to be second nature for most Bohras. Many attend English-medium international schools and are heavily engaged in business or other professional sectors, so most in the island speak fluent English and Sinhala and some are familiar with Tamil as well. So, whether a Bohra is studying engineering in English at Oxford, striking a business deal in Nairobi using Swahili, or trading in Pettah using Sinhala, switching to their native language is second nature. Tajbhoy attributes this linguistic resilience to a “deep sense of cultural pride.”

Ultimately, Lisan ud-Dawat remains a living blueprint of the Dawoodi Bohra’s historic voyage. Every phrase spoken in the markets of Pettah or within the Husaini Masjid Complex carries an echo of the past—from the trading ports of Gujarat to the shores of the Arab world. By keeping this unique tongue alive, the community also cements an enduring heritage directly into the international fabric of modern Colombo.
