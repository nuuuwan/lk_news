Planters seek 46-year lease extension to unlock tea replanting

Regional plantation companies have 19 years left on leases, while replanting can take more than 20 years to pay back

The companies manage about a quarter of Sri Lanka’s tea acreage

Industry body says longer leases would help estates secure bank financing and diversify

Sri Lanka’s plantation companies are seeking a 46-year extension to their land leases, warning that the time left on them is already shorter than it can take to recover the cost of replanting tea.

The leases, originally granted for 99 years in 1992, were subsequently cut to 53 years and have 19 years left, Planters’ Association of Ceylon Chairman Shanaka Samaradiwakara said in a guest column. The Association wants the government to restore the 46 years removed from the original term.

“A tea bush planted today may take five years to yield a crop, a decade to deliver meaningful returns and more than 20 years to repay the investment,” Samaradiwakara said.

He said the shrinking lease term made replanting harder to justify and constrained access to bank financing for projects whose payback period extended beyond the lease. Regional plantation companies manage about a quarter of Sri Lanka’s tea acreage, he said. “Government engagement is welcome, but a timely decision would send a far stronger signal than continued discussion,” he said.

The request comes amid falling output and rising costs. Tea production fell 3 percent to 152.5 million kilogrammes in the first seven months of 2026, while tea export earnings declined to US$701 million from US$ 743 million, according to figures cited in the column. Sheet rubber production fell nearly 18 percent.

Samaradiwakara put labour at about 65 percent of production costs and estimated that a Rs. 200 wage increase had added Rs. 6 billion to the industry’s costs. He said fertiliser prices had risen to around Rs. 220,000 per tonne from about Rs. 100,000, alongside higher freight and energy costs.

Longer leases would also give estates more scope to invest in alternative crops, he said. In parts of Uva, where annual rainfall has fallen below 500 millimetres, estates are exploring coffee, avocado and cinnamon as tea becomes harder to grow. At Malwatte, avocado planting density has risen from 150 to 800 plants per hectare since 2015, he said.
