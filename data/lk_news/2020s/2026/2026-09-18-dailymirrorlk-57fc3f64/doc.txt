The 63rd session of the United Nations Human Rights Council (UNHRC) is currently underway in Geneva. The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk released a comprehensive report on Sri Lanka, urging robust accountability, institutional reforms and a moratorium on the Prevention of Terrorism Act.As an experienced politician who served various portfolios as a foreign minister and minister of external affairs and having represented Sri Lanka on various international platforms, Prof. G. L. Peiris spoke to the Daily Mirror regarding his observations on the High Commissioner’s report. He raised concerns regarding the way the incumbent government is handling the country’s foreign policy while highlighting repercussions of the inability to deliver its promises made to international organisations such as the United Nations. Excerpts:

Q What are your thoughts regarding United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk’s report on Sri Lanka?

The report on Sri Lanka contains some very stringent, serious and far-reaching criticisms. It is utterly irresponsible action on the part of the government of Sri Lanka where a whole string of broken promises have been given to the UN. We (Sri Lanka) have demonstrated our total inability to abide by the assurances that we have solemnly given the UN. The government’s response has been that they shouldn’t exert pressure on our country. The question that arises is who is responsible and where does the problem lie. Should we simply go there and complain that they are exerting too much pressure on us and have a defensive posture? Is that the best policy for us to adopt in this situation. Why are we placed in that vulnerable situation? The report makes references to the Prevention to Terrorism Act (PTA), Chemmani mass graves, establishment of a Truth and Reconciliation Commission, arbitrary arrests and custodial deaths among others.

Q The UN has consistently pressured Sri Lanka to repeal the PTA. The incumbent government has instead proposed the Protection of State against Terrorism Act. What are your thoughts regarding this new Act?

The current government of Sri Lanka has given repeated assurances that this act would be repealed. And that it would be replaced by legislation in conformity with international law. Have they fulfilled that promise? Absolutely not. This is an Act that is 47 years old. It was enacted in 1979. At that time it was entitled as the Prevention of Terrorism (Temporary Provisions) Act. The party now in power acknowledged unequivocally that it is a horrendous piece of legislation; that it is totally inimical to human rights even at the basic level. But in December 2025 there was new legislation posted on the justice ministry website to replace the 1979 act. There was criticism from all quarters and it was conclusively shown that the new proposal was worse than the previous act. I published a detailed analysis of the new legislation and I was able to show that in each area that mattered, this was retrogressive rather than progressive. Everybody from human rights organisations to civil society, university academics opposed it. Then the justice ministry said they are withdrawing the draft and they are not proceeding with it. They said they will present a fresh draft. The next development was that in August 2026 the government announced that the cabinet had approved a new draft. But nobody has seen it. We don’t know what that draft contains. There had been no discussion, no opportunity that had been available for stakeholders to present their views, so that there can be a free and open discussion about the content of the proposed law. Consequently there is every likelihood that the new law would suffer the fate of the old law. The basic flaw is the total lack of openness and transparency. People who have been held in custody under the 1979 legislation should be released. That’s an area in which the government has signally failed. The Online Safety legislation is another draconian piece of legislation which is totally incompatible with human rights even at the basic level. The present government when in the Opposition promised to repeal it but nothing has been done about it either.

Q The report also mentions excavations at the Chemmani mass grave and states that it is a critical test for justice. Your thoughts?

They invited Volker Türk to the country, took him to Jaffna, showed him how the excavations are taking place. There were 583 sets of skeletons that were discovered. All this was done to establish a rapport with the High Commissioner for Human Rights. so that he would understand the situation and the government briefed him fully. Then a proposal was brought before the UN General Assembly in New York to extend the term of office of the high commissioner for another three years. 144 countries supported the high commissioner, 10 countries opposed and 13 countries abstained. Sri Lanka was one among the 13 countries that abstained. Where is the consistency in our foreign policy? We invite him, show him Chemmani, establish a rapport with him and then we don’t support him when his term is being extended. Do we know what we are doing? Is there any compatibility of our actions from day to day? There seems to be some doubt as to whether there was a deliberate decision by the foreign affairs minister to withhold support from Volker Türk or whether there was some failure in communication. Is it that we just didn’t focus on it and take a decision? But now, look at the consequences.

Q The High Commissioner’s report urged Sri Lanka to establish an independent prosecutor’s office separate from the Attorney General’s Department. What do you have to say about this demand?

The government repeatedly said that the Attorney General should not be entrusted with the task of deciding who should be prosecuted. The argument was that the AG was a principled law officer of the state. If action is brought against a President or a minister it is the attorney general who represents the President in courts. Because of his character as the principled law officer of the state, it is anomalous to entrust him with the task of deciding whether there is sufficient evidence to bring prosecutions against leading opposition personalities and others. There is conflict of interest. So the proposal was to introduce the Office of Director of Public Prosecution. This is not entirely new to Sri Lanka and the proposal was to re-establish that office as a solution to the conflict of duties within the AG’s office. This solemn promise was made. The UNHRC has included it in their current report but we have done absolutely nothing about it.

Q Successive governments have held numerous discussions regarding the Truth and Reconciliation Commission especially during election periods. This government also promised to establish this Commission. What are your comments?

There have been repeated references to a Truth and Reconciliation Commission. Time and again we have referred to that. Government ministers visit the Northern Province regularly, President has visited several times and in each of these occasions, senior government functionaries have repeatedly promised the establishment of a Truth and Reconciliation Commission. They have referred to the South African experience and so on. But up to now nothing whatsoever has been done. Nobody has seen any draft of legislation to establish a Truth and Reconciliation Commission. This is why *Sri Lanka is encountering such heavy weather in Geneva at the UNHRC. We have only ourselves to blame. There is nothing to be gained by blaming the human rights council and saying that they are exerting too much pressure. Why is the pressure coming? Because we have utterly irresponsibly given solemn promises about fundamental matters and then gone back on each of those promises.

There are stringent criticisms relating to the Office on Missing Persons. The report says that there should be significant improvement of the independent capacity of the OMP. That they should have adequate investigative powers in order to fulfill their mandate credibly. A significant conclusion set out in the report is that the trust in domestic accountability mechanisms remains low.

Q We have had several domestic processes to ensure justice for communities affected by the war. But many of them have been stalled or lacks tangible progress. What do you have to say?

It is an age-old problem and it’s not something that can be fixed overnight. There’s no need to reinvent the wheel. There’s so much material that is available. There have been so many commissions, committees and so on which have done valuable work. What is required is a fresh evaluation of the abundant material that is already in existence. Not to add one more leaf to the thicket, but to make a comprehensive appraisal of the already existing material. That’s what the government promised to do. But nothing has happened.

QThe government is determined to pass the controversial 22nd Amendment. But you have been quite critical about this move.

Based on UN’s assessment of the OMP what is all the more important is the state of the judiciary. It is the judiciary that should command acceptance without any shadow of doubt. That is where the importance of the 22nd Amendment comes in. The 22A was vigorously opposed by everybody other than the present government including Buddhist clergy, Catholic Bishops, Anglican priests, Bar Association and foreign professional organizations such as Law Asia, trade unions and university academics. But government was very cavalier in their treatment of the opposition that they said they have decided no matter what opposition we encounter we are going to do this. In the past, Sri Lanka resisted interference from overseas by using the argument that there is no justification for foreign intervention because we have credible local institutions, in particular our judiciary. Our judiciary has commanded universal acknowledgment and respect as an independent institution and for the first time that is being doubted. There are questions regarding the independence of the judiciary after the enactment of the 22A. This is going to cause enormous damage to the country. We will no longer be able to use that powerful shield. It was a shield we were using to protect our country.

Yes. We very badly need investments in our country. Thanks to what Ranil Wickremesinghe did when he was President. The negotiations which he carried out with the IMF there is some degree of stability with regards to the economy. But there’s no growth because there are no investments. Serious investors including foreign banks, foreign companies would consider the independence of the judiciary among other factors; about how credible and reliable the judiciary is. Because when disputes arise they want to take those disputes to an impartial body. If there are doubts with regards to the integrity and objectivity of the judiciary there could be a strong disincentive to investments. The 22A among other deleterious consequences will discourage serious foreign investors from coming into the country.

Q The High Commissioner’s report also refers to arbitrary arrests, torture, detentions and custodial deaths which portray a negative picture about the country’s human rights situation. What are your comments?

The report is very damaging. It says that in the year 2025, 1125 cases of arbitrary arrests and detentions have been recorded. In the first quarter of this year there have been 351 cases of arbitrary arrests and detentions. The report also says that in 2025 there were 602 reported cases of torture and ill-treatment. In 2026 up to April there have been 138 cases. This is a record that doesn’t reflect credit on Sri Lanka. It is incorporated in the substance of the report which the high commissioner submitted to the UNHRC. Then there’s a reference to something that is very disturbing and that is deaths in custody. In 2025 there had been 18 such deaths and up to April, 3 people have died in custody. This is a serious indictment on the Sri Lankan state because when a person is taken into custody the state is responsible for the safety, well-being and life of that person. The report makes specific references to death threats in the North and East, false criminal charges and home visits to intimidate people, particularly women who are human rights activists. These are some grave allegations contained in the report of the high commissioner.

Q There was no Sri Lankan delegation at the recently concluded BRICS Summit held in India. Isn’t it an opportunity that we missed?

BRICS is an institution which would have been of enormous value to Sri Lanka. The main theme in the recently concluded BRICS sessions was ‘resistance of unilateralism.’ During their meeting they passed a resolution to say that unilateral tariffs constitute a violation of World Trade Organisation (WTO) rules. They said that BRICS must collectively act against it. Then they referred to emerging markets in the global south and developing countries and it was very much the focus of attention. There was a very powerful participation of countries such as China, India, Russia, South Africa, Brazil and Iran. BRICS is going to establish a New Development Bank to provide resources for emerging countries. This would be on parallel with the IMF, the World Bank, Asian Development Bank and so on. We desperately need those resources. We cannot rely on one source. We need a multiplicity of resources. But Sri Lanka was not represented at all. We didn’t send anybody there. This is inexcusable and we are letting these valuable opportunities slip through our fingers.

Q What are your thoughts regarding the way the country’s foreign policy is being handled by this government?

We are very sorry about this situation regarding the conduct of the foreign policy of the country which entails incalculable damage to the well-being of our nation. The government has absenteed itself from all meetings and deliberations which would have done enormous good to Sri Lanka. BRICS is not the only one. Sri Lanka’s foreign minister didn’t attend the Indian Ocean conference in Mauritius, the Hong Kong Corporation Organisation meeting this year, Colombo security enclave is a valuable opportunity for us, but there’s no progress. Sri Lanka must make use of its natural advantages. Among which is our geographical location. The ocean is very important to us. We are a member of the Indian Ocean Rim Association (IORA). We have been participating in the Bali meeting. Sri Lanka had its own voice in those matters but everything is going by neglect. We are simply not making use of the opportunities our geographical location has given us. Indian Ocean is no longer important as a trade route. What about cyber security, supply chains, energy security – in all these areas the Indian Ocean has become very important. Although those potential advantages are within our reach we have consistently and inexcusably neglected them. How can we handle the country’s foreign policy in this manner? Shouldn’t we take it seriously? It is in fact damaging for the people of Sri Lanka. Some of the issues arising from a lack of foreign policy were dealt with great lucidity by former ambassador Kana Kananathan which was recently published in the Daily Mirror.

Q The incumbent government is carrying out investigations into financial crimes committed during previous regimes and some of them involve high profile figures. But the opposition has been urging the release of some of these individuals currently detained or behind bars. What are your comments?

We have absolutely no compliant against the punishment of people who are guilty. But it must be done according to due process. However, that is not the only thing that a government needs to do. Throw people into jail if they have done wrong. But it won’t make lives of people better, it will not result in better facilities in hospitals, in lifesaving drugs being made available. It will not result in the improvement of facilities in our schools. What about the cost of living? The World Food Programme has said that 6 million people in our country are suffering from malnutrition and that they are below the poverty line. That is almost one quarter of the population. Government has to tackle those problems. Then what about farmers’ issues in Anuradhapura, Polonnaruwa and so on. There’s talk of an increase in the price of fuel. Electricity prices are going up. Locking people up every day and having a media blitzkrieg about the number of people being taken into custody is alright if it’s being done according to due process.

Q Sri Lanka’s peace process failed as mentioned in your book. Today we see many narratives by LTTE sympathisers to gain sympathy from the international community. How do you see these attempts?

That is to be expected. The war ended in 2009 but the diaspora is active in all these countries. Many of these countries have extended their bans on LTTE. The diaspora is doing whatever they are naturally expected to do. But it is not proper for a government to be controlled or for their decisions to be dictated by the diaspora. Diaspora has abundant funds and with that comes influence. But the government must act in the interests of its own people. The diaspora shouldn’t be the determining or defining factor.

Q How optimistic are you that the Opposition has a chance to field a winning candidate in the next election?

It’s much too early to talk of candidates and trying to identify candidates. That is not what we ought to do at this time. What is required is the construction of a broad platform where parties and groups with entirely different identities can agree to work together on a common programme while retaining their separate identities. If the opposition remains fractured and fragmented that would be an enormous strength to the government. We had a meeting tomorrow and all opposition groups agreed to attend it. The purpose is to withstand government initiatives which are detrimental to the very continuance of democracy in our country. Then notwithstanding our divergence of approach with regards to various matters to decide on. It can be called the Minimum Common Programme- identifying objectives of truly national significance and then use the collective strength to work towards identifying goals.
