Forty Years After the Kantale Tank Disaster, Sri Lanka Still Awaits a Dam Safety Regulator

April 20 this year marked the 40th anniversary of one of the darkest and least discussed engineering disasters in Sri Lanka’s history – the failure of the Kantale Tank bund in 1986.

More than 129 people lost their lives when a section of the dam breached in the early hours of the morning, unleashing a wall of water on unsuspecting communities downstream. To this day, the exact number of deaths remains uncertain. Many victims were never formally accounted for, and countless families who lost homes, crops, livestock and livelihoods were left to rebuild their lives with little recognition and inadequate compensation. The rehabilitation cost was estimated to cost in excess of Rs 600 million in 1988 terms.

The tragedy occurred around 3.30am, when most residents were asleep. The darkness and speed of the flood left virtually no time for warning or evacuation. Survivors have recounted harrowing stories of clinging to trees to escape the torrent while elderly relatives and young children were swept away. Entire villages were devastated. Families who had spent generations cultivating the fertile lands nourished by the tank found themselves destitute overnight.

Forty years later, the disaster has largely faded from public memory. Yet the questions surrounding the failure remain as relevant today as they were in 1986.

A Presidential Commission of Inquiry was conducted following the disaster. However, the findings have never been publicly discussed in any meaningful way and the report itself appears to have disappeared from public view. Over the years, speculation has persisted that the findings would expose serious failures in governance and decision-making.

One of the most troubling developments relates to the construction of a pump house on the crest of the dam wall two years before the failure. According to accounts from engineers and others familiar with the incident, the structure was built to supply water to a senior official in government. The pump house was constructed using a pile foundation with steel piles driven through the dam wall.

Such works would be considered highly questionable under accepted dam engineering principles because any penetration through a dam embankment can create pathways for seepage and internal erosion. This decision represented not merely poor judgement but a profound breach of basic dam safety practice. Unsurprisingly, a sinkhole had appeared in the dam embankment after the pump house was constructed.

Reports at the time also indicated that unusual seepage of water had been observed from the embankment months before the breach. Local residents reportedly raised concerns, and there were even newspaper reports drawing attention to the problem. In dam engineering, unexplained seepage is never a matter to be ignored. It is often one of the earliest indicators of internal erosion and potential instability.

The inquiry found that pile driving was very likely a significant contributor to the dam’s failure.

Whether every detail of the structure’s failure can still be proven today is perhaps less important than the broader lesson. The Kantale disaster was not simply a natural event. It was a preventable tragedy that appears to have involved irresponsible construction on a dam wall, ignored warning signs, inadequate oversight and failures in accountability.

What makes this even more concerning is that Sri Lanka still lacks a comprehensive dam safety regulatory framework.

This is remarkable for a country whose civilisation was built around water. Sri Lanka possesses one of the world’s oldest and most sophisticated irrigation traditions. Ironically, the very reservoir that failed in 1986 was itself an engineering legacy of ancient Sri Lanka. Kantale Tank, or Gangathala Wapi, was built during the reign of King Aggabodhi II in the seventh century A.D. and later improved by King Parakramabahu the Great. For more than 1,400 years, it sustained agriculture and livelihoods in the Eastern Province, standing as a testament to the engineering skill of ancient Sri Lanka.

For more than 2,000 years, Sri Lankan kings and engineers constructed reservoirs and canals that transformed dry landscapes into productive agricultural regions. Thousands of tanks and dams remain in operation today, a testament to the ingenuity and skill of our ancestors.

Yet while we celebrate this remarkable hydraulic heritage, we have failed to establish a modern regulatory system to ensure these structures remain safe.

Apart from the ancient dams, Sri Lanka’s other dams constructed in more recent periods are now many decades old. They require continuous monitoring, periodic safety reviews, risk assessments, emergency action plans and independent oversight. At present, dam owners are often responsible for regulating themselves. This creates an obvious gap in accountability.

The need for stronger oversight is not confined to the risk of dam failure alone. In November 2025, Cyclone Ditwah highlighted the importance of emergency preparedness and public warning systems associated with major dams. Communities living downstream of Kotmale Reservoir reportedly received little or no warning before large floods from rainfall and water releases from the reservoir inundated downstream areas. This resulted in loss of many lives and destruction of homes.

While extreme weather events cannot be prevented, their consequences can often be substantially reduced through well-developed and regularly tested emergency action plans, clear warning protocols and effective coordination between dam owners, emergency services and local communities. These are standard requirements in mature dam safety regimes around the world. A national dam safety regulator would help ensure such arrangements are in place, reducing the likelihood that vulnerable communities are left exposed when emergencies occur.

Other countries have recognised this challenge and acted decisively. India, which manages more than 5,000 large dams, enacted the Dam Safety Act in 2021, establishing national and state-level institutions responsible for surveillance, inspection, maintenance and safety oversight. The legislation created formal mechanisms to prevent dam failure disasters and improve public safety.

There have been reports that a Cabinet paper proposing the establishment of a national dam safety regulator was approved several years ago. Yet little appears to have happened since. If true, this represents a missed opportunity to address a critical national risk.

The argument that a regulator would be too costly is unconvincing. Sri Lanka already possesses significant expertise within the Irrigation Department, Mahaweli Authority, Ceylon Electricity Board, National Building Research Organisation, universities and the private engineering sector. A regulator could be established through a new governance framework that draws upon existing expertise while providing independent oversight and accountability. The cost would be negligible compared with the economic and human consequences of a major dam failure.

Scientific projections indicate that Sri Lanka is likely to experience more intense rainfall events and longer-duration storms in the coming decades. Infrastructure designed for historical rainfall patterns may increasingly face conditions beyond those originally anticipated. Extreme floods place enormous stress on dam spillways, embankments and outlet structures. Without proper risk assessments and adaptation measures, the likelihood of failures and near-failures will inevitably increase.

The responsibility of government extends beyond building infrastructure. It includes ensuring that infrastructure remains safe throughout its life. Citizens have a right to expect that known risks will be identified, monitored and managed before disaster strikes.

More than 3,700 years ago, King Hammurabi of Babylon recognised this principle when he enacted laws governing the construction and maintenance of irrigation works. His code stated that if a man neglected to maintain a dam and its failure caused flooding and destruction, he would be held personally responsible for the damage. This principle remains relevant today: those entrusted with public infrastructure have a duty of care to protect the lives and livelihoods of the people who depend upon it.

Forty years after the Kantale disaster, Sri Lanka owes a debt to the victims and their families. The greatest tribute we can pay them is not a memorial plaque or an anniversary speech. It is ensuring that such a tragedy never happens again.

The establishment of an independent national dam safety regulator would be a significant step in that direction. It would demonstrate that the lessons of Kantale have finally been learned and that public safety is being treated with the seriousness it deserves.

The author is a water policy and dam safety specialist who established and led Victoria’s (Australia) dam safety regulatory program.
