Customs needs US$ 55mn scanner upgrade to end container delays:  industry insider

Kerawalapitiya yard yet to take off five years after Cabinet nod

Insider alleges “worldwide mafia” and “speed money” behind resistance to technology

Sri Lanka Customs needs at least eight to ten container scanners, at least double the four now in operation, and an estimated US$ 55 million investment in a technology-led inspection facility to end the chronic clearance delays that inflate import costs, a senior logistics industry insider with direct knowledge of port operations said.

The comments follow the detection of 471 kgs of crystal methamphetamine, or ‘Ice’, valued at over Rs.8 billion, in a container at the Port of Colombo on August 31, which came to light through an overseas informant and a court order obtained by the police rather than through routine inspection.

Most detections still depend on such tip-offs, the insider told Mirror Business on condition of anonymity, as an initial scan gives only a rough indication of what a container holds, and a small consignment hidden among tens of tonnes of tightly packed cargo is nearly impossible to find by hand.

“We need at least eight to ten scanners to clear this cargo,” the insider stressed, noting that one of the four scanning bays is set aside for exports, leaving three for imports, less than a third of the ten required daily, a gap that widens in November and December when import volumes peak.

The US$ 55 million estimate covers scanners and buildings, and the facility could be operational within three years of a tender being awarded, as the staff is already in place and the systems are software-driven, according to the insider.

A feasibility study backed by the Asian Development Bank (ADB) proposed a new inspection centre at Kerawalapitiya, where scanned containers would be sorted as green, amber, red or deep red, with quarantine, health and import control officials granting clearances through the same system. The Cabinet allocated about 19 acres near the Kerawalapitiya Interchange in October 2021, the yard was estimated in 2024 at Rs.7 billion with a 3,000-container capacity, and Budget 2025 set aside Rs.500 million for land acquisition and preparatory work on it and the Bloemendhal Logistics Park.

However, five years on, inspections continue at the Grayline 1, Grayline 2 and Rank Container Terminal (RCT) yards, where, as per Customs, the two Grayline yards can process only 100 containers a day and RCT a maximum of 350.

The insider claimed that Customs has been reluctant to move, and alleged that physical examination persists because it leaves room for informal payments.

“Physical human intervention definitely means corruption,” the insider charged, adding that “speed money” continues to change hands, while conceding uncertainty over whether the practice has eased under the present government. ADB’s own assessment had noted that automation and fewer face-to-face interactions help reduce corruption.

Although selection is described as random, the insider claimed that most containers are still opened. Customs data show that around 35 percent of containers underwent some form of physical inspection in the first quarter of 2026, down from the 80 percent of consignments ADB found were targeted in 2018, but still three and a half times the 10 percent ceiling seen in countries with modern risk management.

In contrast, Dubai Customs runs a drive-through system at Jebel Ali that scans 150 trucks an hour and cut inspection time from two hours to 20 minutes per container, while India is building a scanner at Nhava Sheva rated at 60 to 80 containers an hour, four times the capacity of its existing units, and linked to its risk management system. Singapore and Rotterdam also scan containers on the move or inside the terminal, opening them only when the image shows an anomaly.

The delays are compounded by the way holds are administered. “Once a container is put on hold, it is indefinite,” the insider noted, claiming that the officer who stops a container must also inspect it, even though officers are transferred every six months, while scan reports can sit on a desk for two to three days.

The resulting demurrage falls first on the importer and the clearing agent, who is left to chase the release, and ultimately on the consumer.

Asked why such consignments are not stopped at the port of loading, the insider called it “the golden question”, pointing out that customs and police at the origin are equally expected to check the cargo.

“This is a mafia, a worldwide mafia. They take bribes to load it at that end, and take bribes to release it at this end,” the insider alleged, adding that shipping documents issued overseas cannot be taken on trust for that reason.

Meanwhile, with Customs having moved cargo clearance at the three yards online from April, the insider cautioned that paperless processing could be “very dangerous” if introduced without proper study, as scanned documents can be manipulated.

Since containers leave the port for examination, the insider called for electronic tagging of each box, every step logged from the terminal gate, and a final scan after inspection. ADB had found that the seals in use were not tamper-proof and gave no live tracking.

The insider also proposed time slots and a peak-hour surcharge to spread truck arrivals, and a paid voluntary scan for green channel traders, which would earn Customs additional income.
