Indirect taxes back at 78% of tax revenue; consumers see no relief: KPMG

Direct tax share slumps to 22%, from 33% in 2023, far off 40% goal

Middlemen pocketed 2019 VAT relief worth Rs.540bn; Budget 2027 levy cuts on sprats and Maldive fish face same leak

IMF-backed Tax Policy Analysis Unit at risk of becoming a “white elephant”

Rs.100,000 earner pays 16.2% of income as VAT, Rs.1mn earner just 7.2%

Sri Lanka’s tax burden has swung back onto the consumers, with the direct taxes slumping to 22 percent of the tax revenue, from a peak of 33 percent in 2023, yet the indirect tax relief fails to reach them, as the country lacks an anti-profiteering law and its new tax policy unit has gone quiet, a senior tax expert warned.

Addressing the Institute of Certified Management Accountants of Sri Lanka (CMA Sri Lanka) Budget Highlights 2027 forum in Colombo yesterday, KPMG Sri Lanka Tax and Regulatory Principal Suresh Perera noted that the share of direct taxes in total tax revenue has moved like a yo-yo.

He pointed out that it climbed from 23 percent in 2021 to as much as 33 percent, eased to 27 percent and has now slid to 22 percent, as per the latest numbers published with Budget 2027.

With the direct share shrinking by a third from its peak, the state now collects roughly Rs.3.50 through indirect taxes for every rupee of direct taxes.

Under the 60:40 split between indirect and direct taxes that the successive governments have pledged, that figure would be just Rs.1.50. Perera stressed that reaching the targeted ratio requires a far more sophisticated approach than the ad hoc changes of recent years.

He argued that the heavier reliance on Value Added Tax (VAT) and other consumption taxes makes the system regressive.

To illustrate, he showed that a person earning Rs.100,000 a month, with Rs.90,000 of taxable spending, pays about Rs.16,200 in VAT at 18 percent or 16.2 percent of income.

By contrast, a person earning Rs.1 million a month and spending Rs.400,000 pays Rs.72,000, which is just 7.2 percent of income. The lower earner therefore carries more than twice the relative burden.

According to Perera, that burden falls hardest on the low and middle-income households and runs against Adam Smith’s principle of taxing according to the ability to pay.

Perera pointed out that even when the indirect taxes are cut, the benefit seldom reaches the consumer. Recalling the sweeping tax changes of late 2019, when VAT was slashed from 15 percent to 8 percent, a reduction of nearly 47 percent, he estimated that the state gave up around Rs.540 billion by changing the VAT rules. Questioning whether that relief ever reached the consumers, he was unequivocal.

“The answer is no. Why? The vendors profiteered; the middleman pocketed that benefit,” he told the gathering.

At the time, the Inland Revenue Department put the annual cost of the VAT rate cut and the higher registration threshold alone at Rs.143 billion. Within weeks, its then Commissioner General acknowledged that some bakery manufacturers and hoteliers had not passed the benefit on to the customers.

Perera himself pressed for anti-profiteering clauses in a column published in December 2019, days after the rate cut took effect.

His warning comes days after Budget 2027 slashed the Special Commodity Levy (SCL) on imported sprats from Rs.100 to Rs.10 per kilogramme, a 90 percent cut and on Maldive fish from Rs.302 to Rs.10 per kilogramme, a reduction of nearly 97 percent.

The levy on the dried chillies was lowered by Rs.50 per kilogramme, while that on imported sugar was raised by Rs.10 per kilogramme, with the cuts taking effect from November 13.

“We have a fundamental error in the Sri Lankan VAT Act. We don’t have what is called an anti-profiteering clause,” Perera observed.

He added that even reducing or removing the SCL would not benefit the consumers, unless the country had an active anti-profiteering authority, backed by anti-profiteering clauses in the relevant statutes.

He dismissed the Consumer Affairs Authority Act as ineffective in this role. He recalled that the previous government granted relief in the expectation that the food prices at the canteens and bakeries would come down but nothing happened and the authorities eventually resorted to gazettes to control the prices of hoppers.

“These things are failures because we don’t have an anti-profiteering mechanism in Sri Lanka. It’s a must,” he stressed.

Citing examples from India, Singapore, Malaysia, Australia and New Zealand, Perera highlighted India’s statutory tax-benefit pass-through under Section 171 of its Central GST Act, Singapore’s Committee Against Profiteering and Malaysia’s Price Control and Anti-Profiteering Act of 2011 and its 2018 regulations.

Meanwhile, Perera questioned the whereabouts of the Tax Policy Analysis Unit, which he believes should be steering any shift in Sri Lanka’s tax mix.

The Finance Ministry formally established the unit under its Fiscal Policy Department in February this year, as an IMF structural benchmark, after the IMF conducted a capacity-building programme for its staff from January 19 to 30. The unit’s stated functions include revenue analysis, distributional analysis of tax policies and the evaluation of tax expenditures.

“After that, have you heard anything about it? Any output coming from that unit?” he questioned, more than nine months after its launch.

“Our concern should be that this unit should not end up becoming a white elephant because we have not seen this unit, who is in this unit, what activity is being done in this unit.”

He argued that the long-term plan for Sri Lanka’s tax landscape, one that carries over from one government to the next, must come from this unit rather than from ad hoc political decisions.

He outlined the government’s plan of no new taxes or rate increases, raising additional revenue by broadening the direct and indirect tax base and lowering the VAT rate in a few years. Perera warned that such a transformation could not be achieved without the proper institutions in place.

To broaden the direct tax base, Perera proposed bringing offshore indirect transfers of shares, crypto assets and online betting into the tax net, along with stronger tax administration.

On the indirect side, he called for a reverse charge mechanism and platform liability for digital VAT, together with fewer exemptions. He insisted that any reduction of VAT on essential goods must come with an anti-profiteering mechanism attached.

However, he cautioned that the indirect taxes are only one driver of the consumer burden, with the interest rates, exchange rates, global prices, production and supply chain costs, financing costs and business margins all playing a part.

He also warned that the government could not afford to grant relief irresponsibly, as the country still depends heavily on tax collections.

“Right now, Sri Lanka needs taxes to keep it afloat,” he noted.

Describing the single 18 percent VAT rate as very high compared to the region, Perera advocated a multiple-rate structure with around 5 percent on essentials and 20 to 25 percent on luxury items.

“You can’t have something like perfume and say milk or some essential item being taxed at the same rate. That to me doesn’t sound correct,” he said.

Perera maintained that relief on essentials should be delivered through zero-rating rather than exemptions, as an exempt supplier cannot claim input tax, which then gets built back into the price.

Budget 2027 has proposed exempting printed books and exercise books from VAT.

He also called for the number of VAT exemptions to be brought down. He noted that they once covered more than 200 line items and still stand at around 40, whereas New Zealand and Australia operate with fewer than five.

He further argued that the restrictions on claiming input tax credits under Section 22 of the VAT Act artificially distort the calculation of value addition, meaning that Sri Lanka is not truly collecting a tax on value added.

Describing the Social Security Contribution Levy (SSCL) as a turnover tax under another name, Perera recalled that Sri Lanka abandoned the turnover tax in 1998 because of its cascading effect, only to see it return under different names, including the Nation Building Tax, which was introduced in 2009 and abolished in 2019.

“Social security levy is nothing but turnover tax,” he declared, while urging a gradual phase-out, given the revenue at stake.

The government has already folded the 2.5 percent SSCL into VAT for financial services from July 1 this year, creating a consolidated rate of 20.5 percent.

He also renewed his call to scrap the SCL, which allows the minister to determine the rates without the parliamentary approval. He noted that this mirrors the structure of the Special Goods and Services Tax Bill, several clauses of which the Supreme Court determined in 2022 to be inconsistent with the constitution.

Sri Lanka had pledged under its IMF programme to replace the SCL with VAT but the government decided in December 2024 to retain the levy, although Budget 2026 replaced it with VAT and SSCL on imported coconut oil and palm oil.
