Kethesh Loganathan and the Lost Generation

Many from Kethesh’s generation dreamt of social change and willingly sacrificed their future, when others sought greener pastures in distant lands. That generation of Tamil activists, if not killed in battle, or assassinated, were broken by the trauma of a violent political culture

Twenty years is a long time. I often think of Kethesh Loganathan over these years, even though I had actively known him for just four years. My father had been in touch with Kethesh for decades, and his introduction began a friendship and collaboration with Kethesh which suddenly ended with his assassination on 12 August 2006.

The Tamil community and the country as a whole have lost tremendous activists whose lives were cut short by a violent political culture. Their passion for social change could have reshaped the future of the country, if they had lived to make fuller contributions in the political and economic realms. I write about Kethesh here, because I feel it is important that the younger generations know about such courageous figures, their commitment to dissent and their tragic sacrifices.

Kethesh came from a well-known Tamil family, and was the son of the first Sri Lankan General Manager of the Bank of Ceylon. He was educated in the United States, and some of best social science institutions in Europe. Despite his elite upbringing, he was drawn by the spirit of many Tamil youth of his generation, and he joined one of the Tamil militant groups.

Kethesh’s Marxist leanings led him to the Eelam People’s Revolutionary Liberation Front (EPRLF), an organisation that took up anti-caste and class politics, even as it was swept by Tamil nationalism during the early stages of the civil war. By 1986, the EPRLF had for the most part been crushed by the LTTE in internecine conflict, as the LTTE with its totalitarian politics systematically wiped out dissent, and targeted all other Tamil militant groups. After governing the North-East Provincial Council from 1988 to 1990 under the aegis of the Indian Peace Keeping Forces (IPKF), the EPRLF retreated to India when the IPKF withdrew. Over a dozen EPRLF leaders was massacred by the LTTE in Chennai in June 1990. However, a few of its leaders who were not at that fateful leaders meeting, including Kethesh, survived.

As disagreements rose among the EPRLF leadership, Kethesh left the EPRLF in the mid-1990s and sought a career first in journalism, and then as a political analyst on the conflict. It is during this time that he wrote an important book on the search for a political solution in Sri Lanka titled, ‘Lost Opportunities’. He was well positioned to write that book, because he was a participant during one of the early negotiations towards a political solution in 1985, facilitated by India between the Government of Sri Lanka and a number of Tamil militant groups and political parties in Thimpu. Even though the negotiations failed, Kethesh was central to formulating the Tamil nationalist position now famously known as the Thimpu Principles.

By the time I met Kethesh in 2002 in Colombo, during the much internationalised Norwegian peace process, he had shifted considerably from his earlier Tamil nationalist position. He was working at the Centre for Policy Alternatives at that time, and I vividly remember our first meeting, where after a discussion about a prominent public statement by some of us overseas calling for a peace with democracy and human rights, he introduced me to his colleagues. At our early meetings, while we agreed about the predicament of the Tamil polity overwhelmed by the totalitarian hold of the LTTE, we disagreed on one major point. I was influenced by the University Teachers for Human Rights (Jaffna) and was very sceptical of the LTTE’s intent to negotiate, and believed the LTTE was buying time to re-arm and in the meantime wipe out any remaining dissent within the Tamil polity. Kethesh, however, wanted to engage, and thought the Norwegian peace process was an important opening.

A year later, by mid-2003, when the LTTE’s assassination of Tamil dissidents accelerated, and when his former comrade in the EPRLF, Thambirajah Subathiran known as Robert was assassinated in Jaffna, Kethesh came to the position that for all purposes the peace process was dead. By then he had also become very critical of the role played by Norway, which was increasingly taking the position of appeasing the LTTE, and sought to ensure a deal between the Ranil Wickremesinghe Government and LTTE, even at the cost of human rights, democracy and inclusion of all communities.

While the Colombo establishment, including most NGOs were aligned with the Norwegian approach, Kethesh was one of the few progressive voices in Colombo to challenge the internationalised peace process backed by the US, EU and Japan. This was a context in which Kethesh also had to worry about his safety, and indeed many of us feared for his life, as most Tamil critics had rarely been spared by the LTTE.

During those intense years, many of us looked to Kethesh for analysis of the unfolding situation. He regularly wrote a powerful column in these very pages of the Daily Mirror, under the pen name “Sathya”, meaning truth, which became a devastating critique of the Norwegian peace process. In fact, Kethesh’s column remained an inspiration for me to begin writing my own column here; though I must say I have not been able to emulate the power and lucidity of his prose.

In those years, preceding his assassination, I was constantly in touch with him. A number of us living overseas sought to use the safety and space abroad, to write and speak critically about the worrying developments and unfolding disaster. In the three years before his untimely death, he became one of my mentors. Kethesh as I knew him was a loner, and once he arrived at a position it was hard to get him to shift. But with the few of us he chose to engage, he would listen to the disagreements carefully, and only when he was fully convinced, would he shift his position.

After his demise, and as the war escalated, I used to think to myself, how Kethesh would have responded to a question or a situation. And now, over seventeen years after the war, I often wonder how much Kethesh could have contributed to the debates and developments during our troubled post-war journey. The loss most of all has been for the Tamil community.

Kethesh and the youth of his time embraced militancy, and belonged to the generation after my father and before mine. My father and his contemporaries who survived the ruthless gun culture that had eclipsed Tamil politics fell silent for the most part as they did not know how to engage with the form of armed politics they had never imagined. My generation has yet to come to terms with the legacy of Tamil militancy; a retreat from active engagement or empty slogans characterise the lack of serious reflection and a search for a way forward. When I think of the title of Kethesh’ book ‘Lost Opportunities’, I think of a lost generation. Indeed, losing that generation has had a devastating toll, and for Tamil politics in particular, it has a crippling inter-generational impact.

Many from Kethesh’s generation dreamt of social change and willingly sacrificed their future, when others sought greener pastures in distant lands. That generation of Tamil activists, if not killed in battle, or assassinated, were broken by the trauma of a violent political culture. When I remember Kethesh, I also think of all those others, who were part of that tragic era. We owe it to that generation, to work towards equality and freedom, which in different ways they desired so much, but their lives cut short to contribute in full.
