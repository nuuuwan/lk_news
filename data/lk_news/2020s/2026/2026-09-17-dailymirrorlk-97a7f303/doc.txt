Sri Lanka needs street-smart directors, not those buried in theory: SLID Chairman

Sri Lanka needs better-governed organisations and directors who are street-smart, practical and not buried in theory, as a private sector whose reputation has been battered in recent times must raise its game, Sri Lanka Institute of Directors (SLID) Chairman Dinesh Weerakkody told the institute’s Annual Members Meeting and 25th anniversary celebration in Colombo on Monday.

He called for strong governance and a culture of doing the right thing across corporate Sri Lanka.

“The responsibility for all of us is clear: build credible institutions,” Weerakkody urged.

“Certainly, the next 25 years will demand even more from directors. The ability to navigate through crisis and chaos,” he cautioned, pointing to artificial intelligence, global conflicts and climate disasters as forces that will fundamentally reshape the way business is done.

Recalling his early years as a young board director invited by founder Chairman Deshamanya Ken Balendra to serve on the SLID’s inaugural committee, Weerakkody noted that having no mandated code of corporate governance and no social media at the time, the disclosure requirements were minimal and the related party transactions were rampant.

“Life on boards was easy. You were not expected to rock the boat too much,” he recalled.

The market has since transformed. Sri Lanka had about 235 listed companies, with a market capitalisation of around 100 billion and the rupee traded at Rs.89 to the US dollar. Today, 290 companies are listed, with a market capitalisation of Rs.8 trillion, while the exchange rate stands at Rs.328.

The number of listed companies has grown by around 23 percent, while the market capitalisation has risen eightyfold in rupee terms.

In dollar terms, it has climbed from roughly US $ 1.1 billion to about US $ 24.4 billion, even as the rupee lost close to 73 percent of its value against the dollar. The toughest critic of boards today, he quipped, is social media.

The SLID now represents more than 1,000 members. Over the past year, the institute hosted Sri Lanka Corporate Director Summit 2026, which brought together more than 400 chairpersons, directors and senior business leaders alongside over 20 international speakers and launched the SLID ACCA National Corporate Director Awards to recognise excellence in stewardship. It has also pursued initiatives such as Women on Boards and the Board Ready Directors directory.

Weerakkody closed his address with the words of the late Ratan Tata, whom he had met on several occasions: “Good governance is not about rules. It is about doing the right thing when no one is watching.”

He told the gathering, “The way forward is clear and well lit. All we need is the courage to walk that path.”

Meanwhile, Secretary to Prime Minister G. Pradeep Saputhanthri, attending as the chief guest, pointed out that Sri Lanka is undertaking important economic and structural reforms as it rebuilds from the most difficult economic crisis in its recent history. At the same time, artificial intelligence and other technologies are changing businesses, institutions and the way decisions are made.

“For directors, technology can no longer be left entirely to technical specialists. It has become a question of governance, accountability and institutional responsibility,” he observed.

Drawing on historian Yuval Noah Harari’s distinction that wisdom grows more important as intelligence becomes increasingly abundant, Saputhanthri argued that technology can provide information and generate answers but cannot decide what matters, which risks are worth taking or what kind of institutions should be built. Those, he noted, remain matters of human judgment.

Strong institutions need accountability, integrity, transparency and the rule of law and their quality is shaped not by the government alone but also in boardrooms, businesses and professional bodies, he stressed.

“Good governance and performance are not competing objectives over time. One sustains the other,” Saputhanthri added, noting that leadership is measured not only by results but by the strength of the institutions left behind.
