What America’s Midterms Can Teach Us

Voters react after Democrat Jina Hinojosa won the election for Texas governor

On Tuesday, American voters did something Sri Lankans can only watch from far away: they put a president to the test in the middle of his term. Democrats won both the House and the Senate. They took seats from Ohio to Texas to Kansas, which has not sent a Democrat to the Senate in almost a hundred years.  Voters wanted limits on a president who had told them a month earlier, “I can do whatever I want, if I want.”

The point is not Donald Trump. It is how the system works. A midterm gives voters a fixed date to say “well done” or “not good enough” without waiting for the whole term to end. Congress is chosen on a different schedule from the White House, so people get a second chance to speak. Whatever you think of the result, the system did its job: Power was tested halfway through, in the open, at the ballot box.

Sri Lanka has no such moment, and its recent history shows why one is needed. In September 2024, Anura Kumara Dissanayake won the presidency with about 42 per cent of the votes in the first count. That was the biggest share of any candidate, but it was short of a majority, and he was declared the winner only after second-preference votes were counted. Two months later, his National People’s Power won 159 of the 225 seats in Parliament, the first time a single party had won two-thirds of the seats under the PR.  The government is now two years old, with three to go, and voters who have changed their minds have no set date to say so before the elections due in 2029.

This is not an accusation. A government with a big mandate has the right to use it. But Sri Lankans know what big majorities have done before. The 18th and 20th Amendments passed with a two-thirds vote in Parliament alone, though legal experts said they needed a referendum, and both were later undone. Now the NPP has promised a new constitution, including an end to the executive presidency. Whatever the merits of those plans, a constitution written by one big majority should include a way for voters to check that majority later.

Local council elections in May 2025 were a partial test, but local councils are small, and a country that votes in a big way only at the top gets one real verdict every five years. There is a better tool, and it has been left unused.

The nine provincial councils are long overdue for an election. They have had no elected members since 2018. The reasons have changed over the years, from a fight over how to redraw boundaries to gaps in the law, but the result is the same: a level of government set up under the Thirteenth Amendment sits idle. The government said it aimed to hold the polls in the first half of 2026.

That time has passed. In August the Cabinet spokesman said the government wants them held as soon as possible, once the legal steps are done. Statements have been mixed: in June a JVP leader was reported as saying there was no money for the polls, while the government has since said the money is set aside and only the legal framework is missing. The Centre for Policy Alternatives has warned that the delay hurts citizens’ democratic rights and risks a party-state system.

Here is a two-step proposal. First, the new constitution should INCLUDE a midterm: A fixed vote at the halfway point of every national term, so that no government goes five years without facing voters. The simplest way is to elect the provincial councils then, all nine on one day, so the whole island speaks in the middle of each term. Second, until the new constitution is ready, hold the long-overdue provincial elections early next year. They would be a perfect midterm, and work as one: A vote two years into the government, before its big constitutional plans are finalised.

Nothing in this plan favours one party. A rule in the constitution would bind whoever governs in 2032, and after. That is the difference between a favour and a rule: between a government that allows an election when it feels safe, and one that faces voters whether it feels safe or not. The government has said it wants to hold provincial elections before bringing in constitutional changes. That is an argument for this very order. An early provincial vote would let voters judge the government’s record, and its reform plans, before those plans become law.

Why provincial councils and not an early vote for Parliament? Because they give a verdict without a crisis. A provincial election cannot remove a president or bring down a cabinet. It cannot undo the calm a country still recovering from the 2022 collapse is trying to keep. But it covers the whole island, province by province, and it would let people in the north and east, who have had no provincial representatives for years, have a say in how they are governed. One SLMC Member of Parliament has said that having no councils weakens democratic representation.

A midterm would help the government. If the NPP holds its ground, it can claim a fresh mandate for its reforms, one tested at the polls and not just assumed from 2024. If it loses ground, it learns while there is still time to change course. The opposition, scattered since the Rajapaksa-led SLPP was cut to two elected seats in 2024, would have a reason to rebuild and a date to aim for. Democracy is healthier when both sides know when the next test is coming.

There are fair objections. Some politicians remain cool to the provincial system, which grew out of the 1987 Indo-Lanka Accord and which some see as an unwelcome legacy. Councils with limited powers may be a poor measure of national feeling: Local issues and local personalities can drown out the message voters mean to send, and a government that does badly could simply say the vote was about something else. Running nine elections at once costs money, and the country is still repairing its public finances. The fight over boundaries that has blocked the polls is not easy to settle. Others will say that a real check on a two-thirds government must be built into the rules: A requirement that major constitutional changes go to a referendum, or a fixed mid-term vote for Parliament itself.

Still, the choice is not between a midterm and a perfect check. It is between a midterm and silence. Provincial voters have waited long enough. Let the first midterm come early next year, and let the new constitution make it permanent.
